**Question:** What do SSA and planetary defence try to keep track of, how big is the population (and how much of it do we actually see), and how do we turn incomplete observations into a risk we can act on?
**Leads to:** [[R7031R L2]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
SSA keeps track of artificial objects around Earth (working satellites and debris), planetary defence keeps track of natural near-Earth objects (asteroids and comets); both share the same first problem: **we only see part of what is there**, so every catalogue is the subset our sensors can detect, and the rest is estimated with population models (ESA MASTER-8 for debris, NEOMOD for NEOs). About 47,000 objects are tracked, but models estimate ~68,000 objects > 10 cm, 1.5 million of 1–10 cm and 230 million of 1 mm–1 cm; for NEOs, ~70 % of those > 40 m are known and surveys like Rubin and NEO Surveyor are finding the rest. Risk is not density: it depends on encounter geometry, size, relative velocity and **orbit uncertainty**, which is why the rest of the course is about measuring and shrinking that uncertainty.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **SSA (Space Situational Awareness):** Knowledge of the space-object population, the space environment and the threats and risks. The EU splits it into SST, NEO and SWE.
- **SST (Space Surveillance and Tracking):** Networked sensors + processing that survey and track objects in Earth orbit. Services: collision avoidance, re-entry analysis, fragmentation analysis.
- **STM (Space Traffic Management):** The *rules and means* to use space safely. SST measures the traffic, STM manages it.
- **SDA (Space Domain Awareness):** The US (military) term. SSA is one part of it.
- **Planetary defence:** Finding, tracking and assessing natural objects that may hit Earth, and planning what to do about them.
- **NEO / NEA / PHA:** Near-Earth object (perihelion $q < 1.3$ au) / near-Earth asteroid / potentially hazardous asteroid (MOID $\le 0.05$ au and $H \le 22$).
- **MOID:** Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance: the closest the two *orbits* (not the bodies) come to each other.
- **Absolute magnitude $H$:** Brightness standardised to 1 au from Sun and observer. Smaller $H$ = bigger object. $H = 22$ ≈ 140 m.
- **Catalogue vs population:** The catalogue is what sensors can track. The population includes everything too small or faint, estimated statistically.
- **Population model:** Simulates sources → propagates → applies the survey's biases → fits to the observations (MASTER-8, NEOMOD).
- **Observational bias:** Surveys see only the sky they look at, when they look, and only bright enough objects. The observed distribution is *not* the true one.

### Two populations, one problem ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=2|1.1 s. 2]])
- **SSA:** keeping track of satellites and debris.
- **Planetary defence:** keeping track of near-Earth objects.

Very different objects, but the same first challenge: **we only see part of what is there.** The two guiding questions of the course: *What is out there? What are we missing?*

### Space Situational Awareness ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=3|1.1 s. 3–6]])
Satellites give us communications, navigation, weather and science. They share their orbits with spent rocket stages, retired satellites and fragments from break-ups and collisions. Operators must make decisions (e.g. "do we manoeuvre?") about objects they **cannot observe all the time**, so they act on *orbit estimates*.

> [!important] A catalogue is not the population ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=3|1.1 s. 3]])
> The debris catalogue is only the subset current sensors can detect and track. Everything smaller or fainter is still there, just not in the list.

**Dark and quiet skies ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=6|1.1 s. 6]]):** satellites are a problem for astronomy too:
- **Optical:** bright trails across telescope images hide faint objects.
- **Radio:** satellite transmissions interfere with faint cosmic sources.
- Astronomers need predictions of satellite *positions and brightness* (the same data SSA produces) plus software to mask the trails. The IAU's Centre for the Protection of the Dark and Quiet Sky (CPS) speaks for astronomers at the UN.

### Definitions ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=7|1.1 s. 7–12]])
**EU Space Programme 2021–2027** has five components: Copernicus (Earth observation), EGNOS, Galileo (navigation), GOVSATCOM/Secure SATCOM (connectivity) and **SSA**.

> [!info] EU definition of SSA ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=8|1.1 s. 8]])
> "The comprehensive knowledge, understanding and maintained awareness of (i) the population of space objects, (ii) the space environment, and (iii) the existing threats and risks."
> Three subcomponents:
> 1. **SST**: Space Surveillance and Tracking (artificial objects)
> 2. **NEO**: Near-Earth Objects (natural objects)
> 3. **SWE**: Space Weather Events

**SST in practice ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=9|1.1 s. 9–10]]):** more than 400 organisations receive EU SST services through the Front Desk at EUSPA, protecting more than 600 satellites. The three services:

| Service | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Collision Avoidance (CA)** | Assesses collision risk between satellites, or satellite vs debris, and sends alerts |
| **Re-entry Analysis (RE)** | Assesses uncontrolled re-entries into the atmosphere |
| **Fragmentation Analysis (FG)** | Detects and characterises break-ups and collisions in orbit |

**STM ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=11|1.1 s. 11]]):** "the means and the rules to access, conduct activities in, and return from outer space safely, sustainably, and securely." More satellites, debris, reusable launchers and private operators → more congestion. The EU approach has four areas: operator requirements, operational capabilities (EU SST is the backbone), rules and standards, international coordination.

> [!tip] SST vs STM in one line
> **SST monitors and assesses** the traffic. **STM adds the coordination and rules** needed to manage it. (Like traffic cameras vs traffic laws.)

**SDA ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=12|1.1 s. 12]]):** the US uses *Space Domain Awareness*: "the timely, relevant and actionable understanding of the space operating environment needed to plan, conduct and assess **military** space operations". Mission Delta 2 of the US Space Force does it. SSA (the physical objects) is one part of SDA.

### What is up there ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=13|1.1 s. 13–17]])
Four kinds of artificial objects:
1. **Active spacecraft:** working satellites that can manoeuvre.
2. **Inactive spacecraft and rocket bodies:** large objects left in orbit after their mission.
3. **Mission-related objects:** things released during operations (lens covers, adapters).
4. **Fragmentation debris:** pieces from explosions, collisions or intentional destruction (ASAT tests).

> [!important] ESA space debris by the numbers ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=14|1.1 s. 14]], Oct 2026)
> | Quantity | Number |
> |---|---|
> | Rocket launches since 1957 | ~7,370 |
> | Satellites placed in orbit | ~28,270 |
> | …still in space | ~19,440 |
> | …still functioning | ~15,800 |
> | **Objects regularly tracked and catalogued** | **~47,360** |
> | Break-ups, explosions, collisions | > 660 |
> | Total mass in orbit | > 17,400 t |
> | **Estimated** objects > 10 cm (MASTER-8) | 68,450 (incl. ~11,300 active payloads) |
> | **Estimated** 1–10 cm | 1.5 million |
> | **Estimated** 1 mm–1 cm | 230 million |

**ESA MASTER-8 ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=15|1.1 s. 15]]):** builds a *synthetic* population from known debris sources and checks it against observations:
**model the sources → propagate the fragments → compare with measurements → calibrate and validate.**
For particles too small to track one by one, the "measurements" are impact craters on returned spacecraft surfaces and impact detectors. The output is e.g. the cumulative number of objects crossing LEO as a function of diameter (falls steeply with size).

By orbit ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=17|1.1 s. 17]]): the growth is almost all in **LEO**.

### Sustainability: fragmentation events ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=18|1.1 s. 18–20]])
**Notable payload fragmentations:**

| Event | Date | Mass | Altitude | Inclination | Cause |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fengyun 1C** | 11 Jan 2007 | 958 kg | 843–863 km | 98.6° | Chinese ASAT test |
| **Cosmos-2251 + Iridium 33** | 10 Feb 2009 | 892 + 661 kg | ~776–791 km | 86.4° | First accidental satellite–satellite collision |
| **Cosmos-1408** | 15 Nov 2021 | 2180 kg | 465–490 km | 82.6° | Russian ASAT test |

**Notable rocket-body fragmentations:** Centaur-5 SEC (2019, GTO-like 8,500–35,000 km), HAPS (1996), and two Long March L-15 (YF115) upper stages (2022 and 2024, ~800–850 km, 6000 kg each).

![[R7031R L1.1 s18 debris cloud after Cosmos-Iridium.png]]
*Where the Cosmos/Iridium debris could be 3 hours after the collision ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=18|1.1 s. 18]]): the fragments have already spread into a shell around the whole Earth along the orbit planes.*

**Why does a cloud spread so fast?** Each fragment gets a slightly different velocity ($\Delta v$ of a few m/s to hundreds of m/s). A different speed means a different orbital period, so fragments drift apart *along* the orbit within hours, then (because of Earth's oblateness, $J_2$) their orbit planes spread at different rates over months to years until they form a shell.

![[R7031R L1.1 s20 fragments from events.png]]
*Fragments produced per 5-year bin, coloured by cause ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=20|1.1 s. 20]]). The 2005–2010 peak is Fengyun 1C (deliberate) + Iridium/Cosmos (collision); propulsion-related explosions (red) are the steady background.*

> [!warning] Spatial density is not encounter risk ([[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=21|1.1 s. 21]])
> - Debris density varies with altitude, inclination and orbital regime.
> - Objects with similar inclinations pass repeatedly through the same regions.
> - Objects on **crossing orbits** can meet at very high relative speeds even where the density is modest.
> - Collision risk depends on **encounter geometry, object size, relative velocity and orbit uncertainty**.

Plain words: a crowded road where everyone drives the same direction (co-planar orbits) is less dangerous than a quiet crossing where cars come from the side at full speed.

### Planetary defence ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=2|1.2 s. 2–3]])
- SSA tracks objects **orbiting the Earth**; planetary defence identifies and assesses **natural objects that may approach Earth** (~14,000 potentially hazardous objects known).
- Both fields use the same toolbox: **observations, orbit estimates, population models and risk assessment.**

**The wake-up call ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=3|1.2 s. 3]]):** comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 broke into ~20 pieces and hit Jupiter in July 1994. The impact scars were larger than Earth. It proved that cosmic impacts are not just ancient history.

**Fireballs ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=4|1.2 s. 4]]):** US government sensors have recorded about 1000 fireballs (bolides) between 1988 and 2026, spread evenly over the globe. Small impacts happen all the time; almost all burn up or explode high in the atmosphere. Slide 5 is dash-cam footage of the Chelyabinsk fireball.

**Notable historic impacts ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=6|1.2 s. 6]]):**

| Impact | Object | Crater | When | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vredefort (South Africa) | 20–25 km | 250–300 km | ~2 Gyr ago | Largest confirmed impact structure |
| Chicxulub (Mexico) | 10–15 km | 180 km | ~66 Myr ago | Ended the dinosaurs: 70 % of species extinct |
| Sudbury (Canada) | 10–15 km | 200 km | ~1.85 Gyr ago | Rich nickel/copper/platinum deposits |

**"Modern" events ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=7|1.2 s. 7]]):**

| Event | Size | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Tunguska, 30 Jun 1908 | ~30 m | Air burst, flattened 80 million trees over 2,150 km² |
| Chelyabinsk, 15 Feb 2013 | ~20 m | Exploded ~30 km up; the shockwave broke windows and injured ~1,500 people |
| Barringer (Meteor) Crater, ~50 kyr ago | 45–50 m, iron-nickel | 1.2 km crater |

> [!tip] Plain words
> Small objects (tens of metres) usually **explode in the air** (Tunguska, Chelyabinsk): the danger is the blast wave, not a crater. Only strong (iron) or big objects reach the ground.

**Where do they come from ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=8|1.2 s. 8]]):** near-Earth objects are the *progenitors of fireballs*. NEOs = near-Earth asteroids + Jupiter-family comets.

### NEO orbit classes ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=9|1.2 s. 9]])
Notation: $a$ = semi-major axis, $q = a(1 - e)$ = perihelion distance, $Q = a(1 + e)$ = aphelion distance, $P$ = orbital period. Earth: $q_\oplus = 0.983$ au, $Q_\oplus = 1.017$ au.

| Group | Definition | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **NEOs** | $q < 1.3$ au, $P < 200$ yr | Near-Earth comets and asteroids |
| **NEAs** | $q < 1.3$ au | Near-Earth asteroids |
| **Amors** | $a > 1.0$ au, $1.017 < q < 1.3$ au | Approach Earth from outside but don't cross its orbit |
| **Apollos** | $a > 1.0$ au, $q < 1.017$ au | Earth-crossing, larger orbit than Earth |
| **Atens** | $a < 1.0$ au, $Q > 0.983$ au | Earth-crossing, smaller orbit than Earth |
| **Atiras** | $a < 1.0$ au, $Q < 0.983$ au | Entirely inside Earth's orbit |
| **PHAs** | MOID $\le 0.05$ au, $H \le 22.0$ | Potentially hazardous: come close **and** are big enough (≳ 140 m) |

> [!tip] How to remember
> Start with $a$: bigger than Earth's (Amor, Apollo) or smaller (Aten, Atira)? Then ask: does it **cross** Earth's orbit? Apollo and Aten cross; Amor stays outside, Atira stays inside. Alphabetical inside each pair: Am**o**r (outside), Ap**o**llo; At**e**n, At**i**ra (inside).

0.05 au ≈ 7.5 million km ≈ 20 lunar distances.

### Finding NEOs ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=10|1.2 s. 10–16]])
![[R7031R L1.2 s10 NEAs discovered.png]]
*Cumulative NEA discoveries ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=10|1.2 s. 10]]): all sizes (blue) are still growing steeply, while the large ones (red, > 1 km) levelled off long ago: we have found almost all big ones. The 140 m+ (orange) are about halfway.*

**Surveys ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=11|1.2 s. 11–12]]):** Catalina Sky Survey (USA), Pan-STARRS (Hawaii), ATLAS (four sites worldwide), professional and amateur observers, and now the **Vera C. Rubin Observatory** (Chile), expected to find ~90,000 new NEOs; it found 11,000 new asteroids from early data alone. ~70 % of NEOs > 40 m are known.

> [!important] Survey bias ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=13|1.2 s. 13]])
> - A survey samples only the parts of the sky it observes, at the times it observes them.
> - Discoverability depends on apparent brightness, phase angle, position…
> - Objects near the Sun in the sky are hard to see from the ground.
> - Small NEOs only become bright enough during a close approach, when they also move fast across the sky.
> - **The observed distribution is NOT the intrinsic population distribution.**

**NASA NEO Surveyor ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=14|1.2 s. 14]]):** 50 cm **infrared** telescope with two wavelength channels, launch planned 2027, parked at the Sun–Earth **L1** point (it can look close to the Sun, where ground surveys can't). Expected to discover 200,000–300,000 new NEOs > 10 m; goal: **90 % of asteroids > 140 m within a decade** (US Congressional mandate of 2005).

**From discovery to decision ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=15|1.2 s. 15]]):**
1. **Sky surveys** discover new objects and report them to the **Minor Planet Center (MPC)**.
2. **Follow-up telescopes** observe known objects again and report to the MPC.
3. **Orbit computation centres** refine the orbit and the impact probability (NASA CNEOS with *Sentry* and *Scout*, ESA NEOCC, NEODyS).
4. **IAWN** (International Asteroid Warning Network) issues a warning if the impact probability is high enough.
5. **SMPAG** (Space Mission Planning Advisory Group): space agencies plan characterisation and deflection missions.
6. **UN and national authorities** make the final decision; plan evacuations and emergency response if needed.

### NEO population models ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=17|1.2 s. 17–19]])
Models: Bottke et al. (2000, 2001), Granvik et al. (2016–2018), Nesvorný et al. (2023, 2024): **NEOMOD I, II, III**.

**How a model is built: forward modelling ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=18|1.2 s. 18]]):**
1. Represent the asteroid and comet **source regions** (e.g. resonances in the main belt), integrate test bodies to get their orbital distributions, combine with size-dependent source weights and removal processes (hitting the Sun, ejection…).
2. Apply the **survey biases** (real sky coverage, brightness limit, detection losses) and fit the simulated detections to the real ones, keeping the parameter uncertainties.
3. Convert absolute magnitude $H$ to diameter with a debiased **albedo** model.
4. Check that simulated detections match observations.

Same idea as MASTER-8: you can't count what you can't see, so you **model the true population, simulate what your telescopes would see of it, and tune the model until the simulation matches reality.**

**Impact rates from NEOMOD III ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=19|1.2 s. 19]]):**

| Impactor size | Earth impact flux | ≈ one every… |
|---|---|---|
| $D > 1$ km | 1.51–1.74 per Myr | ~600,000 yr |
| $D > 140$ m | 42–52 per Myr | ~20,000 yr |
| $D > 100$ m | 64.8–81 per Myr | ~14,000 yr |
| $D > 30$ m | 829–1,160 per Myr | ~1,000 yr |
| $D > 10$ m | ~1 per 40 yr | 40 yr (bolide observations suggest 1 per 10 yr) |

### Deflection ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=20|1.2 s. 20–24]])
Three techniques:
1. **Kinetic impactor:** hit it hard with a spacecraft to change its velocity slightly. **Tested for real: NASA DART** hit Dimorphos (moon of Didymos) on 26 Sep 2022 (DRACO images on [[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=21|1.2 s. 21]]).
2. **Gravity tractor:** park a heavy spacecraft next to it for years; its gravity slowly tugs the asteroid. Very gentle, needs a long warning time.
3. **Nuclear explosion:** for large objects or short warning times; stand-off detonation heats the surface and the blown-off material pushes it (deflection), or disrupts it.

![[R7031R L1.2 s20 deflection method vs size and warning time.png]]
*Which method works for which asteroid size and warning time ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=20|1.2 s. 20]]). Short warning or big asteroid → only nuclear. Long warning, smaller asteroid → kinetic impactor (KI) or ion-beam deflection.*

The key point: **a tiny $\Delta v$ applied early moves the impact point a lot** (the change grows every orbit). Warning time is worth more than force, which is why discovering objects early (surveys) is the core of planetary defence.

**Films vs reality ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=22|1.2 s. 22–24]]):** *Armageddon*, *Deep Impact* and *Don't Look Up* all use nuclear deflection. Their biggest mistake: the **cover-up**. In reality:
- Discoveries are reported to the MPC and quickly become **public**.
- Other observatories follow up to confirm.
- Then it must be checked that it's really **new** and not a re-observed known object.

### Apophis 2029 ([[SSA-lecture1.2.pdf#page=25|1.2 s. 25]])
- ~340 m asteroid, passes **31,600 km above Earth on 13 April 2029** (inside the geostationary ring at 35,786 km!).
- A once-in-~7000-years event, visible to the naked eye from Europe and Africa.
- No risk of impact for at least the next century.
- Missions: JAXA-DLR **DESTINY+** flyby before the encounter, ESA-JAXA **RAMSES** flies alongside it, NASA **OSIRIS-APEX** studies it after.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Perihelion and aphelion**
$$
q = a(1 - e), \qquad Q = a(1 + e)
$$
where $a$ = semi-major axis (au), $e$ = eccentricity. Used to sort NEOs into Amor/Apollo/Aten/Atira.

**Orbit-class boundaries** (Earth: $q_\oplus = 0.983$ au, $Q_\oplus = 1.017$ au)
$$
\text{NEO: } q < 1.3 \text{ au}, \qquad \text{PHA: MOID} \le 0.05 \text{ au and } H \le 22
$$

**Impact recurrence**
$$
\text{mean interval} = \frac{1}{\text{flux}}, \qquad \text{e.g. } \frac{10^6 \text{ yr}}{47 \text{ per Myr}} \approx 21{,}000 \text{ yr for } D > 140 \text{ m}
$$

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** Classify three asteroids (Earth: $q_\oplus = 0.983$ au, $Q_\oplus = 1.017$ au):
- A: $a = 1.5$ au, $e = 0.40$
- B: $a = 0.85$ au, $e = 0.25$
- C: $a = 1.8$ au, $e = 0.35$

Then: if A has MOID = 0.02 au and $H = 21$, is it a PHA?

**Solution:**
1. *A:* $q = 1.5(1 - 0.40) = 0.90$ au, $Q = 1.5(1.40) = 2.10$ au. $a > 1$ and $q = 0.90 < 1.017$ → crosses Earth's orbit from outside → **Apollo**.
2. *B:* $q = 0.85(0.75) = 0.64$ au, $Q = 0.85(1.25) = 1.0625$ au. $a < 1$ and $Q = 1.06 > 0.983$ → crosses Earth's orbit from inside → **Aten**.
3. *C:* $q = 1.8(0.65) = 1.17$ au. $a > 1$ and $1.017 < q = 1.17 < 1.3$ → approaches but never crosses → **Amor**.
4. *PHA check for A:* MOID $= 0.02 \le 0.05$ au ✓ and $H = 21 \le 22$ ✓ (brighter = bigger than the limit) → **A is a PHA.**

   Careful with $H$: the condition is $H \le 22$ because a **smaller** $H$ means a **brighter, bigger** object. $H = 23$ would be too small to count as a PHA even with a tiny MOID.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: (The lecture's question, [[SSA-lecture1.1.pdf#page=23|1.1 s. 23]]) How large is the gap between what we track and what we can only estimate?

> [!example]- Answer
> ~47,360 objects are tracked and catalogued, but MASTER-8 estimates ~68,450 objects > 10 cm, 1.5 million of 1–10 cm and 230 million of 1 mm–1 cm. So even above 10 cm we track only ~70 %, and of everything > 1 mm we track roughly **0.02 %**. A 1 cm aluminium sphere (~1.4 g) at 10 km/s carries $\tfrac12 mv^2 \approx 70$ kJ, as much as ~17 g of TNT: the untracked population is where most of the risk to a spacecraft actually is (it can only be shielded against, not avoided).

- Q: What are the three subcomponents of EU SSA, and the three services of EU SST?

> [!example]- Answer
> SSA: **SST** (space surveillance and tracking), **NEO** (near-Earth objects), **SWE** (space weather events). SST services: **collision avoidance**, **re-entry analysis**, **fragmentation analysis**.

- Q: What's the difference between SST and STM?

> [!example]- Answer
> SST measures and assesses the traffic (sensors, data, services). STM is the rules, coordination and means to use space safely; it uses SST as its operational backbone.

- Q: Why is a high spatial density of debris not the same as a high collision risk?

> [!example]- Answer
> Risk depends on encounter geometry, object size, relative velocity and orbit uncertainty. Objects on similar orbits can be dense but meet slowly; objects on crossing orbits can meet at very high speed even where density is low.

- Q: Why does a population model (MASTER-8, NEOMOD) need a model of the *sensors* and not just of the objects?

> [!example]- Answer
> Because the observations are biased: surveys see only certain sky regions, times and brightnesses. To compare a modelled population with real data, you simulate what the sensors would have detected of it (apply the biases) and fit that to what they actually detected.

- Q: Why does warning time matter more than deflection force?

> [!example]- Answer
> A small change in velocity changes the orbital period slightly, and the position error it causes grows every orbit. Applied years early, a few mm/s can move the arrival point by more than an Earth radius; applied late, you need a huge (nuclear) push.
