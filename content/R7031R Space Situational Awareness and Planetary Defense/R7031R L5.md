**Question:** What can radar and laser ranging tell us about *what* an object is and *what it is doing* (size, spin, shape, mass, behaviour) that telescopes cannot, and where are their limits?
**Builds on:** [[R7031R L3]], [[R7031R L4]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Radar gives size without an albedo in two ways: from the radar cross-section (RCS), but only if you use the right scattering regime (Rayleigh vs optical) or NASA's statistical Size Estimation Model, and averaged over a pass; or for an asteroid from the Doppler bandwidth $B = 4\pi D\cos\delta/(\lambda P)$, which with $P$ from the light curve gives a lower limit on $D$. Spin comes from the repeating RCS pattern (beware the half-period), from micro-Doppler sine curves in a spectrogram, from laser-ranging residuals of a satellite's retroreflectors (Envisat: about two minutes, millisecond precision), and imaging (ISAR, delay-Doppler) gives 2-D pictures and 3-D shape models. Precise orbits give area-to-mass ratio, a moon's orbit gives an asteroid's mass and so its density, and pattern-of-life analysis turns repeated observations into a baseline so that changes stand out.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Characterisation:** what an object is and what it is doing. Recap of L4: light curves, size needs an albedo, thermal IR, colours and spectra.
- **Radar cross-section (RCS, $\sigma$):** the area of an ideal isotropic scatterer giving the same echo. Depends on size vs λ, shape, material, orientation, polarization.
- **Effective diameter from RCS:** invert the right regime (optical or Rayleigh); the wrong one biases the size.
- **Size Estimation Model (SEM):** NASA's statistical map from RCS/λ² to equivalent spherical diameter ESD/λ, fitted to real fragments. Good for populations, weak for one object.
- **Doppler bandwidth $B_{Dop}$:** the spread of echo frequencies from a rotating asteroid. Gives size without albedo.
- **Sub-radar latitude $\delta$:** the latitude on the asteroid facing the radar; $\cos\delta$ shrinks the bandwidth near a pole.
- **RCS time series ("radar light curve"):** spin from the repeating echo pattern; works in daylight and through clouds.
- **Micro-Doppler:** each rotating point adds a sinusoidal Doppler shift $2\omega r/\lambda\cdot\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$; a spectrogram shows the sines.
- **ISAR:** inverse synthetic aperture radar; range from delay, cross-range from rotation Doppler → 2-D image.
- **Delay-Doppler imaging / radar albedo:** the asteroid version; inverted into 3-D shape models. High radar albedo hints at metal.
- **Eddy-current damping and YORP:** why defunct satellites change spin over months to years.
- **Area-to-mass ratio (A/m):** from how drag and solar pressure perturb the orbit; MLI fragments have 100–1000× the A/m of intact objects.
- **Pattern-of-life analysis:** baseline → model normal → monitor → interpret departures.

### Recap: characterisation with telescopes ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=2|s. 2]])
From [[R7031R L4]]: light curves give spin period, satellite attitude from glints and binaries; size from brightness needs an albedo (thermal IR removes most of that ambiguity); colours and spectra give asteroid taxonomy and spacecraft materials. **This lecture: what radar and laser ranging add.**

### Radar size, route 1: the radar cross-section ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=3|s. 3–6]])
Radar gives two independent routes to size ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=3|s. 3]]):
1. **For any object: the RCS**, from the radar equation solved for $\sigma$ (L3):
$$
\sigma = \frac{P_r(4\pi)^3R^4L}{P_tG^2\lambda^2}
$$
   ($P_r$, $P_t$ received and transmitted power, $R$ range, $G$ antenna gain, $L$ losses.)
2. **For a rotating asteroid: the spread of Doppler shifts**, which gives size **without an albedo**.

**RCS depends on more than size ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=4|s. 4]]):** size relative to λ, shape, material, orientation, polarization. Reminder for a conducting sphere of **diameter** $a$, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ (see the RCS-vs-$ka$ curve in [[R7031R L3]]):
- **Optical** ($a \gg \lambda$): $\sigma \approx \pi a^2/4$, the projected area.
- **Resonance (Mie)** ($a \approx \lambda$): $\sigma$ oscillates around the projected area.
- **Rayleigh** ($a \ll \lambda$): $\sigma \propto a^6/\lambda^4$, a very weak echo.

Inverting each regime gives an effective diameter:
$$
a_{opt} = \sqrt{\frac{4\sigma}{\pi}}, \qquad a_{Ray} = \left(\frac{4\sigma\lambda^4}{9\pi^5}\right)^{1/6}
$$
(The Rayleigh inversion belongs to $\sigma = \frac{9\pi^5a^6}{4\lambda^4}$ for diameter $a$, the same as $9\pi r^2(kr)^4$ with radius $r = a/2$.)

> [!example] Using the wrong regime biases the size
> $\sigma = 3.2\cdot10^{-3}$ m² at UHF ($\lambda = 0.68$ m): $a_{opt} = \sqrt{4\cdot3.2\cdot10^{-3}/\pi} = 6.4$ cm, but $a_{Ray} = \left(\frac{4\cdot3.2\cdot10^{-3}\cdot0.68^4}{9\pi^5}\right)^{1/6} \approx 10$ cm. A 10 cm object is far smaller than 68 cm, so Rayleigh is right; the optical formula would make it look 40 % smaller.

**NASA's Size Estimation Model, SEM ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]):** real fragments are irregular, so sphere formulas aren't used. The SEM maps RCS to an **equivalent spherical diameter (ESD)**. It was fitted to multi-frequency radar measurements of ~40 fragments from a hypervelocity collision test (Bohannon & Young 1993), at many frequencies and orientations, and relates RCS/λ² to ESD/λ.
![[R7031R L5 s5 NASA size estimation model.png]]
*Mean RCS/λ² vs size/λ for the fragments (dots) with the SEM curves, compared with a perfect sphere (red), which has the resonance wiggles that real fragments don't (Lambour et al. 2004, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]).*

**Limits of RCS sizing ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]):**
- One RCS measurement can be off by **a factor of several** as the orientation changes → average over a pass or several passes.
- The SEM is statistical: reliable for **populations**, much less for one object.
- Non-conducting fragments (insulation, composites) reflect less than metal of the same size.

### Radar size, route 2: the Doppler bandwidth ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=7|s. 7–9]])
A rotating asteroid has one side approaching (echo shifted **up**) and one receding (shifted **down**). The total spread is the **Doppler bandwidth**:
$$
B_{Dop} = \frac{4\pi D\cos\delta}{\lambda P}
$$
$D$ diameter, $P$ spin period, λ wavelength, $\delta$ **sub-radar latitude** (the latitude on the asteroid directly facing the radar).

Where it comes from: each limb moves at $v = \frac{D}{2}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{P} = \frac{\pi D}{P}$, and the radar sees the part $v\cos\delta$ along its line of sight. A radial speed gives a Doppler shift $2v/\lambda$, so one limb is shifted by $+2v\cos\delta/\lambda$ and the other by $-2v\cos\delta/\lambda$. The spread between them is $B = \frac{4v\cos\delta}{\lambda} = \frac{4\pi D\cos\delta}{\lambda P}$.

![[R7031R L5 s7 doppler spread of a rotating asteroid.png]]
*Lines of equal Doppler shift run parallel to the spin axis (strips across the disc); circles of equal echo delay are centred on the sub-radar point (Golden 2013, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]).*

- **Geometry ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]):** near the equator ($\delta \approx 0$, $\cos\delta \approx 1$) the rotation is seen fully. Near a pole ($\delta \approx 90°$) $B_{Dop}$ can be small **even for a large asteroid**.
- The **light curve gives $P$**, radar gives $D\cos\delta$. Since $\cos\delta \le 1$:
$$
D \ge \frac{B\lambda P}{4\pi} \qquad \text{(a lower limit on the diameter)}
$$
- No albedo needed: this is what breaks the optical size–albedo degeneracy of [[R7031R L4]].

### Radar spin: the RCS time series ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=10|s. 10–11]])
A spinning object shows the radar different faces: big flat faces reflect strongly, edges weakly. Like a light curve, the RCS varies and the pattern **repeats once per rotation**.
![[R7031R L5 s10 RCS time series of a spinning object.png]]
*ALCOR narrow-band RCS over one pass, in two polarizations: a noisy but repeating pattern (Cravey et al. 1995, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=10|s. 10]]).*

**Recipe ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):**
1. Get an RCS time series. A 5–10 min LEO pass gives a long, well-sampled series.
2. Look for the repetition:
   - **periodogram** (as for light curves),
   - **autocorrelation** (the series matches itself best when shifted by one period),
   - **epoch folding** (cut into pieces of a trial length and stack them; the right period gives a sharp, repeatable pattern).
3. **Check the half-period ambiguity.** A rocket body seen side-on gives a big spike, and it's side-on **twice per turn** → the true period is usually **twice** the strongest periodicity (same trap as optical light curves, L4).
4. **Correct for geometry:** the viewing direction changes during a pass, so the pattern drifts; several passes refine the period.

The radar counterpart of a light curve (echo strength vs time instead of brightness), and it **works in daylight and through clouds**.

### Radar spin: micro-Doppler ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=12|s. 12]])
- A point on the satellite (e.g. a panel tip) goes round a circle of radius $r$ at speed $\omega r$.
- The orbital motion of the whole satellite gives a large Doppler shift, which is **predicted and removed**.
- A small extra shift remains for that point:
$$
f(t) = \frac{2\omega r}{\lambda}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
$$
  It oscillates around the body's mean Doppler like a sine: **micro-Doppler**.
- **Spectrogram:** $f(t)$ from FFTs over successive slices of the echo. Each bright point traces its own sine.
  - The **period** of the sine is the spin period $P$.
  - Points at different distances (panel tip, body corner, antenna) give **different amplitudes, same period**: together they map the object's dimensions ($r = A\lambda/(2\omega)$ from the amplitude $A$).

![[R7031R L5 s12 microdoppler spectrogram.png]]
*A spectrogram: Doppler difference vs time with echo strength in colour. The bright band wiggles periodically as the scattering points rotate (Heading 2024, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]).*

### Radar imaging: ISAR ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=13|s. 13]])
**Inverse synthetic aperture radar** uses the **target's own rotation** relative to the line of sight to form an image:
- echo **delay** separates parts by distance (range),
- **rotation Doppler** (≈ micro-Doppler) separates them across the image (cross-range),
- together: a 2-D image. It's a radar image, so some surfaces can be hard to see (material, motion).
$$
\Delta r = \frac{c}{2B}, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{\lambda}{2\Delta\theta}
$$
$B$ signal bandwidth (more bandwidth → finer range), $\Delta\theta$ the change in viewing angle during the observation (more rotation → finer cross-range).

![[R7031R L5 s13 ISAR images.png]]
*TIRA (Fraunhofer FHR, Germany) ISAR images: Envisat after its failure and Tiangong-1 during its uncontrolled re-entry in 2018 (Karamanavis et al. 2023; the paper is in the course's additional material, [[radar-isar-sat-karamanavis.pdf]]).*

### Delay-Doppler imaging and radar albedo ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=14|s. 14]])
- For asteroids the same kind of images are called **delay-Doppler images**; a sequence over a rotation is inverted into a **3-D shape model** (the SHAPE software).
- They have revealed contact binaries, moons, boulders, ridges and concavities.
- **Radar albedo** = echo strength relative to projected area: most asteroids ≈ 0.1–0.2; metal-rich (some M-types) much higher → **one of the few remote tests for metal**.

![[R7031R L5 s14 delay-doppler image and shape model.png]]
*A delay-Doppler image (top left), the shape model derived from such images (top right), and the real asteroid seen by a spacecraft (bottom) (Nolan et al. 2013, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]). The images are of Bennu, OSIRIS-REx's target.*

### Why defunct satellites change spin ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=15|s. 15]])
Spin states evolve over **months to years** after control is lost. Monitoring this is required before any removal or servicing mission (you can't grab something tumbling fast). Rocket bodies often settle into **end-over-end tumbling about their axis of maximum inertia** (the energy-dissipation rule: lowest energy for the given momentum is spin about the largest inertia).

| | Eddy-current damping | YORP (Yarkovsky–O'Keefe–Radzievskii–Paddack) |
|---|---|---|
| mechanism | a conducting body spinning in Earth's magnetic field induces currents that brake it | uneven reflection and thermal emission of sunlight give a torque, especially on asymmetric bodies (one solar wing) |
| effect | slows the spin | spins up, down, or through cycles |
| where | strongest in LEO (stronger field) | dominates in GEO |

![[R7031R L5 s15 eddy currents and YORP.png]]
*Left: Earth's magnetic field and eddy-current braking. Middle: sunlight pushing on an asymmetric body (YORP). Right: the rotation period of a defunct GOES satellite growing over 250 days, simulation vs observations (Albuja 2017, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]).*

### Laser ranging: area-to-mass ratio, mass and density ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=16|s. 16]])
Laser ranging doesn't measure size directly, but its **very precise orbits** improve the area-to-mass ratio:
- **A/m from the orbit:** drag and solar radiation pressure accelerate an object in proportion to A/m. Intact satellites and rocket bodies have low A/m; **multi-layer insulation fragments can have 100–1000× higher A/m** and drift quickly under sunlight.
- **Mass and density of asteroids:** mass needs a gravitational effect (a moon's orbit via Kepler's third law, a spacecraft flyby, or long-term orbital perturbations). Mass + volume (from radar or imaging) → **bulk density** → monolith or porous rubble pile (the Didymos example of [[R7031R L4]]).

### Laser ranging: spin ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=17|s. 17]])
- If a tumbling satellite carries a retroreflector, each reflector moves **toward and away from** the station as it turns.
- **Range residuals** = measured range − range predicted for the centre of mass. They form a **repeating pattern whose period is the spin period**.
- Millisecond-level precision on the period; fitting over a pass gives the **spin axis** too.
- When **Envisat** failed (2012), SLR showed a period of about **two minutes, gradually lengthening**.

![[R7031R L5 s17 envisat SLR range residuals.png]]
*Envisat range residuals over one pass: the oscillation comes from the displacement between the rotation axis and the retroreflector panel, which makes the distance from the station to the panel vary (Martínez & Sánchez 2025, [[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]).*

### Pattern-of-life analysis ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=18|s. 18]])
Build a model of an object's **normal behaviour** from repeated observations so that departures stand out. A single observation gives a **state**; a long record gives a **pattern**. What makes up a pattern:
- **orbit behaviour:** manoeuvre cadence, direction and size; station-keeping box; GEO longitude slot,
- **attitude behaviour:** brightness signature, glint timing, seasonal changes,
- **activity:** radio transmissions, pointing modes, safe-mode periods.

![[R7031R L5 s18 pattern of life steps.png]]
*1. Baseline (weeks to months of observations, statistics of each behaviour) → 2. Model normal (predict the next manoeuvre window, brightness, position in the box) → 3. Monitor (test each new observation against the model with uncertainty) → 4. Interpret (missed or unexpected manoeuvre, new signature) ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]).*

### Summary of characterisation ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=19|s. 19]])
- Brightness gives size only with an albedo; thermal IR or radar removes that ambiguity.
- Light curves give spin and shape; glints give satellite attitude.
- Spectra and colours reveal materials and asteroid classes.
- Laser ranging measures the spin of reflector-equipped satellites precisely.
- Object **type** comes from **combining** orbit history, light curve, radar and colour.
- Pattern of life turns repeated observations into a baseline against which change becomes visible.

| question | optical (L4) | radar (this lecture) | laser (this lecture) |
|---|---|---|---|
| size | $H$ + albedo (×3 uncertain), thermal IR | RCS (regime/SEM), Doppler bandwidth (asteroids) | indirectly via A/m |
| spin | light curve, glints | RCS series, micro-Doppler, ISAR | range residuals (retroreflectors) |
| shape | light-curve amplitude (lower limit) | ISAR, delay-Doppler → 3-D model | – |
| material | colours, spectra, polarimetry | radar albedo (metal) | – |
| works when | night, clear sky, sunlit object | day/night, through cloud | clear sky, known pointing |

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**RCS from the radar equation:** $\sigma = \dfrac{P_r(4\pi)^3R^4L}{P_tG^2\lambda^2}$.

**Diameter from RCS** (sphere of diameter $a$)
$$
a_{opt} = \sqrt{\frac{4\sigma}{\pi}} \quad (a \gg \lambda), \qquad a_{Ray} = \left(\frac{4\sigma\lambda^4}{9\pi^5}\right)^{1/6} \quad (a \ll \lambda)
$$
**Doppler bandwidth:** $B_{Dop} = \dfrac{4\pi D\cos\delta}{\lambda P}$ ⇒ $D \ge \dfrac{B\lambda P}{4\pi}$.

**Micro-Doppler:** $f(t) = \dfrac{2\omega r}{\lambda}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$, $\omega = 2\pi/P$.

**ISAR resolution:** $\Delta r = \dfrac{c}{2B}$, $\Delta x = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\Delta\theta}$.

**Density:** $\rho = M/V$, $M$ from a moon's orbit: $M \approx \dfrac{4\pi^2a^3}{GT^2}$.

## Common mistakes
- **Radius or diameter?** [[R7031R L3]] writes the sphere's RCS with $a$ = *radius* ($\sigma \approx \pi a^2$). This lecture follows its slides with $a$ = *diameter* ($\sigma \approx \pi a^2/4$). Check which one a formula uses before you insert numbers.
- **The optical formula for a small object.** For an object smaller than $\lambda$, $\sqrt{4\sigma/\pi}$ underestimates the size (6.4 cm instead of 10 cm in the example). Check $a$ against $\lambda$ afterwards.
- **One RCS value.** It can be off by a factor of several as the object turns. Use a pass average, and the SEM for irregular fragments.
- **The Doppler size as the diameter.** The bandwidth gives $D\cos\delta$, a *lower* limit on $D$. And $P$ must be in seconds.
- **The half-period in RCS series.** A cylinder is side-on twice per turn, so the true period is usually twice the strongest periodicity.
- **Micro-Doppler without removing the orbit.** The satellite's own orbital Doppler is predicted and removed first. Only then do the small sines appear. Use $\omega$ in rad/s in $2\omega r/\lambda$.
- **Degrees in the ISAR formula.** $\Delta\theta$ must be in radians: $5° = 0.0873$ rad.
- **Mass from Kepler in km and hours.** SI units: metres and seconds.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Characterisation_2_Radar_Laser.pdf#page=9|s. 4, 9, 12, 13]]) (a) Goldstone (X-band, $\lambda = 3.5$ cm) measures a Doppler bandwidth of 12.5 Hz on an asteroid whose light curve gives $P = 4.0$ h. What can you say about its diameter? (b) A LEO object has a pass-averaged RCS of $3.2\cdot10^{-3}$ m² at UHF ($\lambda = 0.68$ m). Estimate its size. (c) A tumbling satellite is observed in Ku-band ($\lambda = 1.8$ cm) with a solar-panel tip 10 m from the spin axis and $P = 120$ s. What is the micro-Doppler amplitude of the tip? (d) What ISAR resolution do you get with 1 GHz bandwidth and 5° of rotation?

**Solution:**
1. *Doppler size:* $P = 4.0\cdot3600 = 14\,400$ s.
$$
D\cos\delta = \frac{B\lambda P}{4\pi} = \frac{12.5\cdot0.035\cdot14\,400}{12.57} = \frac{6300}{12.57} = 501\ \text{m}
$$
   So $D \ge 0.50$ km. If the radar views it near its equator it's ~500 m; near a pole it could be much larger.
2. *RCS size:* guess optical: $a_{opt} = \sqrt{4\cdot3.2\cdot10^{-3}/\pi} = 0.064$ m. That is far smaller than λ = 0.68 m, so the optical formula doesn't apply. Rayleigh:
$$
a_{Ray} = \left(\frac{4\cdot3.2\cdot10^{-3}\cdot0.68^4}{9\pi^5}\right)^{1/6} = \left(\frac{2.74\cdot10^{-3}}{2754}\right)^{1/6} = (9.9\cdot10^{-7})^{1/6} \approx 0.10\ \text{m}
$$
   ≈ 10 cm, still $a < \lambda$, so consistent (strictly it's near the resonance edge; the SEM would be used in practice).
3. *Micro-Doppler:* $\omega = 2\pi/120 = 0.0524$ rad/s, tip speed $\omega r = 0.524$ m/s. Amplitude $\frac{2\omega r}{\lambda} = \frac{2\cdot0.524}{0.018} = 58$ Hz, repeating every 120 s. A body corner at 2 m would give 12 Hz with the same period.
4. *ISAR:* $\Delta r = \frac{3\cdot10^8}{2\cdot10^9} = 0.15$ m; $\Delta\theta = 5° = 0.0873$ rad, $\Delta x = \frac{0.018}{2\cdot0.0873} = 0.10$ m. A 15 × 10 cm pixel: a large satellite becomes a recognisable picture.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Why does radar give an asteroid's size without knowing its albedo, while a telescope can't?

> [!example]- Answer
> Optical brightness ∝ albedo × area, so a small bright body looks like a large dark one. The radar Doppler bandwidth depends only on geometry and rotation, $B = 4\pi D\cos\delta/(\lambda P)$; with $P$ from the light curve it gives $D\cos\delta$, a lower limit on $D$.

- Q: Why should an RCS-based size use a pass average and preferably the SEM?

> [!example]- Answer
> One RCS value can be off by a factor of several as the orientation changes, so average over a pass or several. Real fragments are irregular and not perfect spheres; the SEM is fitted to real fragments (RCS/λ² → ESD/λ). It is statistical, reliable for populations, less for single objects; non-metallic fragments reflect less.

- Q: A rocket body's RCS repeats strongly every 30 s. What is its spin period most likely?

> [!example]- Answer
> About 60 s. A cylinder seen side-on gives a big spike twice per rotation, so the strongest periodicity is usually half the true period (the half-period ambiguity).

- Q: In a micro-Doppler spectrogram, two scatterers trace sines with amplitudes 40 Hz and 10 Hz and the same period. What does that tell you?

> [!example]- Answer
> The common period is the spin period. Amplitude $= 2\omega r/\lambda$ is proportional to the distance from the spin axis, so the first point is 4× farther out (e.g. a panel tip vs a body corner): the amplitudes map the object's dimensions.

- Q: How does ISAR get resolution across the line of sight, and how do you improve it?

> [!example]- Answer
> From the target's own rotation: different parts have different Doppler shifts. $\Delta x = \lambda/(2\Delta\theta)$, so observe over a larger change in viewing angle or use a shorter wavelength. Range resolution $c/(2B)$ improves with bandwidth.

- Q: Name two mechanisms that change a dead satellite's spin, and where each dominates.

> [!example]- Answer
> Eddy-current damping (currents induced in a conducting body by Earth's field brake it; strongest in LEO) and YORP (uneven reflection and thermal emission of sunlight give a torque; spins up, down or cycles; dominates in GEO).

- Q: How did laser ranging measure Envisat's spin?

> [!example]- Answer
> Its retroreflector panel sits off the rotation axis, so as Envisat tumbles the panel moves toward and away from the station. The range residuals (measured minus predicted centre-of-mass range) oscillate with the spin period: about two minutes, slowly lengthening, with millisecond precision.

- Q: What are the four steps of pattern-of-life analysis?

> [!example]- Answer
> Baseline (weeks–months of observations), model normal behaviour (predict manoeuvres, brightness, box position), monitor (test new observations against the model with uncertainty), interpret departures (missed or unexpected manoeuvre, new signature).

- (101955) Bennu is about 490 m across and spins in 4.3 h. What Doppler bandwidth would Goldstone (X-band, $\lambda = 3.5$ cm) see if it viewed Bennu equator-on? And from 60° latitude?

> [!example]- Answer
> $P = 4.3 \cdot 3600 = 15\,480$ s. Equator-on: $B = \frac{4\pi\cdot490}{0.035\cdot15\,480} = \frac{6158}{541.8} = 11.4$ Hz. At $\delta = 60°$: $\cos60° = 0.5$, so 5.7 Hz. The same asteroid gives half the bandwidth: that is why $B$ alone gives only a lower limit on $D$.
