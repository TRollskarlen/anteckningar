**Question:** How do we find objects in space and keep knowing where they are, and what exactly can an optical telescope measure (and not measure) on the way from a faint dot in an image to an orbit with an uncertainty?
**Builds on:** [[R7031R L1]] · **Leads to:** [[R7031R L3]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Detection = a signal above the noise; tracking = linking detections to one object and following it well enough to act; the chain is detect → associate → preliminary orbit → refine → catalogue → predict/warn, and optical telescopes mostly do the first two steps (they find almost every NEO and most objects beyond LEO). A telescope is passive (needs sunlight on the target and a dark sky at the observer) and measures **angles** (RA/Dec to 0.1–1″ via astrometry against Gaia stars), angular rates and brightness, but **not distance**; its performance is set by aperture ($\propto D^2$ light, $1.22\lambda/D$ resolution), pixel scale and field of view, SNR, and above all **timing** for fast LEO objects. Because one image gives only 2 of the 6 orbit numbers, angles-only orbits have a long, thin uncertainty along the line of sight, which longer arcs, more sites, or one radar/laser range collapse.

**Why this lecture?** Before an object can be tracked, catalogued or assessed, someone has to *detect* it, and optical telescopes find almost every NEO and do most of the tracking beyond LEO. This lecture covers what a telescope can measure (angles, rates and brightness, but not distance), what sets its performance, and how a faint dot in an image becomes an orbit with an uncertainty. **It is the theory behind Assignment 2.**

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Detection:** A sensor registers a signal from an object **above its noise level** (reflected sunlight or an echo).
- **Tracking:** Linking a series of detections to one object and measuring how its position changes, well enough to act on it.
- **Orbit determination (OD):** Turning measurements into a state vector (or orbital elements) **plus an uncertainty** (covariance matrix).
- **Apparent motion:** Angular rate across the sky. LEO up to ~1°/s; NEOs 0.1–10°/day.
- **Apparent magnitude $m$ / absolute magnitude $H$:** How bright it looks / how bright it would be at 1 au from Sun and observer at zero phase.
- **Phase angle $\alpha$:** Sun–object–observer angle. Small $\alpha$ = fully lit = brightest.
- **Solar elongation $\varepsilon$:** Sun–observer–object angle. Below ~60° the sky is too bright from the ground.
- **Astrometry:** Turning a pixel position into RA/Dec by comparing with catalogue stars (Gaia DR3).
- **Pixel scale / field of view (FOV):** Sky angle per pixel / total sky angle covered.
- **SNR:** Signal-to-noise ratio. Sets whether you detect it and how precisely you measure position and brightness.
- **Rate tracking vs sidereal tracking:** Follow the target (target = point, stars = streaks) vs follow the stars (stars = points, target = streak).
- **Tracklet:** A few detections in a row with constant rate: a position, direction and rate of motion. **Attributable** = $(\alpha, \delta, \dot\alpha, \dot\delta)$.
- **UCT:** Uncorrelated track: a tracklet that matches no catalogued object (new object, manoeuvre, or fragment).

## Notes
<!-- DURING: only what is NOT on the slides. Refer to slides as (s. 12) -->

### Recap: two kinds of objects ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=2|s. 2]])
- **Artificial objects (~47,360 tracked):** working satellites + space debris (all human-made objects in orbit or re-entering that no longer work).
- **Near-Earth objects:** asteroids (~43,000 NEAs, perihelion < 1.3 au), comets (~125 Jupiter-family, perihelion < 1.3 au and period < 200 yr), and PHAs as a subset (MOID ≤ 0.05 au and $H \le 22$). See [[R7031R L1]].

### Detection and tracking ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=3|s. 3–8]])
- **Detection** is *how we find* objects: a sensor registers a signal above its noise level (reflected sunlight or echoes).
- **Tracking** is *keep knowing* where they are well enough to act on it: linking detections to one object and measuring how its position changes.
- Three ways: **optical** (telescopes, e.g. Vera C. Rubin), **radar** (e.g. Arecibo, Green Bank), **laser ranging** (e.g. Izaña-1, Tenerife).

**What each sensor measures ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]):**
- **angles** → optical sensors
- **range and range-rate** → radar
- **very precise range** → laser ranging

Orbit determination turns these into a state vector together with an uncertainty, usually a **covariance matrix**.

**What the two communities do with the orbits ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]):**

| Satellites and debris: **cataloguing** | NEOs: **discovery** |
|---|---|
| Keep a catalogue current by **re-observing** each object | Emphasis on **discovering new** objects |
| Screen it for close approaches → probability of collision | **Extend the observed arc** (add more measurements) |
| Predict re-entries | Propagate orbits **10s–100 years** ahead for impact probability |

> [!important] The processing chain ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=8|s. 8]])
> 1. **Detect:** signal above noise in one frame or pulse.
> 2. **Associate:** link detections to one object.
> 3. **Preliminary orbit:** Gauss / Laplace etc.
> 4. **Refine:** least squares / filtering → a state and its covariance.
> 5. **Catalogue:** keep the state current, or re-acquire the object.
> 6. **Predict, warn:** collision / impact probability.
>
> **Optical sensors enter mostly at steps 1–2. Radar and laser feed steps 3–5 with range, the quantity angles cannot give.**

### Optical: the basics ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=9|s. 9]])
- Optical telescopes discover **almost every NEO** and do **most of the tracking beyond LEO**.
- **Passive:** the Sun illuminates the target and the telescope collects the reflected light.
- Reflected sunlight falls as $R^{-2}$ from the observer (only one way: the Sun already lit it). Compare radar's $R^{-4}$ ([[R7031R L3]]).
- **Main weakness: it measures angles, not distance.**
- One image gives celestial coordinates and a brightness:
  - **equatorial:** right ascension $\alpha$ and declination $\delta$ (fixed to the stars)
  - **horizontal:** azimuth and altitude/elevation (fixed to the observer)

![[R7031R L2 s9 equatorial coordinates.png]]
*Equatorial coordinates ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]): right ascension is measured along the celestial equator from the vernal equinox, declination north/south of it. The star catalogue frame (ICRF) uses these.*

![[R7031R L2 s9 horizontal coordinates.png]]
*Horizontal coordinates ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]): azimuth along the horizon, altitude up from it. Natural for pointing a mount, but they change with time and place.*

### Definitions: motion and brightness ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=10|s. 10–11]])
**Apparent motion** = how fast the object's position changes across the sky as seen by the observer: an angular rate, which depends on the orbit.

| Satellites and debris | Near-Earth objects |
|---|---|
| Up to ~1°/s in LEO | Typically 0.1–10°/day |
| GEO: fixed relative to Earth, so it moves 15″/s against the stars | Much faster during close approaches |

(15″/s = Earth's rotation: 360° per sidereal day = 1,296,000″ / 86,164 s ≈ 15.04″/s.)

**Apparent brightness** = how bright it looks from here: a flux or apparent magnitude. Depends on size, reflectivity, distance, and the Sun–object–observer angles. Satellites can flash when a surface reflects sunlight at you (glints, [[R7031R L4]]); NEO brightness changes as it rotates.

**Absolute magnitude $H$ (NEOs):** the apparent magnitude it would have at **1 au from the Sun and 1 au from the observer, at zero phase angle** (fully lit).

> [!important] Apparent vs absolute magnitude ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=11|s. 11]])
> $$m = H + 5\log_{10}(r\Delta) - 2.5\log_{10}\Phi(\alpha)$$
> $r$ = distance Sun–object (au), $\Delta$ = distance object–observer (au), $\Phi(\alpha)$ = fraction of full-phase brightness at phase angle $\alpha$ (1 at $\alpha = 0$).
> Ignoring phase: $m \approx H + 5\log_{10}(r\Delta)$. At $r = \Delta = 1$ au and $\alpha = 0$: $m = H$.

![[R7031R L2 s11 sun observer body geometry.png]]
*The triangle Sun–observer–body ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]); $\alpha$ is the phase angle at the body.*

> [!tip] Plain words on magnitudes
> Magnitudes are **backwards and logarithmic**: bigger number = fainter, and 5 magnitudes = a factor 100 in brightness (1 mag ≈ 2.512×). The $5\log_{10}(r\Delta)$ term is the inverse-square law twice: once for sunlight reaching the object ($1/r^2$), once for reflected light reaching us ($1/\Delta^2$); $2.5\log_{10}(r^2\Delta^2) = 5\log_{10}(r\Delta)$.

### Measurements ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=12|s. 12]])
An optical sensor can deliver:
- **Angular positions**, typically 0.1–1″ relative to a star catalogue (**astrometry**).
- **Angular rates**, from a short sequence or the length of a streak (→ **timing** matters).
- **Brightness** (**photometry**): magnitude, its variation (light curves) and colours ([[R7031R L4]]).
- **Resolved images**, only of large satellites with large telescopes and adaptive optics (example: ESO 3.6 m, where adaptive optics turns one blob into two satellites).

For scale: 1″ = 1/3600° ≈ 4.85 µrad, i.e. ~5 m at 1000 km distance.

### Observing geometry: satellites ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=13|s. 13–14]])
A satellite is only visible when **it** is sunlit but **the observer's** sky is dark. With the Sun at depression angle $d$ below the horizon, a satellite straight overhead is lit when

$$
h > R_\oplus\left(\frac{1}{\cos d} - 1\right)
$$

![[R7031R L2 s13 earth shadow geometry.png]]
*Earth's shadow above an observer at O, with the Sun below the horizon ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]).*

**Where it comes from:** the shadow edge is the line tangent to the Earth's surface at the terminator. The point straight above the observer at distance $R_\oplus + h$ from Earth's centre is on that line when $\cos d = \dfrac{R_\oplus}{R_\oplus + h}$. Solve for $h$: $h = R_\oplus\left(\dfrac{1}{\cos d} - 1\right)$; anything higher is in sunlight.

- Astronomical twilight, $d = 18°$: $h > 6371(1.0515 - 1) \approx 330$ km.
- $d = 40°$ (middle of the night at mid-latitudes): $h \approx 1950$ km.
- → **LEO satellites are mainly visible just after sunset or before dawn.** MEO and GEO may stay lit almost all night.

**GEO eclipse seasons ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]):** around the equinoxes the Sun is in the equatorial plane, so GEO satellites pass through Earth's shadow near local midnight, up to ~70 min a night over several weeks. Observers lose them exactly when the sky is darkest.

![[R7031R L2 s14 GEO eclipse seasons.png]]
*At the spring and autumn equinoxes, the shadow cone points along the equatorial plane where GEO is ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]).*

### Observing geometry: NEOs ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=15|s. 15]])
- **Small phase angle** $\alpha$ (target opposite the Sun, "at opposition") gives the brightest view.
- **Solar elongation** $\varepsilon$ = Sun–observer–target angle. From the ground, rarely below $\varepsilon \approx 60°$: the sky is too bright.
- NEOs approaching **from the direction of the Sun**, or on orbits mostly inside Earth's (Atens, Atiras), spend their time at small elongation and are **missed**. (Chelyabinsk came from the Sun's direction.)
- **Solution:** twilight surveys and **space-based infrared telescopes** (NEO Surveyor at L1, [[R7031R L1]]).

### Telescopes and detectors ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=16|s. 16–19]])
The important system parameters: **collecting area, field of view, image sampling, and the speed and precision of the detector readout and timestamp.**

**Collecting area ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]):** $\propto D^2$ ($D$ = aperture diameter). Larger aperture → more photons → fainter objects.

**Diffraction limit:** light is a wave, so even a perfect telescope blurs a point. Rayleigh criterion for the smallest resolvable angle:
$$
\theta \approx 1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}
$$
$D = 0.5$ m, $\lambda = 550$ nm → $\theta \approx 1.34\times10^{-6}$ rad = 0.28″.

![[R7031R L2 s17 diffraction limit airy disks.png]]
*Two point sources: clearly separated (left) and at the limit of resolution (right). Each point becomes an Airy pattern of rings ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]).*

> [!warning] An ideal limit, not a guarantee ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=17|s. 17]])
> Real sharpness (and position accuracy) also depends on atmospheric turbulence ("seeing", typically 1–2″ from the ground, much worse than 0.28″), focus, tracking, pixel size and image processing.

**Focal length, focal ratio, pixel scale, FOV ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]):**
- **Focal length $f$:** distance over which the telescope brings parallel light to a focus.
- **Focal ratio** = $f/D$. An f/3 telescope with $D = 0.5$ m has $f = 3 \times 500 = 1500$ mm.
- **Pixel scale** (pixel size $p$ in µm, $f$ in mm):
$$
\text{pixel scale}\ [''/\text{px}] = 206.265\,\frac{p\ [\mu\text{m}]}{f\ [\text{mm}]}
$$
  $f = 1500$ mm, $p = 3.76$ µm → $206.265 \times 3.76/1500 \approx 0.52''$/px.
- **Field of view:** each sensor dimension divided by $f$ (small-angle approximation), in degrees. Full-frame 36 × 24 mm at 1500 mm: $36/1500 = 0.024$ rad = 1.4°, $24/1500 = 0.016$ rad = 0.9°.

> [!tip] Where does 206.265 come from?
> 1 radian = 206,265″. A pixel of size $p$ at focal length $f$ subtends $p/f$ radians. With $p$ in µm and $f$ in mm the units give a factor $10^{-3}$: $206{,}265 \times 10^{-3} = 206.265$.

**Pixel scale = sampling, FOV = coverage.** 0.52″/px does *not* mean you can resolve 0.52″: seeing, optics and tracking blur it more.

**Detectors and timing ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]):** CCD and CMOS.
- LEO satellites move 0.5–1.5°/s across the detector. Every image needs a **precise time**, because **a timing error becomes an along-track position error**.
  - At ~8 km/s, a 1 ms timestamp error = ~8 m of travel. At 1°/s, the satellite moves 3.6″ in 1 ms. For m-level positions you need **sub-ms timing**.
- **Readout speed** controls how often you get a measurement.
- **Global shutter:** all pixels start and stop together → one clear observation time.
- **Rolling shutter:** rows expose one after another → different parts of the image are at different times. A moving satellite looks **skewed**, and one timestamp for the whole image **biases** the position. The row timing must be known and used.

### SNR and the detection threshold ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=20|s. 20]])
**SNR** = the object's measured signal compared with the noise in the same measurement. High SNR → easier to separate from the background and **more precise position**.

| SNR goes up with | SNR goes down with |
|---|---|
| Larger aperture | Fainter object, greater distance |
| Longer exposure (until motion or background limits it) | Bright sky, moonlight, light pollution |
| Brighter object, higher detector efficiency | Detector read noise and thermal noise |
| | Poor seeing (light spread over more pixels) |
| | Fast apparent motion (light spread along a trail) |

![[R7031R L2 s20 SNR examples.png]]
*The same star at SNR 5, 10, 20 and 100 ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]). At 5 you can barely tell it from noise; a typical detection threshold is around SNR 5–7.*

### Tracking modes ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=21|s. 21–23]])
![[R7031R L2 s21 rate vs sidereal tracking.png]]
*Left: rate tracking (stars are streaks, the target marked in red is a point). Right: sidereal tracking (stars are points, the target is a short streak) ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=21|s. 21]], Buzzoni et al. 2024).*

| | **Active (rate) tracking** | **Surveying (sidereal tracking)** |
|---|---|---|
| Telescope | Follows a satellite whose orbit is already **known** | Stays pointed while objects cross the field |
| Target | A point: light concentrated, longer exposures raise SNR | A trail: longer exposure spreads light → lower SNR, so **short exposures** |
| Stars | Trails; long exposures leave fewer usable reference stars for astrometry | Nearly points: good astrometry |
| Mount | Demanding (LEO moves fast) | Easy (just follows the sky) |
| Finds | Poor at unexpected objects or manoeuvres | **Unknown objects**, several in one field |
| Favours | **Signal strength** for a known target | **Coverage and discovery** |

Both benefit from a wide field of view, short exposures and accurate timing.

### Detection and association ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=24|s. 24]])
The processing chain finds (i) **what is moving** in an image and (ii) **which detections belong to the same object**:
1. **Calibrate:** subtract bias and dark, divide by a flat field, mask hot pixels.
2. **Find sources** above a threshold; measure positions and fluxes.
3. **Remove what isn't moving:** match against the Gaia DR3 star catalogue and keep the unmatched sources, or subtract a reference image of the same field (**image differencing**).
4. **Detect streaks.**
5. **Link into tracklets:** detections from a few exposures (minutes to hours) that line up with a constant rate → position, direction and rate.
6. **Classify:** asteroids → the **digest2** score estimates how likely the tracklet is an NEO; satellites → compare with the catalogue.
7. **Associate:**
   - Satellites: propagate every catalogued object to the image time and match within a **gate** set by the prediction's uncertainty. Unmatched → **uncorrelated tracks (UCTs)**.
   - Asteroids: report to the **MPC**, which links to known objects or posts new NEO candidates on the **NEO Confirmation Page (NEOCP)** for follow-up.

> [!warning] False detections ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=24|s. 24]])
> Cosmic rays, hot pixels, image artefacts, satellite glints and, more and more, **streaks from large constellations**. The main defence: require **several detections with consistent motion**. A cosmic ray hits once; a real object moves in a straight line at a steady rate.

### Astrometry ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=25|s. 25]])
Astrometry turns a **pixel position into a direction on the sky**. In my own words: you compare your image with well-known stars in the same image, whose positions are in a catalogue; that tells you where in the sky every pixel points, so also where your object is.
- **Relative method:** the target is measured against reference stars in the *same* image (from e.g. **Gaia DR3**).
- **Plate solving:** match the image's stars to the catalogue → a mapping pixel ↔ (RA, Dec) → apply it to the target.
- **Gaia DR3:** positions and proper motions of ~1.8 billion stars at sub-milliarcsecond precision in the **ICRF**. The star positions must still be moved to the observation date with their proper motions.
- **Centroiding:** optics and atmosphere spread a point over several pixels (the **PSF**, point spread function). The **centroid** = the intensity-weighted centre = the position estimate.

**Centroid precision** (well-sampled point source, random error per coordinate):
$$
\sigma_\text{pos} \approx \frac{\text{FWHM}}{2.35 \cdot \text{SNR}}
$$
FWHM = 2″, SNR = 20 → $2/(2.35 \times 20) \approx 0.04''$. So you can measure a position **50× finer than the blur itself**: you're finding the centre of a well-measured bump, not resolving detail. (2.35 is the factor between FWHM and the standard deviation $\sigma$ of a Gaussian.)

> [!warning] In practice, systematic errors dominate ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=25|s. 25]])
> Seeing in short exposures, uncorrected optical distortion, differential atmospheric refraction across wide fields, poor sampling, and for satellites: **timing**.

### Orbit determination and uncertainty ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=26|s. 26]])
- An orbit has **six** parameters. One optical observation gives **two** (RA, Dec). → Angles-only OD must combine many observations over time.
- A tracklet gives position + angular rate: $(\alpha, \delta, \dot\alpha, \dot\delta)$, called an **attributable**: 4 of 6 numbers; the missing two are **range and range-rate**.
- **Preliminary orbit (two-body)** from ≥ 3 well-separated observations:
  - **Gauss's method:** three directions and the times between them → solve for the three ranges → the orbit. Works best when the observations span a reasonable fraction of the orbit.
  - Others: Laplace, Gooding, double-R, admissible-region methods, statistical ranging, least-squares fitting.
- **Refinement:** batch least squares (or a Kalman filter, see [[R7026R L7]]) with all observations and a **full force model**: Earth's gravity field, Moon and Sun, solar radiation pressure, drag (LEO); planetary perturbations and the **Yarkovsky effect** (thermal recoil) for NEOs. Output: best state + covariance.

> [!important] The shape of angles-only uncertainty ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=26|s. 26]])
> Angles constrain position **across** the line of sight well and **range poorly**. The uncertainty region is a **long, thin ellipsoid along the line of sight**; propagated forward it stretches mainly **along the orbit**.
> Collapse it with: **longer arcs**, observations from **widely separated sites** (parallax), or **one radar or laser range**.

The final orbit accuracy depends on the whole chain: centroiding, reference stars, timing, shutter, and how current the orbit estimate is.

### Bridge to Assignment 2 (my addition, not on the slides)
Assignment 2 measures (65803) Didymos in real telescope images. These are the pieces it needs that the lecture only touches on:
- **Mid-exposure time.** The position you measure is the average over the exposure, so it belongs to the *middle* of it: $t_\text{mid} = \texttt{DATE-OBS} + \texttt{EXPTIME}/2$ (`DATE-OBS` is the start). A wrong time tag becomes a position error of (rate × time error), exactly as in *Detectors and timing* above.
- **WCS (World Coordinate System).** The FITS header keywords that store the plate solution, i.e. the mapping pixel ↔ (RA, Dec): the reference pixel `CRPIX1/2`, its sky position `CRVAL1/2`, and the `CD` matrix (degrees per pixel, including rotation). The pixel scale is $\sqrt{\texttt{CD1\_1}^2 + \texttt{CD2\_1}^2}\times3600$ ″/px. Compare it with $206.265\,p/f$.
- **O − C residuals.** Observed minus computed: your measured position minus the ephemeris position (JPL Horizons) at the same time. In RA, use $\Delta\alpha\cos\delta$. Lines of constant RA converge toward the poles, so the same RA difference is a smaller angle on the sky at high declination: at $\delta = 60°$, a 2″ difference in RA is only 1″ on the sky.
- **Reading the residuals.** Residuals that scatter around zero give your measurement precision. A *trend* (residuals drifting with time) points to a systematic error: a time offset (it shows along the direction of motion), a wrong observatory code, or an ephemeris that needs more data.
- **Arc length.** A short arc leaves the range, and so the orbit, poorly constrained (the thin ellipsoid above). The longer the arc, the smaller the uncertainty of a Find_Orb fit. This is why part E asks you to discuss the arc length when you compare with JPL SBDB.
- **MPC 80-column format.** One fixed-width text line per observation: designation, date in UT as a decimal day, RA (h m s), Dec (° ′ ″), magnitude, and the observatory code. AstroImageJ's Multi-Aperture output can be turned into these lines.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Magnitude**
$$
m = H + 5\log_{10}(r\Delta) - 2.5\log_{10}\Phi(\alpha) \;\approx\; H + 5\log_{10}(r\Delta)
$$
$m$ = apparent magnitude, $H$ = absolute magnitude, $r$ = Sun–object distance (au), $\Delta$ = object–observer distance (au), $\Phi(\alpha)$ = phase function. Brightness ratio: $F_1/F_2 = 10^{-0.4(m_1 - m_2)}$.

**Earth's shadow (satellite at zenith lit if)**
$$
h > R_\oplus\left(\frac{1}{\cos d} - 1\right)
$$
$d$ = Sun's depression angle below the horizon, $R_\oplus = 6371$ km.

**Diffraction limit**
$$
\theta \approx 1.22\frac{\lambda}{D} \quad [\text{rad}]
$$

**Pixel scale and field of view**
$$
\text{pixel scale}\ [''/\text{px}] = 206.265\,\frac{p\ [\mu\text{m}]}{f\ [\text{mm}]}, \qquad \text{FOV} \approx \frac{\text{sensor size}}{f}\ [\text{rad}], \qquad f = (f/D)\cdot D
$$

**Centroid precision**
$$
\sigma_\text{pos} \approx \frac{\text{FWHM}}{2.35\cdot\text{SNR}}
$$

**Timing error → along-track error**
$$
\Delta x = v\,\Delta t, \qquad \Delta\theta = \dot\theta\,\Delta t
$$

″ = arcseconds (1° = 3600″, 1 rad = 206,265″).

**Mid-exposure time and RA residual (Assignment 2)**
$$
t_\text{mid} = \texttt{DATE-OBS} + \tfrac12\texttt{EXPTIME}, \qquad (O - C)_\alpha = (\alpha_\text{obs} - \alpha_\text{eph})\cos\delta, \qquad (O - C)_\delta = \delta_\text{obs} - \delta_\text{eph}
$$

## Common mistakes
- **Magnitudes backwards.** A larger $m$ is *fainter*. 5 magnitudes = a factor 100, so 1 mag ≈ 2.512×.
- **Kilometres in the magnitude formula.** $r$ and $\Delta$ go in as au.
- **Pixel scale = resolution.** The pixel scale is only the sampling. The real blur is set by seeing (1–2″ from the ground), optics and tracking.
- **Losing the 206,265.** Convert arcseconds to radians before multiplying by a range: 1″ at 1000 km is about 5 m.
- **Degrees, radians and millimetres in the FOV.** Sensor size / focal length (same units) gives radians. Convert to degrees afterwards.
- **Time-tagging at the start of the exposure.** `DATE-OBS` is the start. Use the middle of the exposure.
- **RA residuals without $\cos\delta$.** They come out too large, more so at high declination.
- **"More pixels = more precise."** The centroid precision depends on the FWHM and the object's SNR. For fast LEO objects, timing usually dominates anyway (worked example).

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=17|s. 17–19]], [[Detection_and_tracking_optical.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]) An SSA station has a 0.5 m f/3 telescope with a full-frame 36 × 24 mm CMOS camera, 3.76 µm pixels. Seeing gives star images of FWHM = 2″. It observes a LEO satellite at 1000 km range moving at 7.5 km/s across the line of sight, and reaches SNR = 20.
1. Focal length, pixel scale and field of view?
2. Diffraction limit at 550 nm? Is the image limited by diffraction or seeing?
3. Random centroid error? In metres at the satellite?
4. The timestamp is off by 1 ms. Compare the resulting error with (3).
5. Is the satellite lit at the zenith when the Sun is 18° below the horizon, if it orbits at 500 km?

**Solution:**
1. $f = 3 \times 500 = 1500$ mm. Pixel scale $= 206.265 \times 3.76/1500 = 0.517''$/px. FOV $= 36/1500 = 0.024$ rad $= 1.38°$ by $24/1500 = 0.016$ rad $= 0.92°$.
2. $\theta = 1.22 \times 550\times10^{-9}/0.5 = 1.34\times10^{-6}$ rad $= 1.34\times10^{-6} \times 206{,}265 = 0.28''$. Seeing (2″) is 7× worse → **seeing-limited**. The 2″ star covers $2/0.517 \approx 4$ pixels across: well sampled.
3. $\sigma_\text{pos} = 2/(2.35 \times 20) = 0.043''$. In radians: $0.043/206{,}265 = 2.1\times10^{-7}$ rad; at 1000 km: $2.1\times10^{-7}\times10^6\text{ m} \approx 0.2$ m.
4. Angular rate $= v/\rho = 7.5/1000 = 7.5\times10^{-3}$ rad/s $= 0.43°$/s. In 1 ms the satellite moves $v\Delta t = 7.5$ km/s × 1 ms = **7.5 m** along track, i.e. $0.43°/\text{s} \times 0.001\text{ s} = 1.5''$. That's **~35× larger** than the centroid error (7.5 m vs 0.2 m): **timing dominates**, exactly the slide's point. Sub-ms (ideally µs, GNSS-disciplined) timing is needed.
5. Shadow height at $d = 18°$: $6371(1/\cos 18° - 1) = 6371 \times 0.0515 = 328$ km. $500 > 328$ → **lit**, visible. (At $d = 40°$ the shadow is at 1946 km: a 500 km satellite would be in darkness.)

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Why is optical mostly in steps 1–2 of the chain, and radar/laser in 3–5?

> [!example]- Answer
> Telescopes see far, cover a lot of sky and detect faint things (good for finding and linking), but give only angles. Steps 3–5 (orbit, refine, catalogue) need distance, which only radar and laser measure directly.

- Q: An NEO has $H = 22$ and is at $r = 1.2$ au, $\Delta = 0.3$ au. Apparent magnitude, ignoring phase?

> [!example]- Answer
> $m = 22 + 5\log_{10}(1.2 \times 0.3) = 22 + 5\log_{10}(0.36) = 22 - 2.22 = 19.8$.

- Q: Rate tracking or sidereal tracking for a survey looking for new debris after a break-up? Why?

> [!example]- Answer
> Sidereal (survey) tracking: the orbits of the new fragments aren't known yet, so you can't follow them; staring catches unknown objects, several per field, with good reference stars. Use short exposures to keep the trails' SNR up.

- Q: Why are NEOs coming from the direction of the Sun missed, and what fixes it?

> [!example]- Answer
> They are at small solar elongation ($\varepsilon < 60°$), where the sky is too bright from the ground. Twilight surveys help a little; space-based IR telescopes (NEO Surveyor at L1) can look close to the Sun.

- Q: What does the uncertainty of an angles-only orbit look like, and what shrinks it fastest?

> [!example]- Answer
> A long thin ellipsoid along the line of sight (range unknown), which stretches along the orbit when propagated. A single radar or laser range measurement collapses it; longer arcs and widely separated sites also help.

- Q: How do you tell a real moving object from a cosmic ray?

> [!example]- Answer
> Require several detections in different exposures that line up with a consistent rate (a tracklet). A cosmic ray or hot pixel appears once or doesn't move consistently.

- Q: A frame has `DATE-OBS = 2022-09-27T03:15:20` and `EXPTIME = 60`. Didymos is measured at RA 05h 20m 10.40s, Dec +20° 30′ 00.0″, and Horizons gives RA 05h 20m 10.30s, Dec +20° 29′ 59.5″ for the same time. Time tag and O − C in arcseconds?

> [!example]- Answer
> $t_\text{mid}$ = 03:15:20 + 30 s = **03:15:50** UT. RA difference 0.10 s of time = $0.10 \times 15 = 1.5''$ of RA; times $\cos 20.5° = 0.937$ → $(O - C)_\alpha = 1.4''$. $(O - C)_\delta = +0.5''$. (1 second of RA = 15″, because 24 h = 360°.)
