**Question:** Once we know *where* an object is, how can we tell *what* it is and what it is doing (size, shape, spin, attitude, material) when almost every object is just an unresolved dot?
**Builds on:** [[R7031R L2]], [[R7031R L3]] · **Leads to:** [[R7031R L5]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Characterisation infers size, shape, spin, attitude, material and behaviour **indirectly**, from how measurable quantities vary: the light curve (brightness vs time) gives the spin period (beware: a double-peaked curve tempts you to half the true period), shape (amplitude $\Delta m \approx 2.5\log_{10}(a/b)$ is a lower limit on elongation) and, for satellites, the attitude state (stable vs tumbling) and flat surfaces (glints). Brightness gives only size × albedo ($D = 1329/\sqrt{p_V}\cdot10^{-H/5}$ km, a factor ~3 uncertain), which thermal infrared breaks because heat depends mainly on area; colours and spectra reveal material (asteroid classes C/S/X/V/D, solar cells, gold MLI, bare aluminium) and polarimetry the surface texture. Spin rates reveal rubble piles (the ~2.2 h spin barrier), mutual events reveal binaries and their mass (Kepler's third law, how DART's effect was measured).

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Characterisation:** Determining *what* an object is and *what it's doing*: size, shape, spin, attitude, material, type, behaviour.
- **Unresolved object:** A point for the sensor: no image detail, so everything comes from variations in measured quantities.
- **Light curve:** Brightness vs time. The richest single source of information for an unresolved object.
- **Periodogram (Lomb–Scargle):** Shows which frequencies carry power in unevenly sampled data → spin period.
- **Phase dispersion minimisation (PDM):** Fold the data at trial periods; the true period gives the tightest curve.
- **Half-period alias:** An elongated body gives two maxima per rotation → the strongest periodogram peak is often at **half** the true period.
- **Geometric albedo $p_V$:** How reflective the surface is (0.03 dark carbon … 0.5 bright).
- **Size–albedo degeneracy:** Optical brightness ∝ albedo × area. A small bright object looks like a big dark one.
- **Thermal infrared:** Re-emitted heat (~10 µm). Depends mainly on area → size to 10–20 %, albedo from $H$.
- **Spin barrier:** No asteroid larger than ~200–300 m spins faster than ~2.2 h → they're rubble piles held by gravity.
- **Mutual events:** Eclipses/occultations in a binary system → sharp dips → moon's orbital period → system mass.
- **Glint:** A flat surface flashes when its normal **bisects** the Sun–object–observer angle.
- **Colour index:** Magnitude difference between two filters, e.g. $g - r$. A cheap fingerprint of material.
- **Asteroid taxonomy:** C (carbonaceous, dark), S (stony), X (E/M/P), V (basaltic, Vesta), D (very red, primitive).
- **Polarimetry:** Degree of linear polarisation vs phase angle → albedo, surface texture, mirror-like vs diffuse.

## Notes

### Recap and motivation ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=2|s. 2–3]])
Detection finds objects, tracking links detections, OD turns angles/range/range-rate into a state + covariance, and the outputs are a catalogue, collision probability and impact probability ([[R7031R L2]], [[R7031R L3]]). **All this says where an object is and will be, but almost nothing about what it is.**

**Characterisation** = what an object is and what it's doing: size, shape, spin, attitude, material, type and behaviour. Why it matters:
- **Collision and re-entry risk** depend on mass, size and **area-to-mass ratio**.
- **Planetary defence** depends on size, density, spin and structure: they set the **impact energy** and **how the asteroid responds to deflection** (a rubble pile absorbs a kinetic impactor differently than a monolith).
- **Debris removal and in-orbit servicing** need the target's spin state and attitude before approaching.
- **Pattern-of-life (PoL)** analysis: the normal sequence of natural and operational behaviours of a satellite, so deviations stand out.

**Most objects are unresolved points for every sensor.** Characterisation comes indirectly, from how measurable quantities **vary**.

### What each observable reveals ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=4|s. 4]])

| Observable | Sensor | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Brightness and its variation (light curve) | Optical | Size (with albedo), spin period, shape, attitude, glints from flat surfaces |
| Colours and spectra | Optical, near-IR | Material and surface type, asteroid taxonomy, satellite surfaces |
| Thermal emission | Infrared | Size (almost independent of albedo) |
| Polarisation | Optical, radar | Surface texture and roughness, man-made vs natural |
| RCS and its fluctuation | Radar | Size class, spin, tumbling |
| Range–Doppler and ISAR images | Radar | Shape, dimensions, rotation axis, appendages |
| Range residuals to reflectors | Laser | Precise spin period and axis of reflector-equipped satellites |
| Orbit history | All | Manoeuvres, decay, area-to-mass ratio, break-ups |

### Light curves ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=5|s. 5]])
A light curve is brightness measured over time. Three effects overlap:
1. **Geometry:** distance to Sun and observer, phase angle → **slow** changes, removed by **reduction** (calibrating the raw data to a standard distance and phase).
2. **Rotation:** the projected sunlit area and surface angles change → the **periodic** part.
3. **Surface scattering:** diffuse surfaces (rock, paint) vary **smoothly**; mirror-like surfaces (solar panels, foil) give **short, bright glints**.

![[R7031R L4 s5 light curve of a rotating body.png]]
*An elongated asteroid rotating once in ~7 h: brightest when we see its long side, faintest end-on. **Two maxima per rotation** ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]).*

### Finding the spin period ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=6|s. 6]])
- **Periodograms** (Lomb–Scargle for uneven sampling): which frequencies carry power.
- **Fourier-series fit** of the folded light curve, with period and number of harmonics as free parameters → **the standard for asteroids**.
- **Phase dispersion minimisation:** fold at trial periods, keep the one giving the tightest curve.
- **Glint timing** (satellites): repeated glints from the same panel give the period **directly**.

"Folding" = plotting brightness against $(t \bmod P)/P$, the rotation phase. At the right $P$ all the rotations lie on top of each other.

> [!warning] Pitfalls ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=7|s. 7–9]])
> - **Half-period alias:** an elongated body shows **two maxima and two minima per rotation** → the strongest peak is often at **half** the true period.
> - **Sampling gaps** (daylight, weather, one site) create spurious periods and aliasing → multi-longitude campaigns break them.
> - The observed (synodic) period shifts slightly as the geometry changes.
> - Defunct satellites can spin in **seconds** → exposures must be much shorter than the period.
> - In one LEO pass the **phase angle changes by tens of degrees**, so brightness can change even if attitude and rotation don't.

How to catch the half-period trap: fold at both $P$ and $2P$. If the two halves of the curve at $2P$ look **different** (real bodies are never perfectly symmetric), $2P$ is the true period.

### Size ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=10|s. 10–12]])
**No sensor measures the size of an unresolved object directly.** Each method measures size × something unknown:

| Optical | Thermal IR | Radar | Orbit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size × albedo | Size (weakly albedo) | RCS: size, shape, material, orientation | Area-to-mass ratio |

**Combining methods breaks the degeneracies**, e.g. thermal size + optical brightness → albedo.

**Optical size of an asteroid ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):** reflected light ∝ albedo × cross-section:
$$
D = \frac{1329\ \text{km}}{\sqrt{p_V}}\cdot10^{-H/5}
$$

| $H$ | $D$ at $p_V = 0.05$ | $D$ at $p_V = 0.14$ | $D$ at $p_V = 0.25$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17.75 | ≈ 1.7 km | ≈ 1.0 km | ≈ 0.75 km |
| 22.0 | ≈ 240 m | ≈ 140 m | ≈ 105 m |
| 25.0 | ≈ 60 m | ≈ 35 m | ≈ 27 m |

- Albedo ranges from 0.03 (dark carbonaceous) to ~0.5 → **diameter uncertain by a factor ~3** from visible light alone.
- That's where the PHA limit "$H \le 22$ ≈ 140 m" ([[R7031R L1]]) comes from: it assumes $p_V = 0.14$.
- Satellites: the same degeneracy (area × reflectivity), made worse by glints and attitude.

> [!tip] Why $\sqrt{p_V}$ and $10^{-H/5}$?
> Brightness ∝ $p_V D^2$. A fixed brightness means $D \propto 1/\sqrt{p_V}$. And 5 magnitudes = factor 100 in brightness = factor 10 in $D$ (because brightness ∝ $D^2$), hence $10^{-H/5}$.

**Thermal infrared ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]):**
- Absorbed sunlight is re-emitted as heat, peaking near **10 µm** for NEOs at ~1 au.
- Thermal flux depends mainly on **area**, only weakly on albedo: a dark object absorbs more and gets warmer, compensating for its low reflectivity.
- Thermal models give diameters to **~10–20 %**.
- Thermal diameter + $H$ → albedo → a hint at composition.
- **NEOWISE** sized tens of thousands of asteroids this way; **NEO Surveyor** is built around the same principle.
- Needs cooled detectors and a telescope **in space** (the atmosphere absorbs and glows in the IR).

### Shape: amplitude and elongation ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=13|s. 13]])
For a triaxial ellipsoid ($a \ge b \ge c$) seen **equator-on** at **zero phase**, the peak-to-peak amplitude:
$$
\Delta m \approx 2.5\log_{10}(a/b)
$$
- $\Delta m = 0.75$ mag → $a/b = 10^{0.75/2.5} = 10^{0.3} \approx 2$.
- Amplitude **grows with phase angle** (shadows deepen) → reduce to zero phase first.
- **Pole-on**, there's almost no variation, however elongated the body.

![[R7031R L4 s13 phase angle.png]]
*Phase angle: Sun–asteroid–observer ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]). At large phase, shadows on the surface exaggerate the amplitude.*

> [!important] The amplitude is a **lower limit** on elongation
> Any viewing direction other than equator-on reduces the brightness swing, so the true $a/b$ is **at least** $10^{\Delta m/2.5}$.

### The spin barrier ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=14|s. 14–15]])
- Rotation periods range from under a minute to months.
- **Almost no asteroid larger than ~200–300 m spins faster than ~2.2 h.** At that rate the centrifugal force at the equator equals gravity.
- → Most larger asteroids are **gravity-held rubble piles** (loose aggregates).
- Smaller, faster rotators must be held together by internal strength or cohesion (**monoliths**).
- Record: a main-belt asteroid of $D = 710$ m spinning in **1.88 min** (Rubin, 2025 MN45) → must be solid rock.

![[R7031R L4 s14 spin barrier.png]]
*Period vs diameter for thousands of asteroids ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]). Above ~0.2 km the points stop at the red dashed line (~2.2 h): the spin barrier. Only small ones go faster.*

**Where does ~2.2 h come from?** At the equator of a sphere of density $\rho$: gravity $= GM/R^2$ with $M = \frac43\pi R^3\rho$; centrifugal $= \omega^2R$. Equal when $\omega^2 = \frac43\pi G\rho$, so
$$
P_\text{crit} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{G\rho}}
$$
$\rho = 2000$ kg/m³ → $\sqrt{3\pi/(6.674\times10^{-11}\times2000)} = 8400$ s ≈ **2.3 h**. Independent of size! Faster than this, loose rubble would fly off the equator.

### Binaries and mutual events ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=16|s. 16]])
- ~**15 %** of NEAs larger than a few hundred metres have a moon.
- Light curve: **two periods superimposed**, plus **sharp dips** when one body eclipses or occults the other (mutual events).
- Timing the mutual events → the moon's orbital period → **system mass** via Kepler's third law:
$$
M = \frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GT^2}
$$
- This is how the change of **Dimorphos's orbit after DART** was measured from the ground: the period dropped from 11 h 55 min to 11 h 23 min (−32 min).

![[R7031R L4 s16 binary mutual events.png]]
*Dimorphos orbiting Didymos; a dip in brightness when it passes into Didymos's shadow ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]).*

### Satellites: attitude states ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=17|s. 17]])

| Attitude state | Typical light curve |
|---|---|
| **Three-axis stabilised** | Smooth → glints at predictable moments |
| **Spin-stabilised** | Strictly periodic, stable period |
| **Slow tumbling** (defunct) | Large-amplitude periodic variation → period drifts |
| **Fast or complex tumbling** | Two or more periods → may never repeat |

**A satellite moving from the first row to the third has almost certainly lost attitude control.**

![[R7031R L4 s17 spin-stabilised vs tumbling light curves.png]]
*Light curves (top) and periodograms (bottom) of a box-wing satellite: spin-stabilised (left, one clean peak) vs tumbling (right, several peaks and irregular variation) ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]).*

### Glints ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=18|s. 18]])
- A flat panel glints only when its **normal bisects the Sun–object–observer angle** (mirror law: angle in = angle out).
- A glint's timing → the panel's orientation **at that instant**.
- Glints seen **simultaneously from two sites**, or **repeatedly in one pass**, constrain the attitude.
- Their **repetition gives the spin period**.
- GEO satellites near the **equinoxes** show strong glints from their Sun-tracking solar panels (the Sun is in the equatorial plane, so the geometry lines up for observers near the equator).

![[R7031R L4 s18 glint light curve.png]]
*A tumbling satellite: sharp, regularly repeating glints (red circles) on top of the smooth light curve ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]).*

### Colours ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=19|s. 19]])
- How reflectance changes with wavelength is a **fingerprint of surface material**.
- **Colour index** = magnitude difference between two filters, e.g. $g - r$. (Bigger $g - r$ = redder.)
- Dividing by the Sun's colour → a **reflectance slope**.
- Colours need far less light than spectra → surveys (Rubin) can classify **very many faint objects**.

> [!warning] Interleave filters fast
> If the object rotates between the $g$ and $r$ exposures, a brightness change looks like a colour. Interleave filters quickly or correct for the rotation.

### Asteroid taxonomy ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=20|s. 20]])

| Class | Spectrum | Albedo | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C** | Flat (sometimes a 0.7 µm band) | ≈ 0.05 | Carbonaceous (Bennu, Ryugu) |
| **S** | Red, bands near 1 and 2 µm | ≈ 0.25 | Stony (Itokawa, Eros) |
| **X** (E, M, P) | Featureless, moderately red | E high, M mid, P low | Enstatite, metal-rich?, primitive |
| **V** | Very deep 1 and 2 µm bands | ≈ 0.35 | Basaltic, from Vesta |
| **D** | Very red, featureless | ≈ 0.05 | Organic-rich, primitive |

![[R7031R L4 s20 Bus-DeMeo taxonomy key.png]]
*The Bus–DeMeo taxonomy key: reflectance spectra shape for each class from 0.45 to 2.45 µm ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]).*

Note how X splits only by **albedo**: the spectra look alike, so you need thermal IR (→ albedo) to tell a metal asteroid (M) from a primitive one (P). Another case of combining methods.

### Satellite materials ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=21|s. 21]])
Colours can tell a payload covered in solar panels from a bare rocket body, or identify insulation fragments:
- **Solar cells:** dark and bluish → reflectance drops into the near-IR where the cells absorb.
- **Multi-layer insulation (MLI):** gold Kapton absorbs blue strongly → **steep red slope**.
- **Aluminium, white paint:** bright and fairly flat. Aluminium has a weak feature near 0.85 µm.

![[R7031R L4 s21 satellite material reflectance.png]]
*Lab reflectance spectra of spacecraft materials: metals, polymers, composites, ceramics ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=21|s. 21]], Reyes et al. 2023).*

### Polarimetry ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=22|s. 22]])
Degree of linear polarisation vs phase angle:
- **Asteroids:** relates to albedo and surface texture.
- **Satellites:** specular (mirror-like) surfaces polarise strongly at certain angles → mirror-like vs diffuse materials.

### The Armageddon asteroid ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=23|s. 23–24]])
What would our methods have told mission planners before launch?

| Property | Method | What it would show |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Brightness + thermal IR | "Size of Texas" ≈ 1000 km, comparable to Ceres. No known NEO is close: it would have been catalogued long before an 18-day warning |
| Shape | Light curves | Real small bodies are spinning tops (Bennu, Ryugu), peanuts (Itokawa), elongated (Eros): no tall jagged spires |
| Composition | Colours, spectra, albedo | One night from the ground places it in a class (C, S, X…) and constrains density and strength |
| Rotation | Light curve | Spin period and whether it tumbles (essential for any landing) |

### Summary ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=25|s. 25]])
- **Light curves** → spin period, shape and, for satellites, attitude (watch the half-period alias!).
- **Brightness** → size only together with an albedo. **Thermal IR** largely removes that ambiguity.
- **Amplitude** is a lower limit on elongation. **Spin rates** reveal rubble piles. **Mutual events** reveal binaries.
- **Glints** reveal flat surfaces and their orientation. Repeated glints reveal tumbling.
- **Colours and spectra** reveal materials: asteroid classes, solar cells, insulation, bare metal.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Asteroid diameter from $H$**
$$
D = \frac{1329\ \text{km}}{\sqrt{p_V}}\,10^{-H/5}
$$
$p_V$ = geometric albedo, $H$ = absolute magnitude.

**Light-curve amplitude (equator-on, zero phase)**
$$
\Delta m \approx 2.5\log_{10}(a/b) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a/b \ge 10^{\Delta m/2.5}
$$

**Critical spin period (rubble pile)**
$$
P_\text{crit} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{G\rho}} \approx 2.2\text{–}2.3\ \text{h for } \rho \approx 2000\text{–}2200\ \text{kg/m}^3
$$

**Binary system mass (Kepler III)**
$$
M = \frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GT^2}
$$
$a$ = moon's orbital radius, $T$ = its orbital period, $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻².

**Colour index:** $g - r = m_g - m_r$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=35|s. 35–36]], [[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=11|s. 11]], [[Characterisation_1_Optical.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]) A newly found NEA has $H = 22.0$. Its light curve varies by 0.8 mag; the periodogram's best period is 2.5 h, but the two halves of the folded curve don't look alike. Later, short dips appear every ~11.9 h.
1. Diameter range (albedo unknown, 0.05–0.25)? What would settle it?
2. True rotation period and minimum elongation?
3. Is it above the spin barrier? Rubble pile or monolith?
4. The dips come from a moon orbiting at ~1.19 km. System mass and (if the primary is ~780 m across) density?

**Solution:**
1. $10^{-22/5} = 10^{-4.4} = 3.98\times10^{-5}$.
   - $p_V = 0.05$: $D = 1329/0.224 \times 3.98\times10^{-5} = 5943 \times 3.98\times10^{-5} = 0.237$ km ≈ **240 m**.
   - $p_V = 0.25$: $D = 1329/0.5 \times 3.98\times10^{-5} = 0.106$ km ≈ **105 m**.
   - Factor ~2.3 apart. **Thermal infrared** (size almost independent of albedo) or colours/spectrum (→ class → typical albedo) would settle it.
2. Unequal halves → 2.5 h is the half-period alias; **true $P = 5.0$ h** (two maxima per rotation). $a/b \ge 10^{0.8/2.5} = 10^{0.32} = 2.1$: at least twice as long as wide (lower limit, since we may not see it equator-on).
3. 5.0 h > ~2.2 h → **slower than the spin barrier**, so a rubble pile is possible (nothing forces it to be a monolith). Had it really spun in 2.5 h at 240 m, it would be close to the limit.
4. $T = 11.9 \times 3600 = 42{,}840$ s. $M = 4\pi^2(1190)^3/(6.674\times10^{-11}\times42{,}840^2) = 4\pi^2 \times 1.685\times10^9/(6.674\times10^{-11}\times1.835\times10^9) = 6.65\times10^{10}/0.1225 = $ **5.4 × 10¹¹ kg**.
   Volume of a 780 m sphere: $\frac43\pi(390)^3 = 2.48\times10^8$ m³ → $\rho = 5.4\times10^{11}/2.48\times10^8 \approx$ **2200 kg/m³**, lower than solid rock (~3000+) → porous → **rubble pile**. (These are the real Didymos–Dimorphos numbers.)

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: (In-class 1) A GEO object has stayed within 0.1° of the same longitude for a year. Its brightness changes smoothly through the night, with one bright flash around midnight.

> [!example]- Answer
> An **active, stabilised GEO satellite**. The flash is a solar-panel glint. Confirm: repeated astrometry shows it stays in its longitude box (station-keeping), and the glint returns at the same time on the following nights.

- Q: (In-class 2) A GEO object hasn't manoeuvred for 8 months and drifts slowly east. Its brightness repeats every 40 s with sharp flashes.

> [!example]- Answer
> A **dead GEO payload, tumbling**. No station-keeping (drift), flashes from flat panels rotating every 40 s. Confirm: astrometry over more weeks to follow the drift, light curves to follow the spin period (it will drift).

- Q: (In-class 3) A LEO object has never had its orbit adjusted. Light curve repeats every 20 s with two equal peaks per cycle; colours are grey and fairly flat.

> [!example]- Answer
> A **rocket body tumbling end over end**: two equal peaks from a cylinder (broadside twice per rotation), bare-metal colours, no manoeuvres. Confirm: light curves over several nights (stable double-peaked period, no panel glints) and multi-colour photometry.

- Q: (In-class 4) Several faint new objects near an old satellite's orbit. They are very red, and sunlight pushes their orbits around much faster than expected.

> [!example]- Answer
> **MLI (insulation) fragments** shed by the satellite: very red (gold Kapton), and a very high area-to-mass ratio (solar radiation pressure moves them a lot). Confirm: trace their orbits back to the parent; compare colours with insulation.

- Q: (In-class 5) An asteroid varies by 0.8 mag; best period 2.5 h, but the two halves of the folded curve differ.

> [!example]- Answer
> An **elongated asteroid with true period 5 h**: two maxima per rotation, unequal halves mean 2.5 h is half the period. $a/b \ge 10^{0.8/2.5} \approx 2.1$. Confirm: fold at 5 h; observe at another geometry.

- Q: (In-class 6) One period in the light curve, plus short sharp dips every ~12 h.

> [!example]- Answer
> A **binary asteroid**: the dips are mutual events (eclipses/occultations), the moon orbits in ~12 h. Confirm: time the dips over several nights → the moon's orbit → system mass.

- Q: Why can't you get an asteroid's size from its brightness alone, and how do you fix it?

> [!example]- Answer
> Brightness ∝ albedo × area: a small bright asteroid looks like a big dark one (albedo 0.03–0.5 → size uncertain by ~3×). Thermal IR depends mainly on area, giving size to 10–20 %; then $H$ + size gives the albedo.

- Q: Why is the light-curve amplitude only a lower limit on elongation?

> [!example]- Answer
> The full swing $2.5\log_{10}(a/b)$ is only seen equator-on. From any other angle the projected area varies less (pole-on: not at all), so the observed amplitude underestimates $a/b$.
