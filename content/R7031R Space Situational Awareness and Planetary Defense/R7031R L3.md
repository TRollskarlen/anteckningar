**Question:** How do radar and laser ranging measure the distance (and speed) that telescopes can't, what limits them, and how do we combine optical, radar and laser to shrink an orbit's uncertainty fastest?
**Builds on:** [[R7031R L2]] · **Leads to:** [[R7031R L4]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Radar brings its own light: it measures range from the echo delay ($\rho = c\Delta t/2$), range-rate from the Doppler shift ($f_D = -2\dot\rho/\lambda$), angles from the beam, and a radar cross-section, day or night and through cloud; but the echo falls as $R^{-4}$ (radar equation), so it rules LEO while GEO is left to telescopes and planetary radar needs huge dishes. Design choices trade against each other: waveform (pulsed vs CW), bandwidth ($\Delta R \approx c/2B$, but more noise), beamwidth ($\approx 1.2\lambda/D$, narrow = high gain but small coverage), wavelength band (long λ searches cheaply but small debris falls into the weak Rayleigh regime). Laser ranging gives mm-level range to retroreflectors (m-level to debris) but only one object at a time, under clear sky, when you already know where to point, so the three sensors complement each other: optical finds, radar maintains LEO, laser pins down, and adding range collapses the long angles-only uncertainty ellipse.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Radar:** Transmit radio waves, receive the echo. **Active**: works day/night, through cloud, sunlit or not.
- **Radar equation:** $P_r \propto 1/R^4$: the echo power falls with the fourth power of range.
- **Range $\rho$ (slant range):** From round-trip delay: $\rho = c\Delta t/2$.
- **Range-rate $\dot\rho$:** From the Doppler shift: $f_D = -2\dot\rho/\lambda$. Only the radial velocity counts.
- **Monostatic / bistatic:** Same antenna transmits and receives / transmitter and receiver apart (can transmit continuously).
- **Pulsed vs continuous wave (CW):** Pulses measure range by delay; unmodulated CW measures Doppler precisely but no range; FM-CW can measure range.
- **Bandwidth $B$:** Range of frequencies in the signal. Range resolution $\Delta R \approx c/(2B)$.
- **Radar cross-section (RCS, $\sigma$):** The equivalent area of an isotropic reflector giving the same echo. m² or dBsm.
- **Rayleigh / resonance / optical regime:** Object much smaller than λ (weak echo $\propto a^6/\lambda^4$) / about λ (oscillates) / much larger ($\sigma \approx \pi a^2$).
- **Beamwidth (HPBW):** $\theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx 1.2\lambda/D$. Narrower beam ↔ higher gain $G \approx \eta(\pi D/\lambda)^2$.
- **Monopulse / interferometry:** Ways to measure the direction more precisely than the beamwidth.
- **SLR (Satellite Laser Ranging):** Short laser pulses + retroreflectors → range to mm level.
- **ILRS:** International Laser Ranging Service: ~40 stations, data formats (CPF predictions, CRD data), bias monitoring.

### Recap ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=2|s. 2]])
Detection = how we find objects (signal above noise). Tracking = keep knowing where they are well enough. Three tools: optical ([[R7031R L2]]), **radar**, **laser ranging** (this lecture).

### Radar infrastructure ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=3|s. 3–5]])
- **Europe:** TIRA (Germany, tracking & imaging), GRAVES (France, bistatic surveillance), GESTRA (Germany, phased array), CAMRa (UK), Medicina (Italy), **EISCAT 3D** (Sweden, Norway, Finland: Skibotn/Kaiseniemi/Karesuvanto, a phased-array research radar, also useful for debris).
- **USA:** Haystack/HUSIR, **Space Fence** (Kwajalein, S-band), **Goldstone** (planetary radar), Green Bank (receiver).
- **Commercial:** LeoLabs (worldwide phased arrays, UHF and S-band).

**What radar does ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=4|s. 4]]):**
- Detects a satellite by transmitting radio waves and measuring the echo.
- **Unlike optical: measures range and range-rate directly.**
- Works day and night, through clouds, sunlit or not (**active sensing**).
- In SSA: radar observations **maintain the catalogue in LEO**.
- For NEOs: rarely a discovery tool, but **a single radar track can shrink an orbit's uncertainty more than years of optical astrometry**.

(Slide 5 is a video about Swedish radar history, IRF.)

### The radar equation ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=6|s. 6]])
$$
P_r = \frac{P_t\,G^2\,\lambda^2\,\sigma}{(4\pi)^3\,R^4\,L}
$$
$P_t$ = transmitted power, $G$ = antenna gain (used twice: transmit and receive), $\lambda$ = wavelength, $\sigma$ = radar cross-section, $R$ = range, $L$ = combined losses (cables, atmosphere, processing).

> [!tip] Where does $R^4$ come from?
> The transmitted power spreads over a sphere on the way out: $1/(4\pi R^2)$. The target reflects a little ($\sigma$), and that reflection spreads over a sphere again on the way back: another $1/(4\pi R^2)$. Two trips → $R^{-4}$. A telescope only has one $R^{-2}$ (from target to us), because the Sun does the "transmitting" and it's far away anyway.

**Consequences:**
- Radars are practical for **LEO** surveillance.
- **GEO** satellites are mostly left to telescopes: GEO (36,000 km) vs LEO (800 km) is a factor 45 in range → $45^4 = 4\times10^6$ → **66 dB** weaker echo.
- **Planetary radars** need huge dishes and hundreds of kW.

### Radar geometry ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=7|s. 7]])
![[R7031R L3 s7 monostatic vs bistatic.png]]
*(a) Monostatic: one antenna transmits and receives. (b) Bistatic: transmitter and receiver apart, with the bistatic angle $\beta$ between them; no leakage from transmitter into receiver ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]).*

- **Monostatic:** same antenna (or co-located). Must **switch** between transmitting and listening.
- **Bistatic:** transmitter and receiver apart, sometimes hundreds of km. Can **transmit continuously** and use cheap receivers. Examples: **GRAVES** (SSA); planetary defence: **Goldstone transmits, Green Bank receives** (a round trip to an NEO takes longer than any pulse, so the same dish couldn't switch fast enough anyway).
- **Multistatic:** several receivers.

### What radar measures ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=8|s. 8]])
Four observables:
1. **Range $\rho$**, from the round-trip delay.
2. **Range-rate $\dot\rho$**, from the Doppler shift.
3. **Angles** (azimuth, elevation), from the beam direction.
4. **Object signature:** RCS, its fluctuation (spin, attitude), and images (needs bandwidth). Example: radar images of the binary asteroid 2017 YE5 and an ISAR image of a satellite (Fraunhofer).

**Range ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]):**
$$
\rho = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}
$$
Divide by 2 because the signal goes there and back. A 1 µs timing error → $3\times10^8 \times 10^{-6}/2 = 150$ m range error → radar stations follow **GNSS time**.

**Range-rate ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=10|s. 10]]):** motion along the line of sight shifts the echo frequency (Doppler):
$$
f_D = -\frac{2\dot\rho}{\lambda}
$$
- Approaching target ($\dot\rho < 0$) → echo at **higher** frequency → $f_D > 0$ (by convention).
- Factor 2: the target "receives" a shifted signal and re-transmits it shifted again.
- **Only the radial component** of the velocity counts. A satellite passing perpendicular to the line of sight gives zero Doppler.
- Relativistic terms are below $10^{-9}$ for Earth orbit; only matter for precise planetary ranging.

### Waveforms ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=11|s. 11]])
**Pulsed:** short bursts at a pulse repetition frequency (PRF).
- Delay measures range directly.
- Doppler is sampled once per pulse (phase change between pulses). Fast targets can **alias**: if the target moves half a wavelength between pulses (a full $2\pi$ of phase for the round trip), it looks identical to a stationary one.
- **Unmodulated pulse:** constant frequency and phase during the pulse.
- **Compressed pulse:** a longer pulse whose frequency (a "chirp") or phase changes in a known pattern → long pulse (lots of energy) with the resolution of a short one.

**Continuous wave (CW):** transmits all the time.
- **Unmodulated CW:** one steady frequency → **very precise Doppler but no range** (nothing to time).
- **FM (or phase-modulated) CW** (e.g. repeated frequency sweeps) → can measure range.

### Bandwidth and range resolution ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=12|s. 12]])
$$
\Delta R \approx \frac{c}{2B}
$$
- Unmodulated pulse of length $\tau$ has $B \approx 1/\tau$: a 10 µs pulse → $\Delta R = c\tau/2 = 1.5$ km.
- Compressed pulse with $B = 1$ MHz → $\Delta R = 3\times10^8/(2\times10^6) = 150$ m, **even if the pulse lasts much longer than 1 µs**.
- Price: the receiver must accept a wider frequency range → **more noise**.

Resolution = how close two targets can be in range and still be seen as two (not the same as how precisely you measure one target's range, which also depends on SNR).

### SNR ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=13|s. 13]])
$$
\text{SNR}_\text{dB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_\text{signal}}{P_\text{noise}}
$$
Higher SNR → easier detection and better range and range-rate estimates. Depends on transmitted power, antenna gain, RCS, range, receiver noise and **how many echoes are integrated** (adding pulses raises SNR).

dB reminders: ×2 = +3 dB, ×10 = +10 dB, ×100 = +20 dB; $R^4$: doubling the range costs 12 dB.

### Radar cross-section ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=14|s. 14]])
- RCS = how much energy a target reflects **back to the radar**. Depends on **size, shape, material and aspect angle**.
- Defined as the area of an imagined **isotropic** scatterer that would return the same power. In m² or **dBsm** (dB relative to 1 m²): 1 m² = 0 dBsm, 0.01 m² = −20 dBsm.

For a perfectly conducting sphere of radius $a$, with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$:

| Regime | Condition | RCS |
|---|---|---|
| **Rayleigh** | $ka \ll 1$ (much smaller than λ) | $\sigma \approx 9\pi a^2(ka)^4$ → **weak echo** |
| **Resonance (Mie)** | $ka \approx 1$–10 | $\sigma$ **oscillates** with λ and aspect |
| **Optical** | $ka \gg 1$ | $\sigma \approx \pi a^2$ (the projected area) |

![[R7031R L3 s14 RCS of a sphere vs ka.png]]
*Normalised RCS $\sigma/\pi a^2$ vs $ka$ ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]): steep rise in the Rayleigh regime ($\propto (ka)^4$), oscillations in the resonance regime, flat at 1 in the optical regime.*

**Why it matters:** small debris in a long-wavelength radar is deep in the Rayleigh regime and almost invisible. That's why radars hunting cm-size debris use **short wavelengths** (S, X band).

### Beamwidth and gain ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=15|s. 15]])
- **Beamwidth:** angular span of the main lobe, where the power is concentrated. Scales as $\lambda/D$.
- **Half Power Beam Width (HPBW):** between the −3 dB (half-power) points:
$$
\theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx 1.2\frac{\lambda}{D}, \qquad G \approx \eta\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2
$$
  $\eta$ = aperture efficiency (~0.5–0.7).
- Bigger $D$ → narrower beam → higher gain.

![[R7031R L3 s15 antenna beam pattern.png]]
*Antenna pattern: the main lobe (beamwidth $\theta$ at −3 dB), nulls, and side lobes ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]).*

| Narrow beam | Wide beam |
|---|---|
| ✓ High gain, long range, fine angular resolution | ✓ Covers a broad area fast: searching, many targets |
| ✗ Must point precisely, covers little sky at once | ✗ Lower gain, coarser resolution |

Same formula as the optical diffraction limit ($1.22\lambda/D$, [[R7031R L2]]): but λ is ~$10^5$–$10^6$ times longer for radio, so radar beams are degrees wide, not arcseconds.

### Measuring angles ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=16|s. 16]])
The beam alone only says "somewhere in the illuminated area". More precise direction:
- **Monopulse:** the antenna forms overlapping beam parts and compares their echoes to see how far the target is from the beam centre (like your two ears locating a sound).
- **Interferometry:** two or more antennas receive the same echo; the **phase difference** gives the direction.

### Architectures ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=17|s. 17]])
- **Search radar:** covers a wide region, revisits regularly. **Tracking radar:** focuses on one known object for longer.

| Design | How | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Fence** | Broad beam in a fixed direction; objects crossing it are detected | Surveillance, but only a short piece of each orbit |
| **Electronically scanned array** | Steers beams electronically, very fast | Search **and** track many objects (Space Fence, GESTRA, LeoLabs) |
| **Tracking dish** | Large dish follows one target | Precise measurements, radar images (TIRA, HUSIR) |
| **Beam-park** | Points in one direction for a long time (e.g. 24 h) | **Statistics** of debris crossing the beam, including objects too small to catalogue (feeds MASTER, [[R7031R L1]]) |

### Frequency bands ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=18|s. 18]])

| Band | λ | Strengths | Weaknesses | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **VHF** | 1–10 m | Cheap power, wide beams for search | Small debris deep in Rayleigh | GRAVES, EISCAT 3D |
| **UHF** | 30–100 cm | Good search compromise | Sub-10 cm debris in Rayleigh, ionospheric delay | LeoLabs |
| **L** | 15–30 cm | Search and tracking | Moderate small-debris sensitivity | GESTRA, TIRA |
| **S** | 7.5–15 cm | 10 cm debris near the optical regime | More array elements per m² for steered arrays | Space Fence, LeoLabs |
| **X and above** | ≤ 4 cm | Fine resolution, imaging | Narrow beams: mostly tracking. **Rain!** | Goldstone, HUSIR, TIRA |

Pattern: **longer λ = cheap wide search but blind to small debris; shorter λ = sees small things and makes images but has narrow beams** (and rain absorbs it).

### Planetary radar ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=19|s. 19]])
- Measures NEO distances to **metre accuracy**.
- A **follow-up** tool: the NEO must be close enough for the weak echo; wide-field optical surveys find targets first.
- Precise orbits → better predictions of future close approaches → tell a **potential impact from a flyby**.
- Since Arecibo collapsed (2020), the **Goldstone Solar System Radar** is the only planetary radar in regular operation (often received bistatically at Green Bank).
- > 1000 NEAs observed by radar since 1968 (image: 3200 Phaethon, Arecibo, Dec 2017).

### Laser ranging ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=20|s. 20–24]])
**Satellite Laser Ranging (SLR)** is the **most precise** ranging technique:
- **mm-level** to cooperative targets with **retroreflectors**; **m-level** to uncooperative targets (debris).
- The target's position must already be known to **a few arcseconds** (the laser beam is tiny).
- **One object at a time, under a clear sky.**

![[R7031R L3 s20 satellite laser ranging.png]]
*An SLR telescope on its elevation/azimuth mount sends a laser pulse to a satellite's retroreflector array and times the return ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]).*

**A station ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]):** a pulsed laser + a telescope that points the beam and collects the return + a fast photon detector + a precise clock, all synced to GNSS time.
- Very short pulses (usually **green**, 532 nm).
- One telescope for both, or separate transmit/receive telescopes.
- A sensitive detector catches the few returning photons; clocks record departure and return; filters and **timing windows** reject sunlight and background.
- The telescope tracks the target; measurements are calibrated on a nearby target at known distance.
- Local weather (pressure, temperature, humidity) corrects the atmospheric delay.

**Range ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]):**
$$
R_\text{CoM} = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2} - \Delta R_\text{atm} - \Delta R_\text{sys} - \Delta R_\text{rel} + \Delta R_\text{CoM}
$$
- The atmosphere, the station's internal delay and relativity **lengthen** the measured time → subtract them.
- The offset from the reflectors to the satellite's **centre of mass** is added → range to the centre of mass (which is what the orbit describes).

**ILRS ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]):** the International Laser Ranging Service coordinates ~40 stations, defines data formats (**CPF**, Consolidated Prediction Format, for predictions; **CRD**, Consolidated Ranging Data, for observations), and assesses each station's biases against the network.

**Lunar laser ranging ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]):**
- Reflectors left by Apollo 11, 14, 15 and Lunokhod 1 and 2.
- Only a few photons come back per ~100,000 shots.
- mm-level accuracy. Used to test general relativity, measure the Moon's recession (~3.8 cm/yr) and probe its fluid core.

### Sensor comparison ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=25|s. 25–26]])

| Criterion | Passive telescope | Radar | Laser ranging |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong measurement | **Angles** | **Range and range-rate** | **Precise range** |
| Common role | Discovery and follow-up | LEO surveillance, close NEOs | Selected Earth-orbit follow-up |
| Illumination | Sunlight (or thermal emission) | Own transmitter | Own transmitter |
| Weather | Cloud-limited | Works through cloud | Cloud-limited |
| Main constraint | Brightness and geometry | $R^{-4}$ range penalty and resources | Acquisition and weak returns |

![[R7031R L3 s26 uncertainty ellipse angles vs range.png]]
*Angles only: the uncertainty (blue) is stretched along the line of sight from the observer. With range added: it collapses to the small orange blob ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=26|s. 26]]).*

> [!important] The big picture ([[Detection_and_tracking_radar-laser.pdf#page=26|s. 26]])
> - **Optical:** passive, "wide" and "deep": discovers new objects but measures only direction.
> - **Radar:** $R^{-4}$ limits its reach, but range and range-rate **transform orbit accuracy**.
> - **Laser:** the precision tool (mm on reflectors, m on debris), one object at a time.
>
> **SSA and planetary defence are both problems of orbit uncertainty; the sensor mix sets how fast it shrinks.**

### Exercise: a break-up in LEO ([[Detection_and_Tracking_exercise.pdf#page=2|exercise s. 2–7]])
Scenario: at 03:20 UTC a satellite breaks up at ~800 km. Available: a ground-based radar, an optical telescope network, and an SLR station (ranges to reflector-equipped satellites). Answers are in the Self-test below.

**Main takeaways ([[Detection_and_Tracking_exercise.pdf#page=8|exercise s. 8]]):**
- Seeing an object once isn't enough. It's only useful once you can **predict where it will be next**.
- Telescopes see far and cover lots of sky, but only tell you **which way** to look, not how far.
- Radar brings its own light: night, day or cloud, it tells you **how far and how fast**. Its signal fades fast with distance → ideal close to Earth.
- Lasers measure distance almost perfectly, but **only if you already know where to point**.
- Every sensor has a blind spot; combining them covers each other's gaps.
- Debris and asteroids pose the same problem: you judge risk from an orbit you don't know exactly, and **the best next observation is the one that removes the most doubt**.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Radar equation**
$$
P_r = \frac{P_tG^2\lambda^2\sigma}{(4\pi)^3R^4L}
$$
$P_t$ = transmit power, $G$ = gain, $\lambda$ = wavelength, $\sigma$ = RCS, $R$ = range, $L$ = losses.

**Range and range-rate**
$$
\rho = \frac{c\Delta t}{2}, \qquad f_D = -\frac{2\dot\rho}{\lambda}
$$

**Range resolution**
$$
\Delta R \approx \frac{c}{2B} \quad (\text{unmodulated pulse: } B \approx 1/\tau \Rightarrow \Delta R = c\tau/2)
$$

**SNR in dB**
$$
\text{SNR}_\text{dB} = 10\log_{10}(P_\text{signal}/P_\text{noise})
$$

**RCS of a conducting sphere** ($k = 2\pi/\lambda$)
$$
ka \ll 1:\ \sigma \approx 9\pi a^2(ka)^4, \qquad ka \gg 1:\ \sigma \approx \pi a^2
$$

**Beamwidth and gain**
$$
\theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx 1.2\frac{\lambda}{D}, \qquad G \approx \eta\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2
$$

**Laser range**
$$
R_\text{CoM} = \frac{c\Delta t}{2} - \Delta R_\text{atm} - \Delta R_\text{sys} - \Delta R_\text{rel} + \Delta R_\text{CoM}
$$

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** An L-band tracking radar ($\lambda = 0.23$ m, dish $D = 34$ m, efficiency $\eta = 0.6$) observes LEO objects.
1. Beamwidth and gain (in dBi)?
2. A satellite approaches with $\dot\rho = -5$ km/s. Doppler shift?
3. The echo returns 6.0 ms after transmission. Range? What range error does a 1 µs clock error give?
4. Compare the RCS of a 1 cm-radius metal sphere with its projected area. Which regime?
5. How much weaker is the echo from the same object at GEO (36,000 km) than at 800 km?
6. The radar uses a compressed pulse with $B = 1$ MHz. Range resolution?

**Solution:**
1. $\theta_{3\text{dB}} = 1.2 \times 0.23/34 = 8.1\times10^{-3}$ rad $= 0.47°$. $G = 0.6 \times (\pi \times 34/0.23)^2 = 0.6 \times 464^2 = 1.29\times10^5$ → $10\log_{10}(1.29\times10^5) = 51$ dBi.
2. $f_D = -2(-5000)/0.23 = +43.5$ kHz. Positive: approaching, as the convention says.
3. $\rho = 3\times10^8 \times 6.0\times10^{-3}/2 = 900$ km. Clock error: $3\times10^8\times10^{-6}/2 = 150$ m.
4. $k = 2\pi/0.23 = 27.3$ m⁻¹, $ka = 27.3 \times 0.01 = 0.27 \ll 1$ → **Rayleigh**. $\sigma = 9\pi(0.01)^2(0.27)^4 = 9\pi \times 10^{-4}\times 5.6\times10^{-3} = 1.6\times10^{-5}$ m². Projected area $\pi a^2 = 3.1\times10^{-4}$ m². The RCS is **~20× smaller** than the area: −48 dBsm. That's why cm debris is hard for L-band and S/X-band radars are used for it.
5. $(36{,}000/800)^4 = 45^4 = 4.1\times10^6$ → $40\log_{10}45 = 66$ dB weaker. → GEO is for telescopes.
6. $\Delta R = 3\times10^8/(2\times10^6) = 150$ m.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: (Exercise Q1) Daylight, cloudy. Which sensor do you task first to look for fragments, and what does it tell you?

> [!example]- Answer
> **The radar**: the only sensor that works in daylight and through cloud. It gives range, range-rate and approximate angles of returns near the parent's orbit → how many fragments, roughly where, how fast they spread, and (from RCS) their rough size. Telescopes need a dark sky and clear weather; the laser needs both and a known target.

- Q: (Exercise Q2) The radar sees several returns near the predicted pass. How do you turn detections into individual tracks?

> [!example]- Answer
> Associate: check each return against the catalogue (propagated parent and known objects, within an uncertainty gate). Link the unmatched ones over consecutive passes/revisits by consistent range and range-rate → tracklets. Fit preliminary orbits per fragment, then refine with more passes (least squares/filter) → state + covariance → add as new catalogue objects (UCTs become tracks).

- Q: (Exercise Q3) Next night, the telescopes record streaks: angles over time, no distance. What can you infer, what's ambiguous?

> [!example]- Answer
> From each streak: the direction and angular rate (an attributable), the plane/direction of motion, and with accurate timing a precise along-track timing; brightness hints at size. Ambiguous: the **range and range-rate**, so the orbit is poorly constrained along the line of sight (long thin uncertainty), and which streak is which fragment if several are close. Linking with the radar tracks (or observing from another site) resolves it.

- Q: (Exercise Q4) Could the laser station discover and track the new fragments?

> [!example]- Answer
> **Not discover**: it needs the position to within a few arcseconds before it can point, sees one object at a time, and needs a clear sky. Fragments have no retroreflectors, so a conventional station (designed for reflectors) gets no usable return; only high-power debris laser ranging could give m-level ranges, and only after radar/optical have provided good predictions. It can, however, range to the reflector-equipped *intact* satellites near the cloud.

- Q: (Exercise Q5) One more observing window. What do you task and which uncertainty do you reduce?

> [!example]- Answer
> The measurement that removes the largest uncertainty for the objects that matter most (e.g. fragments with close approaches to active satellites). Angles-only orbits are weakest in range, so add **range**: task the radar (any weather) on the fragments with the largest along-line-of-sight/along-track uncertainty; or if the weakness is a short arc, observe again to extend the arc. "The best next observation is the one that removes the most doubt."

- Q: Why does a radar echo fall as $R^{-4}$ but reflected sunlight only as $R^{-2}$?

> [!example]- Answer
> Radar: the signal spreads on the way out ($1/R^2$) and again on the way back ($1/R^2$). Sunlight: the Sun is (effectively) at a fixed distance, so only the trip from the object to the observer depends on $R$.

- Q: Unmodulated CW radar: what can it measure and what can't it?

> [!example]- Answer
> Very precise Doppler (range-rate), but not range: a constant signal has no timing marks, so you can't time the round trip. Modulate it (FM-CW sweeps) to get range.

- Q: Why can a single planetary radar track shrink an NEO's orbit uncertainty more than years of optical data?

> [!example]- Answer
> Optical gives only angles, leaving range (and range-rate) poorly known. Radar measures range to metres and range-rate precisely, which is exactly the missing direction of the uncertainty ellipsoid, so it collapses it.
