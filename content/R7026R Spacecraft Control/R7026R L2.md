**Question:** How do we write any system as $\dot x = f(x, u)$, find its equilibria, decide if they are stable without solving the equations (Lyapunov), and turn it into a linear model $\dot x = Ax + Bu$?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L1]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L3]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
A state-space model $\dot x = f(x,u)$, $y = g(x,u)$ collects everything the system "remembers" in the state $x$; any $n$-th order ODE becomes $n$ first-order ones, and the linear case is $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx + Du$. Stability belongs to each equilibrium: Lyapunov's method proves it without solving anything by finding an "energy" $V$ that is positive and only decreases (for detumbling, the kinetic energy; for $\dot x = Ax$, solve $PA + A^TP = -Q$). Linearizing with Jacobians at an equilibrium gives $A, B, C, D$, valid only nearby, and the poles of $G(s) = C(sI-A)^{-1}B + D$ are the eigenvalues of $A$, so asymptotically stable ⇔ all $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i(A) < 0$.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **State $x$:** The smallest set of numbers that, together with future inputs, predicts the whole future. The "memory" of the system.
- **State-space model:** $\dot x = f(x,u)$, $y = g(x,u)$. Linear case $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx + Du$. Every model in the rest of the course has this form.
- **$A, B, C, D$:** system matrix (how the state evolves by itself), input matrix (how inputs push the state), output matrix (what the sensors see), direct feed-through (usually 0).
- **Equilibrium:** A state where the system stays forever if it starts there: $f(x_e, u_e) = 0$.
- **Stable / asymptotically stable / unstable:** stays close / stays close *and* returns / runs away. A property of each equilibrium, not of the whole nonlinear system.
- **BIBO stability:** Bounded input ⇒ bounded output.
- **Lyapunov function $V$:** An "energy" that is positive everywhere except at the equilibrium and never increases along trajectories. Proves stability without solving the dynamics.
- **Positive definite (d.p.) matrix $P$:** $x^TPx > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$ → a "bowl". Check with Sylvester (leading minors $> 0$) or eigenvalues $> 0$.
- **Lyapunov equation $PA + A^TP = -Q$:** Turns the search for $V$ into solving a linear matrix equation.
- **Linearization:** Replace $f$ by its tangent (Jacobians) at an equilibrium → $A, B, C, D$. Only valid nearby.
- **Matrix exponential $e^{At}$:** The solution of $\dot x = Ax$: $x(t) = e^{At}x(0)$.
- **Poles = eigenvalues of $A$:** So $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i(A) < 0$ for all $i$ ⇔ asymptotically stable. (This is what "a stable matrix" means.)

### From L1 to this lecture ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3]])
L1 described systems from the outside: input → $G(s)$ → output. Today we open the box and describe what happens *inside* with a **state**. By the end of the lecture every model in the course is of the form $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, valid near an equilibrium.

### What is a state? ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–5]])
A **state-space model** describes a system with a finite set of inputs $u$, outputs $y$ and state variables $x$, related by **first-order** differential equations.

The key property of the state: **the current state contains all the information needed to predict the future**, given the future inputs.
- The future output depends only on the current state and the future input.
- The past inputs matter only through the current state.
- → The state is the "memory" of the system.

Spacecraft example ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4]]): input $u$ = wheel torques, state $x$ = attitude, rates and wheel speeds, output $y$ = star tracker and gyro readings.

> [!tip] Plain words
> If you know where the spacecraft points and how fast it turns *right now*, you don't need its history to know what it will do next. Those numbers are the state.

### The general model ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7–8]])
Continuous time, $m$ inputs, $p$ outputs, $n$ states:
$$
\dot x = f(x, u), \qquad y = g(x, u)
$$
- $f$ = **state transition map**: the "velocity" of the state, tells how it moves.
- $g$ = **output map**: what we can measure.

Discrete time: $x(k+1) = f(x(k), u(k))$, $y(k) = g(x(k), u(k))$. How to go from continuous to discrete is L5. We usually start in continuous time since physics gives differential equations.

**Is any $f$ ok? ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]])** No: for every start $x_0$ and input $u$ a solution must **exist and be unique**, at least for a short time. Enough: $f$ continuously differentiable. Example $\dot x = -x^2$, $x_0 = -1$ gives $x(t) = \frac{1}{t - 1}$, which blows up at $t = 1$: a valid model with a **finite escape time** (linear systems never do this).

**Can every system be written like this? ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]])** No. Exceptions: systems described by PDEs (non-lumped, e.g. a flexible beam with infinitely many points) and continuous-time systems with delays.

### Higher order → first order ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]])
Any $n$-th order ODE becomes $n$ first-order ODEs: **use $y$ and its first $n-1$ derivatives as the state.**
$$
\dddot y = \phi(\ddot y, \dot y, y, u), \qquad
x = \begin{bmatrix} \ddot y \\ \dot y \\ y \end{bmatrix}
\;\Rightarrow\;
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} \dddot y \\ \ddot y \\ \dot y \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} \phi(x, u) \\ [1\ 0\ 0]\,x \\ [0\ 1\ 0]\,x \end{bmatrix} = f(x, u)
$$
The trick: the derivative of each state is either the next state up, or the equation itself at the top.

### Why we love state space ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12]])
1. **First order:** any order and any number of axes fit $\dot x = f(x,u)$.
2. **The present predicts the future:** $x(t_0)$ and $u(t)$ give the whole trajectory.
3. **Easy to simulate:** $x(t+h) \approx x(t) + h\,f(x,u)$, exactly what `ode45` and Simulink do.
4. **Easy to solve when linear:** $x(t) = e^{At}x_0 + \int_0^t e^{A(t-\tau)}Bu(\tau)\,d\tau$.
5. **Geometry:** trajectories show stability without solving (Lyapunov, today).
6. **Inside the box:** shows modes a transfer function hides (L3), and feedback of the whole state can place every pole (L4).

*The state is a point in the plane ($\theta$, $\dot\theta$), $f$ is the arrow that tells where it moves next ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]).*

### Linear state space: $A, B, C, D$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–14]])
$$
\dot x = Ax + Bu, \qquad y = Cx + Du
$$

| Symbol | Size | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x$ | $n \times 1$ | state | the memory |
| $u$ | $m \times 1$ | input | what we control |
| $y$ | $p \times 1$ | output | what we measure |
| $A$ | $n \times n$ | system matrix | how the state evolves by itself |
| $B$ | $n \times m$ | input matrix | how the input pushes the state |
| $C$ | $p \times n$ | output matrix | which combination of states the sensors see |
| $D$ | $p \times m$ | direct transmission | input straight to output (spacecraft: $D = 0$) |

In a block diagram the **integrators hold the state**: $\dot x$ goes in, $x$ comes out.

**Example: one spacecraft axis ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]).** Rigid body, inertia $I$, torque $T$, angle $\theta$ measured by a star tracker. Newton–Euler: $I\ddot\theta = T$.
$$
x = \begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ \dot\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad u = T, \quad y = \theta
\;\Rightarrow\;
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I \end{bmatrix} u, \quad
y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} x
$$
Read row by row: "$\dot\theta$ is the second state" and "$\ddot\theta = T/I$". The state is what we must know now to predict the future: where the axis points and how fast it turns.

### Equilibria ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17–20]])
With a constant input $u = \bar u$, an **equilibrium** is a state where the system stays forever:
$$
\dot x = f(x_e, \bar u) = 0 \qquad \text{(discrete: } x(k+1) = f(x_e, \bar u) = x_e)
$$
Nonlinear systems can have **several** equilibria, or none.

**Pendulum with motor ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]):** $\ddot\theta + \sin\theta + u = 0$. Equilibrium: $\ddot\theta = 0 \Rightarrow \sin\theta_e = -u$. The motor can hold the arm at an angle, but only for $|u| \le 1$ (since $|\sin| \le 1$), and then there are several solutions.

**Question 3 ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]):** $\dot x = -\tan x + \sqrt{u}$ → $\tan x_e = \sqrt{u_e}$ → $x_e = \arctan\sqrt{u_e}$ (plus multiples of $\pi$: again more than one).

**Gravity-gradient pitch ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19–20]]).** A spacecraft in a circular orbit with orbital rate $\omega_o$. Gravity pulls a little harder on the end closer to Earth, which gives a torque that tries to align the long axis with the vertical:
$$
I_y\ddot\theta = -\tfrac{3}{2}\omega_o^2 (I_x - I_z)\sin 2\theta + T
$$
With $x = [\theta,\ \dot\theta]^T$, $u = T$ and $\sigma_y = (I_x - I_z)/I_y$:
$$
\dot x = f(x,u) = \begin{bmatrix} \dot\theta \\ -\tfrac32\omega_o^2\sigma_y\sin 2\theta + u/I_y \end{bmatrix}
$$
![[R7026R L2 s19 gravity gradient pitch.png]]
*Pitch $\theta$ turns the long body in the orbit plane, away from the local vertical (nadir) ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]).*

Equilibria for $T = 0$ (Question 4): $\dot\theta_e = 0$ and $\sin 2\theta_e = 0$ ⇒ $\theta_e \in \{0, \frac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}\}$. **Four isolated equilibria**: the body axis along the local vertical or along the flight path. Are they equally good? No, see below.

### Different kinds of stability ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21–23]])
![[R7026R L2 s21 pendulum phase portrait.png]]
*Pendulum with friction, $\ddot\theta = -c\dot\theta - g\sin\theta$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]). Near the bottom trajectories spiral in; near the top they all leave.*

![[R7026R L2 s22 kinds of stability.png]]

For an equilibrium (with constant input):
1. **Stable:** no friction, the ball rolls to and fro but stays close.
2. **Unstable:** any push and it rolls away.
3. **Asymptotically stable:** with friction it comes to rest at the bottom.

For a whole system: **BIBO stable** (and ISS, not in the course).

**BIBO ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]):** bounded input ⇒ bounded output: $\|u\| \le \gamma_u \Rightarrow \|y\| \le \gamma_y$.
Question 6: $\dot x = -x^2u$, $y = x$ is **not** BIBO: the bounded input $u \equiv -1$ gives $\dot x = x^2$, which escapes to infinity in finite time from any $x_0 > 0$.

### Lyapunov stability: definitions ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24–25]])
![[R7026R L2 s24 lyapunov epsilon delta.png]]
An orbit (trajectory) $x^*(t)$ is:
- **stable** if for each $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ so that $|x^*(0) - x(0)| < \delta \Rightarrow |x^*(t) - x(t)| < \varepsilon$ for all $t$.
- **unstable** if not stable.
- **asymptotically stable** if stable *and* there is a $\delta$ so that starting within $\delta$ gives $|x^*(t) - x(t)| \to 0$.

> [!tip] Plain words: the ε–δ game
> Someone gives you a tube of radius $\varepsilon$ around the reference trajectory ("never leave this tube"). If you can always answer with a starting distance $\delta$ that keeps you inside the tube forever, it's **stable**. If you also end up exactly on the reference, it's **asymptotically stable**.

For an equilibrium $x_0$ (an equilibrium is just an orbit that doesn't move, $x(t) \equiv x_0$):
- **locally stable / unstable / locally asymptotically stable**: as above.
- **globally asymptotically stable**: *every* trajectory converges to $x_0$, from any start.

### Lyapunov's method: the idea ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]])
![[R7026R L2 s26 energy bowl.png]]
1. Consider $\dot x = f(x)$.
2. Do **not** try to solve the dynamics. Instead find an "energy function" $V(x)$.
3. Check how $\dot V$ (the change of energy over time) behaves.

- If the energy **decreases** along every trajectory → the system settles at the bottom: **asymptotically stable**.
- If the energy **never increases** → it can't climb away: **stable**.

Pendulum with friction: mechanical energy is dissipated by friction, so it ends at the bottom.

### Definite functions ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27–29]])
$V(x)$ defined near 0:
- **positive definite (d.p.):** $V(0) = 0$ and $V(x) > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$ nearby → a **bowl** with its bottom at the origin.
- **positive semi-definite (s.d.p.):** $V(x) \ge 0$ → a bowl that may have a **flat valley**.
- **negative (semi-)definite (d.n., s.d.n.):** $-V$ is d.p. (s.d.p.).

![[R7026R L2 s27 definite functions.png]]
*Left: $x_1^2 + x_2^2$ is d.p. (zero only at the origin). Right: $x_1^2$ is only s.d.p. (zero along the whole $x_2$ axis, a flat valley) ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27]]).*

**Quadratic forms ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28]])**, the most important example:
$$
V(x) = x^TPx = \sum_i\sum_j x_i P_{ij} x_j, \qquad P = P^T
$$
$V$ is d.p. ⇔ $P$ is d.p. How to check:

> [!important] Sylvester's criterion
> $P$ is d.p. ⇔ all **leading principal minors** are positive:
> $$p_{11} > 0, \quad \det\begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} \\ p_{21} & p_{22}\end{bmatrix} > 0, \quad \det\begin{bmatrix} p_{11} & p_{12} & p_{13} \\ p_{21} & p_{22} & p_{23} \\ p_{31} & p_{32} & p_{33}\end{bmatrix} > 0, \ \dots$$
> Equivalently: all eigenvalues of $P$ are positive.
> $P$ is d.n. ⇔ $-P$ is d.p. ⇔ the minors **alternate** in sign: $< 0$, $> 0$, $< 0$, …

For a $2\times2$ matrix this is the rule of thumb $p_{11} > 0$ and $p_{11}p_{22} - p_{12}^2 > 0$ (i.e. $p_{11}p_{22} > p_{12}^2$). This comes back in L4–L5 (LQR weights).

**Question 7 ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]):** is the kinetic energy $V = \frac12\omega^TJ\omega$ d.p. for
$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 15 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 20 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{kg m}^2 \;?
$$
As a function of $\omega$: **yes**. $P = \frac12 J$ and the minors of $J$ are $10 > 0$, $10\cdot15 - 2\cdot2 = 146 > 0$, $\det J = 20 \cdot 146 = 2920 > 0$.
As a function of the whole state (attitude + $\omega$): only **s.d.p.**, because $V = 0$ for $\omega = 0$ at *any* attitude. The energy sees the spin, not the pointing → it can prove **detumbling**, not pointing.

### $\dot V$ along trajectories and Lyapunov's theorem ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30–31]])
By the chain rule:
$$
\dot V(x) = \frac{dV(x(t))}{dt} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} = \sum_i \frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i} f_i(x)
$$
**Important:** we only need $f(x)$, not the solution $x(t)$, to compute $\dot V$.

> [!important] Lyapunov's stability theorem
> If $0$ is an equilibrium of $\dot x = f(x)$, and $V$ is $C^1$ and **d.p.** near 0, then:
> - $\dot V$ **d.n.** ⇒ 0 is **asymptotically stable**
> - $\dot V$ **s.d.n.** ⇒ 0 is (simply) **stable**
>
> $V$ is then called a **Lyapunov function**. If moreover it holds in all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $V \to \infty$ as $\|x\| \to \infty$, the result is **global**.

![[R7026R L2 s31 level curves.png]]
*$\dot V < 0$: every trajectory crosses the level curves $V = c$ inwards, so it must end at the center ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]]).*

The conditions are only **sufficient**: if one $V$ fails, it proves nothing. Try another one.

### The linear case and the Lyapunov equation ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32–34]])
For $\dot x = Ax$ and $V = x^TPx$:
$$
\dot V = \dot x^TPx + x^TP\dot x = (Ax)^TPx + x^TP(Ax) = x^T(PA + A^TP)\,x
$$
So: **$P$ d.p. and $PA + A^TP$ d.n. ⇒ asymptotically stable.**

**Example ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]):** $\ddot y = -3\dot y - 2y$ ⇒ $A = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
- Try 1, $P = I$: $A + A^T = \begin{bmatrix} -6 & -1 \\ -1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$. First minor $-6 < 0$ ok, but $\det = 0 - 1 = -1 < 0$ → **not d.n., no conclusion**.
- Try 2, $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}$ (d.p.: $1 > 0$, $5 - 1 > 0$):
$$
PA = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^TP = (PA)^T = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad PA + A^TP = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}
$$
d.n. ⇒ asymptotically stable (the eigenvalues $-1$, $-2$ agree).

**Instead of guessing $P$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]):** fix a d.p. $Q$ and solve the **Lyapunov equation**
$$
PA + A^TP = -Q
$$

> [!important] Theorem
> All eigenvalues of $A$ in the open left half plane ⇔ for any d.p. $Q$ the Lyapunov equation has a unique solution $P$, and $P$ is d.p.

Algorithm: (1) pick $Q$ (e.g. $I$), (2) solve, in MATLAB `P = lyap(A', Q)` (note the transpose: `lyap(A,Q)` solves $AX + XA^T + Q = 0$), (3) check if $P$ is d.p. In the example above, $Q = 4I$ gives exactly $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}$.

### Nonlinear example: detumbling a spacecraft ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35–36]])
Euler's equations with a rate-damping torque $T = -K_d\omega$, $K_d > 0$:
$$
J\dot\omega = -\omega \times J\omega + T
$$
Nonlinear because of the gyroscopic term $\omega \times J\omega$. Lyapunov function: kinetic energy $V = \frac12\omega^TJ\omega$ (d.p. since $J$ is):
$$
\dot V = \omega^TJ\dot\omega = -\underbrace{\omega^T(\omega \times J\omega)}_{=0} - K_d\,\omega^T\omega = -K_d\|\omega\|^2
$$
The gyroscopic term vanishes because $\omega \times J\omega$ is perpendicular to $\omega$ (a cross product is perpendicular to both its factors).
- $K_d > 0$: $\dot V$ d.n. ⇒ $\omega = 0$ is **globally asymptotically stable**: detumbling works from **any** spin.
- $K_d = 0$: $\dot V = 0$ ⇒ only stable: energy is conserved.

We never solved Euler's equations. The gyroscopic term moves momentum between the axes but does no work.

![[R7026R L2 s36 detumbling simulation.png]]
*$J = \mathrm{diag}(10, 15, 20)$, $\omega(0) = (0.2, -0.15, 0.1)$ rad/s, $K_d = 0.5$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]). $V$ only falls; $\omega_z$ first grows and $\omega_y$ changes sign (gyroscopic coupling), but no energy is added.*

### Linearization ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37–43]])
![[R7026R L2 s37 linearization of sine.png]]
*The gravity-gradient torque $-\sin 2\theta$ is a sine. Near $\theta = 0$ the straight line $-2\theta$ is a good copy; at 45° it is not ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]]).*

**Why linearize? ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]])**
1. Near the equilibrium the linear model is **right about stability** (Lyapunov's indirect method): all $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i(A) < 0$ ⇒ locally asymptotically stable; one $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i(A) > 0$ ⇒ unstable. Eigenvalues instead of searching for $V$. **But eigenvalues on the imaginary axis decide nothing** (e.g. gravity-gradient pitch at $\theta_e = 0$).
2. Control keeps deviations small (arcsecond pointing errors, a chaser a few km away), exactly where the linear model is accurate ($\sin 2\theta \approx 2\theta$ within 1 % for $|\theta| < 7°$).
3. Superposition: responses add and scale → $e^{At}$, transfer functions, frequency response.
4. Insight in numbers: modes and frequencies from the eigenvalues.
5. Systematic design with guarantees (pole placement, LQR, Kalman). Nonlinear design has no general recipe.

**Where? ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]])** Around the point where we want the system to stay: an equilibrium (the operating point).

**Procedure ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40–42]]).** Write $x = x_{eq} + \Delta x$, $u = u_{eq} + \Delta u$ and Taylor expand to first order (L1):
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(x_{eq} + \Delta x) = \Delta\dot x \approx \underbrace{f(x_{eq}, u_{eq})}_{=0} + \nabla_x f\big|_{eq}\,\Delta x + \nabla_u f\big|_{eq}\,\Delta u
$$
Same for the output. Result:
$$
\Delta\dot x = A\,\Delta x + B\,\Delta u, \qquad \Delta y = C\,\Delta x + D\,\Delta u
$$
$$
A = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big|_{eq}, \quad B = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\Big|_{eq}, \quad C = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\Big|_{eq}, \quad D = \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\Big|_{eq}
$$
$A, B, C, D$ are **Jacobians evaluated at the equilibrium**. A different equilibrium gives a different linear model. The model is in **deviations** $\Delta x$ from the equilibrium, not in $x$ itself.

**Discrete time ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43]]):** the same formulas hold. The equilibrium condition $f(x_{eq}, u_{eq}) = x_{eq}$ cancels on both sides.

### Gravity-gradient pitch, linearized ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44–46]])
Near $\theta_e$: $\sin 2\theta \approx \sin 2\theta_e + 2\cos 2\theta_e\,\Delta\theta$, so
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y\cos 2\theta_e & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I_y \end{bmatrix}
$$

| | $\theta_e = 0$ (long axis along the vertical) | $\theta_e = \pi/2$ (long axis along the flight path) |
|---|---|---|
| Linear model | $\Delta\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y\Delta\theta = T/I_y$ | $\Delta\ddot\theta - 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y\Delta\theta = T/I_y$ |
| Eigenvalues ($\sigma_y > 0$) | $\pm j\omega_\theta$ | $\pm\omega_\theta$ (one in RHP) |
| Behaviour | oscillation (**libration**), never damped | **saddle**, unstable |

with $\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$. So if $I_x > I_z$ you fly at $\theta_e = 0$ (Question 11).

Numbers ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=45|s. 45]]): 800 km, $\omega_o = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s, $I = (6, 8, 4)$ kg m² ⇒ $\sigma_y = (6-4)/8 = 0.25$, libration period 117 min.

![[R7026R L2 s46 pitch phase portrait.png]]
*The whole nonlinear picture ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=46|s. 46]]): closed curves around 0° and ±180° = libration (as the linear model at $\theta_e = 0$), crosses at ±90° = saddles (as at $\theta_e = \pi/2$). Outside the red curve the spacecraft tumbles, which no linear model shows. A pitch rate above $0.87\,\omega_o$ at $\theta = 0$ is enough to tumble.*

Each linear model describes one neighbourhood; the nonlinear model connects them.

### Relative motion in orbit: Clohessy–Wiltshire ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=47|s. 47–51]])
Rendezvous and formation flying: where is the **chaser**, seen from the **target**?

![[R7026R L2 s47 hill frame.png]]
*The Hill frame rides with the target and turns once per orbit: $x$ radial (up), $y$ along the flight direction, $z$ out of the orbit plane ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=47|s. 47]]).*

Target on a circular orbit, radius $r_0$, orbital rate $n = \sqrt{\mu/r_0^3}$, $\mu = 398\,600$ km³/s². Chaser offset $\rho = [x, y, z]^T$.

**Step 1, Newton in the inertial frame ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=48|s. 48]]):** target $\ddot r_0 = g_t$, chaser $\ddot r_0 + \ddot\rho_N = g_c + u$. Subtract: $\ddot\rho_N = g_c - g_t + u$. Only the **gravity difference** and the thrust accelerate the chaser relative to the target.

**Step 2, seen from the turning frame ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=49|s. 49]]):** with $\frac{da}{dt}\big|_N = \frac{da}{dt}\big|_{Hill} + \omega \times a$ (L8):
$$
\ddot\rho = (g_c - g_t) - \underbrace{2\omega \times \dot\rho}_{\text{Coriolis}} - \underbrace{\omega \times (\omega \times \rho)}_{\text{centrifugal}} + u
$$
On a merry-go-round a ball rolled straight seems to curve (Coriolis) and drift outward (centrifugal).

**Step 3, linearize around $\rho = 0$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=50|s. 50]]):** Coriolis and centrifugal are already linear; only gravity is nonlinear. Dropping squares of small terms gives $g_c - g_t \approx n^2[2x, -y, -z]^T$. Adding up:

| | gravity | centrifugal | Coriolis | **Clohessy–Wiltshire** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x$ (radial) | $2n^2x$ | $n^2x$ | $2n\dot y$ | $\ddot x = 3n^2x + 2n\dot y + u_x$ |
| $y$ (along-track) | $-n^2y$ | $n^2y$ | $-2n\dot x$ | $\ddot y = -2n\dot x + u_y$ |
| $z$ (out of plane) | $-n^2z$ | — | — | $\ddot z = -n^2z + u_z$ |

Intuition: radially, lower = stronger gravity, so it falls further ($3n^2x$); along-track there is no "spring", it can stay anywhere on the target's orbit; out of plane the two orbit planes cross twice per orbit (pure oscillation).

**State-space form, in-plane ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=51|s. 51]]):**
$$
x = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \dot x \\ \dot y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 3n^2 & 0 & 0 & 2n \\ 0 & 0 & -2n & 0 \end{bmatrix} x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} u_x \\ u_y \end{bmatrix}
$$
Eigenvalues: $\pm jn$ (motion that repeats once per orbit) and a double 0 (a constant along-track offset stays where it is, plus a drift). 400 km: $n = 1.131\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s, period 92.6 min.

Question 12: can a radial-only thruster steer the chaser anywhere? Can a radial-only radar tell where it is? → L3 answers both with one rank test each.

### Linear vs nonlinear ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=52|s. 52]])

| | Linear $\dot x = Ax$ | Nonlinear $\dot x = f(x)$ |
|---|---|---|
| Solutions | exist for all $t$, at most exponential growth | may escape to infinity in finite time ($\dot x = x^2$) |
| Equilibria | the origin, or a whole subspace | may be isolated (pendulum; pitch: four) |
| Oscillations | families of neighbouring periodic orbits (CW: $\pm jn$) | isolated limit cycles (Van der Pol) |
| Stability | a property of the **system**: eigenvalues of $A$ | a property of **each equilibrium** ($\theta_e = 0$ vs $\pi/2$) |
| Initial conditions | small sensitivity | possibly very high sensitivity (chaos) |

A linearization is only valid near the equilibrium where it was computed.

### From a linear ODE to state space ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54]])
Mass–spring–damper ($m = 1$, $c = 2$, $k = 3$, $F = 6u$), e.g. the first bending mode of a solar array:
$$
\ddot y + 2\dot y + 3y = 6u
$$
Choose $x_1 = y$, $x_2 = \dot y$: $\dot x_1 = x_2$, $\dot x_2 = -3x_1 - 2x_2 + 6u$:
$$
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3 & -2 \end{bmatrix}x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}u, \qquad y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}x
$$
Question 13: for $\ddot y + 2\dot y + 3y = 5\dot u + 6u$ this choice **fails**: $\dot x_2$ would contain $\dot u$. Derivatives of the input need a cleverer state → the control canonical form (L3).

### Dynamic response: from state space to $G(s)$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=57|s. 57–60]])
Laplace transform with $x(0) = 0$:
$$
sX = AX + BU \;\Rightarrow\; (sI - A)X = BU \;\Rightarrow\; X = (sI - A)^{-1}BU
$$
$$
\boxed{\,G(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D\,}
$$
**Poles are eigenvalues of $A$ ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=58|s. 58]]):**
$$
G(s) = C\,\frac{\operatorname{adj}(sI - A)}{\det(sI - A)}\,B + D
$$
The denominator is $\det(sI - A)$, so the poles are the roots of $\det(sI - A) = 0$, which is exactly the eigenvalue equation of $A$. (Unless a zero cancels a pole; L3 tells when.)

Example, one spacecraft axis:
$$
(sI - A)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \\ 0 & s \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac1s & \frac1{s^2} \\ 0 & \frac1s \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
G(s) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \frac1s & \frac1{s^2} \\ 0 & \frac1s \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac1I \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{Is^2}
$$
A double integrator: double pole at $s = 0$, the same as in L1.

**Solution in the time domain ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=59|s. 59]]):** keeping $x(0)$, $X = (sI - A)^{-1}x(0) + (sI - A)^{-1}BU$, and back in time:
$$
x(t) = \underbrace{e^{At}x(0)}_{\text{free response}} + \underbrace{\int_0^t e^{A(t-\tau)}Bu(\tau)\,d\tau}_{\text{forced response}}
$$
with the **matrix exponential** (MATLAB `expm(A*t)`):
$$
e^{At} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{(sI - A)^{-1}\} = I + At + A^2\frac{t^2}{2!} + A^3\frac{t^3}{3!} + \dots
$$
Same series as $e^{at}$ for a number, just with matrices. Used again in L5 (observer error decays as $e^{(A-LC)t}$, and sampling this solution gives the exact discrete model).

**Example ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=60|s. 60]]):** one axis, constant torque $T$. $A^2 = 0$, so the series stops: $e^{At} = I + At = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Result:
$$
\theta(t) = \theta(0) + \dot\theta(0)\,t + \frac{T}{2I}t^2, \qquad \dot\theta(t) = \dot\theta(0) + \frac{T}{I}t
$$
The same as integrating $I\ddot\theta = T$ twice by hand, but $e^{At}$ does it for any $A$.

### Stability summary ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=61|s. 61–62]])
- In general: BIBO stable ≠ asymptotically stable ≠ simply stable.
- For LTI: BIBO stable = asymptotically stable ≠ simply stable (the "=" only if there are no hidden modes, L3).
- For $\dot x = Ax$: **asymptotically stable ⇔ all eigenvalues of $A$ have $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i < 0$.**
  - Gravity-gradient pitch at $\theta_e = 0$: $\lambda = \pm j\omega_\theta$ ⇒ simply stable, not asymptotically.
  - One spacecraft axis: $\lambda = 0, 0$ in a Jordan block ⇒ **unstable** ($\theta$ drifts at constant rate).

![[R7026R L2 s62 three kinds of stability.png]]
*Three spacecraft ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=62|s. 62]]). Left: one axis, no control, small initial rate, $\theta$ grows without bound. Middle: gravity-gradient pitch from 5°, swings forever. Right: the same with a damper ($\zeta = 0.1$), returns to $\theta_e$.*

> [!important] Stable is not enough for pointing
> We want **asymptotic** stability, and that needs **damping**, passive or active.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**State-space model**
$$
\dot x = f(x,u),\ y = g(x,u) \qquad\qquad \dot x = Ax + Bu,\ y = Cx + Du
$$
where $x$ = state ($n$), $u$ = input ($m$), $y$ = output ($p$).

**Equilibrium**
$$
f(x_e, u_e) = 0
$$
**Linearization** (Jacobians at the equilibrium, model in deviations $\Delta x = x - x_e$)
$$
A = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big|_{eq},\quad B = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\Big|_{eq},\quad C = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\Big|_{eq},\quad D = \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\Big|_{eq}
$$
**Lyapunov**
$$
\dot V = \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}f(x), \qquad V \text{ d.p.},\ \dot V \text{ d.n.} \Rightarrow \text{asympt. stable}
$$
$$
V = x^TPx:\quad \dot V = x^T(PA + A^TP)x, \qquad PA + A^TP = -Q
$$
where $P, Q$ symmetric; $Q$ d.p. chosen, $P$ solved for; $P$ d.p. ⇔ $A$ stable.

**Sylvester** ($P$ d.p.): all leading principal minors $> 0$. For $2\times2$: $p_{11} > 0$, $p_{11}p_{22} - p_{12}^2 > 0$.

**Transfer function and solution**
$$
G(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D, \qquad \text{poles} = \text{eig}(A)
$$
$$
x(t) = e^{At}x(0) + \int_0^t e^{A(t-\tau)}Bu(\tau)\,d\tau, \qquad e^{At} = I + At + A^2\frac{t^2}{2!} + \dots
$$
**Spacecraft models**
$$
\text{one axis: } A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix},\ B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I \end{bmatrix}; \qquad
\text{GG pitch: } \omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y},\ \sigma_y = \frac{I_x - I_z}{I_y}
$$

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44–45]]) Linearize the gravity-gradient pitch model $I_y\ddot\theta = -\frac32\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\sin 2\theta$ ($T = 0$) at $\theta_e = 0$ and $\theta_e = \pi/2$. Which equilibrium is stable? Compute the libration period for 800 km, $\omega_o = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s, $I = (6, 8, 4)$ kg m².

**Solution:**
1. *State form:* $x = [\theta, \dot\theta]^T$, $f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} \dot\theta \\ -\frac32\omega_o^2\sigma_y\sin 2\theta \end{bmatrix}$, $\sigma_y = \frac{I_x - I_z}{I_y} = \frac{6 - 4}{8} = 0.25$.
2. *Jacobian:* $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(-\frac32\omega_o^2\sigma_y\sin 2\theta\right) = -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y\cos 2\theta$, so
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y\cos 2\theta_e & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
3. *At $\theta_e = 0$:* $\cos 0 = 1$, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. $\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y = 0$ ⇒ $\lambda = \pm j\omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$. On the imaginary axis: **libration**, simply stable (and the linearization alone can't decide, but the nonlinear phase portrait shows closed curves).
4. *At $\theta_e = \pi/2$:* $\cos\pi = -1$, $\lambda^2 - 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y = 0$ ⇒ $\lambda = \pm\omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$. One positive real eigenvalue ⇒ **unstable** (saddle).
5. *Libration period:*
$$
\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3 \cdot 0.25} = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}\cdot 0.866 = 8.99\cdot10^{-4}\ \text{rad/s}, \qquad
T_\theta = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_\theta} = 6990\ \text{s} \approx 117\ \text{min}
$$
6. *Conclusion:* fly with the long axis along the local vertical ($\theta_e = 0$). But it librates forever, so a damper is needed for asymptotic stability ([[Lecture_2_handout.pdf#page=62|s. 62]]).

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Write $\ddot y + 4\dot y + 5y = 2u$ in state-space form. What are the poles?

> [!example]- Answer
> $x = [y, \dot y]^T$: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -5 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [1\ 0]$, $D = 0$. $\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^2 + 4\lambda + 5 = 0$ ⇒ $\lambda = -2 \pm j$. Asymptotically stable.

- Q: Is $P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ positive definite?

> [!example]- Answer
> No. $p_{11} = 2 > 0$, but $\det P = 8 - 9 = -1 < 0$. (Its eigenvalues have opposite signs: a saddle, not a bowl.)

- Q: Why does the kinetic energy prove detumbling but not pointing?

> [!example]- Answer
> $V = \frac12\omega^TJ\omega$ is d.p. in $\omega$ but only s.d.p. in the full state (attitude + rate): it is zero for $\omega = 0$ at any attitude. So $V \to 0$ means the spin stops, but says nothing about where the spacecraft ends up pointing.

- Q: The linearization of a system at an equilibrium has eigenvalues $\pm 2j$. What can you conclude about the nonlinear system?

> [!example]- Answer
> Nothing. Eigenvalues on the imaginary axis decide nothing (Lyapunov's indirect method only works if all $\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0$ or some $\operatorname{Re}\lambda > 0$). You need a Lyapunov function or the nonlinear phase portrait.

- Q: What are the poles of $\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}x$, and why is it unstable and not just "simply stable"?

> [!example]- Answer
> Double eigenvalue at 0 in a Jordan block ($A \neq 0$). $e^{At} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ grows linearly in $t$: a small initial rate makes $\theta$ drift without bound.
