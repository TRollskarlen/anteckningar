The goal of "reglerteknik" is to be able to predict and correct system behaviour without manual intervention. To do this we need to be able to describe the actions of the system mathematically. This always comes in the form of differential equations. 

While they are complicated as they are, having to deal with multiple at the same time doesn't make things easier. To counter this issue we use the Laplace transformation.  

Problem: Physics gives us differential solution. 
$$
\ddot{y} + 2\dot{y} +3y = 6u
$$
Solution: Laplace transformation. Derivatives in the time domain becomes multiplication is the Laplace domain (s-domain).
$$
\dot{y} \rightarrow sY(s),\quad \ddot{y} \rightarrow s^2Y(s)
$$
Ex: Lecture 1 (s.29)
$$ 
\ddot y + a_1\dot y + a_2 y = b_0\ddot u + b_1\dot u + b_2 u
$$
Using Laplace on every term gives us: 

$$
s^2Y + a_{1}sY + a_{2}Y = b_{0}s^2U + b_{1}sU + b_{2}U
$$
Factor out Y and U from their respective side and the divide Y with U and it will give you what is called the transfer function. 
$$
G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{b_{0}s^2 + b_{1}s + b_{2}}{s^2 + a_{1}s + a_{2}}
$$
The transfer function is the "instructions" to how the input becomes the output. Best of all is when you connect several blocks in series, instead of having to solve new differential equations, you now only need to multiply the transfer functions in the Laplace plane making it so much fucking easier. The course assumes the systems are LTI (Linear Time-Invariant). Linear = double the input = double the output. Time invariant = the system will behave the same today as tomorrow. Knowing the transfer function allows you to calculate the input or output of a system, given that you now the other. 

Now, while I know the transfer function, how will that help me tell how the system will behave? 

The answer to that is the poles and zero inside $G(s)$. First we take a look what is known as the "poles" of a transfer function. The poles are values of $s$ which makes the denominator of $G(s)$ go to zero. 
$$
G(s) = \frac{1}{s+2}
$$
This transfer function has a pole $s = 2$. If the order of the equation in the denominator would be higher, lets say a second order. There would be 2 values (poles) which cause the denominator to become zero and so on. 

Finding the poles: denominator = zero and solve for $s$.

### Why $\frac{1}{s-p} \leftrightarrow e^{pt}$

**Method 1: Back to the differential equation (intuition)**

Which system has the transfer function $G(s) = \frac{1}{s-p}$? Work backwards:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} &= \frac{1}{s - p} \\
(s - p)\,Y(s) &= U(s) \\
sY(s) - p\,Y(s) &= U(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Back to the time domain ($sY \to \dot y$):

$$
\dot y - p\,y = u \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \dot y = p\,y + u
$$

With no input ($u = 0$), the rate of change is proportional to the amount itself:

$$
\dot y = p\,y \quad\Rightarrow\quad y(t) = y(0)\,e^{pt}
$$

The pole $p$ is the "rate" in $\dot y = p\,y$:
- $p < 0$: decays, e.g. $p = -2 \Rightarrow e^{-2t}$
- $p > 0$: grows, e.g. $p = 2 \Rightarrow e^{2t}$ (unstable)

**Method 2: Direct Laplace transform (proof, L1 s. 25)**

Definition:

$$
\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt
$$

Insert $f(t) = e^{pt}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}\{e^{pt}\} &= \int_0^\infty e^{pt}\,e^{-st}\,dt \\
&= \int_0^\infty e^{(p - s)t}\,dt \\
&= \left[\frac{e^{(p-s)t}}{p - s}\right]_0^\infty \\
&= 0 - \frac{1}{p - s} \\
&= \frac{1}{s - p}
\end{aligned}
$$

At $t \to \infty$ the term vanishes since $\operatorname{Re}(s) > \operatorname{Re}(p)$; at $t = 0$, $e^0 = 1$.

$$
\boxed{\;\mathcal{L}\{e^{pt}\} = \frac{1}{s - p}\;}
$$

**Summary:** the pole is the exponent. A system with a pole at $p$ responds to a kick as $e^{pt}$.

|                    | s-domain                            | time domain              |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| System             | $G(s) = \frac{1}{s-p}$              | $\dot y = p\,y + u$      |
| Response to a kick |                                     | $e^{pt}$                 |
| Pole               | where the denominator is 0: $s = p$ | the rate in the exponent |
The above proof above is important due to it explaining why the denominator either explodes to infinity or approaches zero over time in the time domain. 
$$
G(s) = \frac{1}{s-p}, \quad Laplace\ domain. \\
g(t) = e^{pt}, \quad Time \ domain
$$
When $p$ becomes either negative or positive the time domain will either approach zero or grow to infinity. IMPORTANT NOTE: The Laplace Domain DOES NOT!!! tell you how the system will behave. It is merely a tool to tell you how the system will behave in the TIME DOMAIN. $s = p$ does not mean that something grows in reality. The time domain and laplace/s - domain are two separate worlds. 

Nyquist - diagram and Bode-diagram will not appear in this course. The entire repitition can be found in Franklin chapter 1-6. 

The most important part is to have respect for the poles. Almost everything from Lyapunov and pole-placement for the observer is about where the poles are. 

*Ex: Transfer function and Laplace*

$$
I \dot{\theta} = T \Rightarrow Is^2 \Theta(s) = T(s) \Rightarrow G(s) = \frac{1}{Is^2}
$$
The main advantage is that differential equations become regular algebra in the s-domain.
