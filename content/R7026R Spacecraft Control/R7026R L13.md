**Question:** How are reaction wheels, CMGs and momentum-bias wheels used to control a three-axis-stabilized spacecraft: which control laws for small and large maneuvers, how is wheel momentum dumped, and how can a momentum bias control yaw without a yaw sensor?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L8]], [[R7026R L9]], [[R7026R L10]], [[R7026R L4]], [[R7026R L11]], [[R7026R L12]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R E1]], [[R7026R E2]], [[R7026R L14]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Small maneuvers use PD on each Euler-angle error ($K = I\omega_n^2$, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI$), and large maneuvers use the quaternion error law $T_{ci} = 2K_iq_{iE}q_{4E} - K_{id}\omega_i$, which turns about the Euler axis with no singularity. Wheels only exchange momentum, so a constant disturbance must be dumped by an external torque (magnetorquers: $\mathbf M = K_w(\Delta\mathbf h_w\times\mathbf B)/B^2$, effective only on the share $\lambda_y$), four wheels with a pseudo-inverse give redundancy, and CMGs give far more torque but need steering away from singular gimbal sets. A momentum-bias wheel along the orbit normal gives roll–yaw stiffness and makes yaw observable from roll, so roll-only control $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$ (with $a < 0$) can hold yaw: gains from the steady-state errors and from matching the polynomial to two damped modes.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Torque sources:** magnetorquers (1–10 mN m), thrusters (large, fuel), solar pressure (µN m), momentum exchange (wheels 0.001–1 N m, CMGs hundreds). Only the first three change total $\mathbf h$.
- **Reaction wheel with current loop:** delivers the commanded torque within milliseconds; limited torque, speed, friction near zero speed (run with a speed bias).
- **Euler-angle PD law:** $T_c = K\theta_E - K_dp$ per axis; fine for small angles.
- **DCM error law / quaternion error law:** drive $[A_E] = [A_T][A_S]^T$ to 1; the quaternion version $2q_{iE}q_{4E} = e_i\sin\alpha$ turns about the Euler axis.
- **Feed forward:** add $K_d\dot\theta_{com}$ (and $I\ddot\theta_{com}$) so ramps and parabolas are followed without error.
- **Momentum dumping:** external torque $-K_wh_w$ keeps $h_{w,ss} = T_d/K_w$.
- **Magnetic unloading:** $\mathbf M = \frac{K_w}{B^2}\Delta\mathbf h_w\times\mathbf B$ removes only the part normal to $\mathbf B$; on average the share $\lambda_y = \langle1 - \hat b_y^2\rangle$, which depends on inclination (0 at the equator).
- **Pseudo-inverse:** $T_w = [A_w]^T([A_w][A_w]^T)^{-1}T$, minimum-norm wheel torques for 4 wheels on 3 axes.
- **CMG:** a constant-speed rotor on a gimbal; $\mathbf T = -\dot\delta(\hat g\times\mathbf h)$. Torque amplification, but the steering matrix $A(\delta)$ can lose rank (singularities).
- **Momentum bias:** one wheel along the orbit normal; roll–yaw gyroscopic stiffness, nutation at $h/\sqrt{I_xI_z}$, errors $T_d/(\omega_oh)$.
- **Roll-only yaw control:** $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$; works because a yaw error becomes a roll error a quarter of an orbit later.

### From L8–L12 to this lecture ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3]])
You have the error quaternion (L8), the linearized equations with wheels (L9), wheels as actuators and the attitude filter (L10), LQR and pole placement on one axis (L4, L11), dual spin (L12). New: attitude maneuver laws from small to large angles, reaction wheels (model, loop, dumping, four wheels), CMGs (amplification, steering, singularities), momentum bias (one wheel, three axes, no yaw sensor). **This is how most three-axis spacecraft are controlled today.**

### Producing control torques ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–5]])
| means | typical torque | changes total $h$? | drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| magnetorquers | 1–10 mN m | yes | weak; Earth's field only |
| thrusters | almost unbounded | yes | pulses, fuel |
| solar pressure | tens of µN m | yes | too weak |
| momentum exchange | 0.001–1 N m (wheels) | **no** | momentum builds up |

Reaction wheels: smooth, continuous, accurate torque → precise pointing and moderate slews. CMGs: hundreds of N m, heavy, complex → stations and agile satellites. **A wheel only moves momentum between itself and the body.**

![[R7026R L13 s5 reaction wheel torque.png]]
*The motor spins the flywheel up ($+\dot h_w$), the spacecraft turns the other way ($-\dot h_w$); total momentum unchanged ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]).*

A bare motor responds to voltage with a time constant (not a clean torque); a **current loop** makes the wheel deliver $-\dot h_w \approx T_c$ within ms. Limits: max torque, max speed, **friction near zero speed**. Question 1: friction means the wheel doesn't move until the command exceeds $T_{fric}$, leaving an error $T_{fric}/K$ → wheels are run with a speed bias.

### Small maneuvers: Euler-angle errors ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]])
Small angles + diagonal inertia: three separate double integrators $T_c = I\ddot\theta$:
$$
T_{cx} = K_x\phi_E - K_{xd}p, \qquad T_{cy} = K_y\theta_E - K_{yd}q, \qquad T_{cz} = K_z\psi_E - K_{zd}r
$$
$\phi_E = \phi_{com} - \phi$ etc. Rates $(p, q, r)$: $\boldsymbol\omega_{BI}$ from gyros for an inertial target, $\boldsymbol\omega_{BR}$ (L8) for Earth pointing.

**Gains (Question 2):** $Is^2 + K_ds + K = I(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_ns + \omega_n^2)$ ⇒ $K = I\omega_n^2$, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI$ (L4 pole placement). Example: $I_x = 1000$, $\omega_n = 0.1$, $\zeta = 0.7$ → $K_x = 10$, $K_{xd} = 140$: 0.17 N m for a 1° error.

### Large maneuvers ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7–11]])
Three things go wrong with large angles:
1. $T = I\ddot\theta$ no longer holds: products of inertia, gyroscopic and orbital terms matter.
2. Actuator torque and speed limits are reached → nonlinear (time-optimal slews: E1).
3. Euler-angle kinematics become singular (gimbal lock at $\theta = 90°$).

![[R7026R L13 s7 large slew between targets.png]]
*An Earth-observation satellite slews its camera from target A to target B as fast as its wheels allow ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]).*

Question 3: the DCM or the quaternion avoids (3); the quaternion needs the fewest operations.

**DCM error law ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]):** $[A_E] = [A_T][A_S]^T$ (= 1 at target). Drive the off-diagonal elements to zero:
$$
T_{cx} = K_xa_{23E} - K_{xd}p, \qquad T_{cy} = -K_ya_{13E} - K_{yd}q, \qquad T_{cz} = K_za_{12E} - K_{zd}r
$$
![[R7026R L13 s8 spacecraft and target axes.png]]
*Spacecraft axes S and target axes T: each $a_{ijE}$ is a target axis dotted with a spacecraft axis (Sidi Fig. 7.2.1, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]).*

- Small errors: $[A_E] \approx 1 - [\boldsymbol\alpha_E\times]$ ⇒ $a_{23E} \approx \phi_E$, $-a_{13E} \approx \theta_E$, $a_{12E} \approx \psi_E$: the Euler law again.
- Using half differences, $\frac12(a_{23E} - a_{32E}) = e_x\sin\alpha$, turns the spacecraft about the **Euler axis** of $[A_E]$: the shortest rotation, $\cos\alpha = \frac12(\operatorname{tr}[A_E] - 1)$.
- Price: six error elements from an integrated DCM, every cycle.

**Quaternion error law ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]):** $q_E$ from L8's product with $q' = q_T$, $q = q_S^*$:
$$
T_{cx} = 2K_xq_{1E}q_{4E} - K_{xd}p, \qquad T_{cy} = 2K_yq_{2E}q_{4E} - K_{yd}q, \qquad T_{cz} = 2K_zq_{3E}q_{4E} - K_{zd}r
$$
Question 4: $q_{iE} = e_i\sin\frac\alpha2$, $q_{4E} = \cos\frac\alpha2$ ⇒ $2q_{iE}q_{4E} = e_i\sin\alpha$: **the torque points along the Euler axis of the error**. Small errors: $2q_{iE} \approx$ the Euler-angle error → same gains as one axis. No trig, no singularity. Integrate all four components and renormalize ($q_4 = \sqrt{1 - |\mathbf q|^2}$ would lose the sign of $q_4$).

**Simulation ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]]):** $I = \operatorname{diag}(1000, 500, 700)$, both laws $\omega_n = 1$, $\zeta = 1$, no torque limits (idealized). Plotted: error angle $\alpha$ about the Euler axis.
![[R7026R L13 s10 euler vs quaternion slew.png]]
*(40°, −40°, −60°): quaternion ∫α dt = 156 deg s, Euler 196: the Euler law takes a longer path. (60°, 80°, 120°), near gimbal lock: the Euler law first turns the wrong way (α grows from 105 to 135°), 860 vs 281 ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]]). Small commands: identical.*

**Rates without gyros ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):** invert L8's kinematics: $\dot q_S = \frac12[Q]\boldsymbol\omega_{BR}$, $[Q]^T[Q] = 1$ ⇒ $\boldsymbol\omega_{BR} = 2[Q]^T\dot q_S$, with
$$
[Q] = \begin{bmatrix} q_4 & -q_3 & q_2 \\ q_3 & q_4 & -q_1 \\ -q_2 & q_1 & q_4 \\ -q_1 & -q_2 & -q_3 \end{bmatrix}
$$
![[R7026R L13 s11 rates from star tracker.png]]
Differentiating noisy attitude amplifies noise → low-pass filter. Question 5: better, an **observer** (L5): it uses the model and the known torque, so it predicts the rate instead of differentiating noise; the Kalman filter (L7, L10) is the optimal one.

### The basic attitude loop and feed forward ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13]])
One axis, PD on a wheel torque:
$$
\frac{e(s)}{\theta_{com}(s)} = \frac{s(s + K_d/I)}{s^2 + (K_ds + K)/I}
$$
- Step: zero steady-state error (final value theorem: $s\cdot\frac1s\cdot e(s)/\theta_{com} \to 0$).
- Ramp $\dot\theta_{com}$: error $\dot\theta_{com}K_d/K$.
- **Feed forward** $K_1\dot\theta_{com}$ with $K_1 = K_d$: zero ramp error; add $K_2\ddot\theta_{com}$ with $K_2 = I$: zero parabola error too.

![[R7026R L13 s13 one-axis loop with feed forward.png]]
*The one-axis loop with the two feed-forward paths (Sidi Fig. 7.3.3, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]).*

This is L4's reference-input design: the command is known, so feed it forward instead of waiting for an error. No extra noise enters.

### Momentum accumulation and dumping ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]])
Question 6: holding the attitude against a constant $T_d$ needs $T_c = -T_d$, so $\dot h_w = -T_c = T_d$: **the momentum goes into the wheel** until it saturates (L11: 0.061 N m s per orbit).

**Dumping:** measure $h_w$ and apply an **external** torque $-K_wh_w$ (magnetorquers or thrusters): $I\ddot\theta = T_d - \dot h_w - K_wh_w$ ⇒ in steady state
$$
h_{w,ss} = \frac{T_d}{K_w}
$$
![[R7026R L13 s14 momentum dumping loop.png]]
*The wheel torque on the body is $-\dot h_w$; the dumping path $K_w$ (dashed) acts from outside (after Sidi Fig. 7.3.4, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]).*

Applying $K_wh_w$ **through the wheel itself** would not work: it just moves the momentum back into the body. Every wheel-controlled spacecraft needs a second, external actuator.

### Magnetic unloading ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15–17]])
Want $\mathbf T = -K_w\Delta\mathbf h_w$ against the excess $\Delta\mathbf h_w = \mathbf h_w - \mathbf h_N$. But $\mathbf M\times\mathbf B$ is never along $\mathbf B$, so it can't be solved exactly. Choose $\mathbf M$ normal to $\mathbf B$:
$$
\mathbf M = \frac{K_w}{B^2}(\Delta\mathbf h_w\times\mathbf B) \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B = -K_w\Delta\mathbf h_{w\perp}
$$
(Check: $(\Delta\mathbf h\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B = (\Delta\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf B)\mathbf B - B^2\Delta\mathbf h = -B^2\Delta\mathbf h_\perp$.)
![[R7026R L13 s15 magnetic unloading vectors.png]]
*The torque opposes only the part of $\Delta\mathbf h_w$ normal to $\mathbf B$; $\mathbf M$ is normal to both ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]).*

- Needs a 3-axis magnetometer and the wheel speeds.
- Momentum along $\mathbf B$ stays, but $\mathbf B$ turns over an orbit → in an inclined orbit every direction is unloaded **on average**. In an equatorial orbit $\mathbf B$ stays along $Y_B$: **pitch-wheel momentum can never be removed magnetically**.

**Choosing $K_w$ ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]):** averaged over an orbit, $\Delta\dot{\mathbf h}_w \approx -K_w\langle1 - \hat b\hat b^T\rangle\Delta\mathbf h_w + \mathbf T_d$. For $Y_B$ momentum the gain shrinks to $\lambda_yK_w$, $\lambda_y = \langle1 - \hat b_y^2\rangle$:
$$
\Delta h_{w,ss} \approx \frac{T_d}{\lambda_yK_w}
$$
| inclination | 0° | 20° | 40° | 60° | 98° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\lambda_y$ | 0 | 0.24 | 0.61 | 0.86 | 0.99 |

![[R7026R L13 s16 unloading share vs inclination.png]]
*The share $1 - \hat b_y^2$ of a $Y_B$ momentum that the field can remove, along one orbit at 400 km; dashed: the orbit average $\lambda_y$ ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]).*

Recipe: choose $K_w$ from the allowed momentum, check the dipole $|\mathbf M| \le K_w|\Delta\mathbf h_w|/B$, simulate the periodic loop.

**Example ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]):** $I = (1000, 500, 700)$, 400 km, $i = 40°$, $T_{dy} = 1$ mN m, magnetorquers 100 A m², $\Delta h \le 3$ N m s.
- No unloading: $10^{-3}\cdot5554\ \text{s} = 5.55$ N m s per orbit → limit in half an orbit.
- $K_w \ge T_d/(\lambda_y\Delta h_{max}) = 10^{-3}/(0.61\cdot3) = 5.5\cdot10^{-4}$; take $K_w = 10^{-3}$ s⁻¹ → predicted 1.65 N m s.
- Simulated: 1.8 average, 1.9 max; dipole ≤ 51 A m². Adding $T_{dx}$: all wheels within 2.2 N m s.

![[R7026R L13 s17 magnetic unloading simulation.png]]

### Four wheels for three axes ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18–19]])
Three wheels: one failure ends the mission. A fourth, skewed wheel adds redundancy. Wheel axes as columns of $[A_w]$ (3×4): $\mathbf T = [A_w]\mathbf T_w$, 4 unknowns, 3 equations → **minimum-norm (minimum-power) solution**:
$$
\mathbf T_w = [A_w]^T\left([A_w][A_w]^T\right)^{-1}\mathbf T \qquad (\texttt{pinv(Aw)*T})
$$
![[R7026R L13 s18 four wheel pyramid.png]]
*Four wheels in a pyramid, each spin axis tilted by $\beta$ from $Z_B$ (Sidi Fig. 7.3.5, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]).*

Example: $\beta = 60°$, $\mathbf T = (10, -20, 5)$ mN m → $\mathbf T_w = (8.3, -9.1, -3.3, 14.0)$ mN m. Any one wheel can fail: the other three still span 3D (rank 3).

**Kepler ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]):** four wheels; one failed 2012 (still rank 3), a second in 2013. Question 7: **controllability** (L3): with no stored momentum the axes don't couple, and the axis without wheel torque is a free double integrator: rank$[B\ AB] = 4 < 6$. Rescue (K2, 2014–18): balance solar pressure about the free axis and trim with short thruster firings. (With a momentum bias one torque can reach two axes: below.)

### Control moment gyros ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20–23]])
A rotor at **constant** speed ($h_0$) on a motorized gimbal; the gimbal turns $\mathbf h$ without changing its size:
$$
\mathbf T = -\dot{\mathbf h} = -\dot\delta(\hat g\times\mathbf h), \qquad |\mathbf T| = h_0\dot\delta
$$
![[R7026R L13 s20 control moment gyro.png]]
*The gimbal turns $\mathbf h$; the body feels $-\dot\delta\,\hat g\times\mathbf h$ ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]).*

**Torque amplification:** a 10 N m s rotor gimballed at 1 rad/s → 10 N m, 50× a 0.2 N m wheel. Used on the ISS and agile imagers (Pléiades, WorldView). Question 8: the torque direction turns with $\mathbf h$ → one CMG can't hold a direction → **clusters**.

**Cluster and steering ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]):** four single-gimbal CMGs on a pyramid (gimbals 54.7° from $Z_B$). $\dot{\mathbf h} = A(\delta)\dot\delta$, column $i$ = $\hat g_i\times\mathbf h_i$. Steering = the pseudo-inverse:
$$
\dot\delta = A^T(AA^T)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf h}_{cmd}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf h}_{cmd} = -\mathbf T_c - \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h
$$
![[R7026R L13 s21 CMG pyramid cluster.png]]
*Seen along $Z_B$ with all $\delta_i = 0$: the four momenta cancel, $\mathbf h = 0$ ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]).*

Difference from wheels: **$A$ changes as the gimbals move**, is recomputed every cycle, and **its rank can drop**.

**Singularities (Question 9, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]):** at $\delta = (90°, 0, -90°, 0)$ all four columns lie in the $Y_B$–$Z_B$ plane: rank $A = 2$, **no roll torque**. CMGs 1 and 3 already point their momentum as far along $-X_B$ as possible.
![[R7026R L13 s22 CMG singular set.png]]
- Same rank test as Kepler, but the loss **depends on $\delta$**: moving the gimbals can restore it.
- 4 gimbals, 3 axes → one **null motion** (changes no torque); steering uses it to avoid singular sets.
- **Singularity-robust inverse** $A^T(AA^T + \lambda I)^{-1}$: accept a small torque error to pass through.

**Agile imager rolls 30° ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]):** $I = \operatorname{diag}(1000, 500, 700)$, quaternion law rate-limited to 60 % of actuator momentum.
![[R7026R L13 s23 wheels vs CMG roll slew.png]]
*Wheels (0.2 N m each): 79 s, torque-limited (bang-bang would need 78 s). CMGs from $\delta = 0$: 41 s, 21 s of it stuck at the singular set. CMGs from (45°, −45°, 45°, −45°): 33 s, up to 10 N m, momentum-limited at 1.1°/s ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]).*

**Wheels are torque-limited, CMGs momentum-limited**: more than twice as fast, if the steering keeps away from singularities.

### Momentum-biased stabilization ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24–28]])
Close relative of dual spin: comsats, weather satellites, Earth imagers. A high-momentum wheel gives gyroscopic stiffness; the platform stays Earth-pointing with little control effort.
![[R7026R L13 s24 momentum wheel in orbit frame.png]]
*Orbit frame: $Z_R$ toward the Earth, $Y_R$ opposite the orbit normal; the momentum wheel (MW) spins about the orbit normal (Sidi Fig. 8.2.1, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]).*

- One wheel on $Y_B$ at large constant speed: $h_{wy} = -h$ ($h > 0$), i.e. $\mathbf h$ along the orbit normal (since $Y_B$ points opposite).
- Roll–yaw get gyroscopic stiffness, like a dual-spin rotor without a spinning platform.
- **Pitch** is controlled by changing the wheel speed (the one-axis loop above).
- Used by most GEO comsats.

![[R7026R L13 s26 momentum bias roll yaw.png]]
*The bias wheel $\mathbf h$ along the orbit normal; $\phi$, $\psi$ the roll and yaw errors ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]]). Seen along the velocity, a roll tilts the Earth in the Earth sensor's view, but a yaw about the nadir line does not ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]).*

Question 10: the Earth sensor gives roll and pitch, not yaw. Yaw is controlled **through the bias**, which turns a yaw error into a roll error a quarter orbit later.

**Roll–yaw dynamics ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]]):** L9's equations with the bias, small inertia terms dropped against $h$:
$$
I_x\ddot\phi + \omega_oh\phi + h\dot\psi = T_x, \qquad I_z\ddot\psi - h\dot\phi + \omega_oh\psi = T_z
$$
$$
\Delta(s) \approx (s^2 + \omega_o^2)\left(s^2 + \frac{h^2}{I_xI_z}\right)
$$
Two undamped modes: the **orbital rate** $\omega_o$ and the **nutation rate** $\omega_{nut} = h/\sqrt{I_xI_z}$. Average errors under constant disturbances:
$$
\phi_{av} = \frac{T_{dx}}{\omega_oh}, \qquad \psi_{av} = \frac{T_{dz}}{\omega_oh}
$$
A larger bias → smaller errors.

**Example (Question 11, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27]]):** GEO, $\omega_o = 7.29\cdot10^{-5}$ rad/s, $I_x = 800$, $I_z = 1000$, $h = 10$ N m s, $T_{dx} = 10^{-5}$ N m: $\omega_{nut} = 10/\sqrt{8\cdot10^5} = 0.0112$ rad/s (562 s period); average roll error 0.79°, largest 1.57°.
![[R7026R L13 s27 passive bias roll error.png]]
A comsat allows ~0.05°: **15–30× too large**, and both modes undamped; a disturbance at $\omega_o$ (solar pressure has one) would grow without bound. **Active control is mandatory.**

### Active control with a yaw measurement ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28–30]])
PD on both axes (star tracker or Sun sensor for yaw):
$$
T_{cx} = -(k_x\phi + k_{xd}\dot\phi), \qquad T_{cz} = -(k_z\psi + k_{zd}\dot\psi)
$$
Insert, Laplace ($\kappa_x = k_x + \omega_oh$, $\kappa_z = k_z + \omega_oh$):
$$
\begin{bmatrix} I_xs^2 + k_{xd}s + \kappa_x & hs \\ -hs & I_zs^2 + k_{zd}s + \kappa_z \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \phi \\ \psi \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} T_{dx} \\ T_{dz} \end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
\Delta(s) = I_xI_zs^4 + (I_zk_{xd} + I_xk_{zd})s^3 + (k_{xd}k_{zd} + h^2 + I_z\kappa_x + I_x\kappa_z)s^2 + (k_{xd}\kappa_z + k_{zd}\kappa_x)s + \kappa_x\kappa_z
$$
- $hs$: gyroscopic roll–yaw coupling. $\omega_oh$: the orbit frame turns $\mathbf h$ at $\omega_o$, so a tilt meets a gyroscopic torque: a **spring, restoring only for $\mathbf h$ along the orbit normal** (sign of the bias matters).
- **Steady state:** $\phi_{ss} = T_{dx}/\kappa_x$, $\psi_{ss} = T_{dz}/\kappa_z$ ⇒ $k_x = \frac{T_{dx}}{\phi_{ss}} - \omega_oh$, $k_z = \frac{T_{dz}}{\psi_{ss}} - \omega_oh$.

**Matching two damped modes ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30]]):** target $(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_1s + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_2s + \omega_2^2)$ with $\Sigma = \omega_1 + \omega_2$, $\Pi = \omega_1\omega_2$:
$$
= s^4 + 2\zeta\Sigma s^3 + \left(\Sigma^2 - (2 - 4\zeta^2)\Pi\right)s^2 + 2\zeta\Sigma\Pi s + \Pi^2
$$
Divide $\Delta$ by $I_xI_z$ → $s^4 + c_3s^3 + c_2s^2 + c_1s + c_0$ and match from the bottom:
1. $s^0$: $\Pi = \sqrt{c_0}$ (fixed by $k_x, k_z$).
2. $s^1 \div s^3$: $c_1 = \Pi c_3$ → $k_{zd}/k_{xd} = (I_z\Pi - \kappa_z)/(\kappa_x - I_x\Pi)$.
3. $s^3$: $\Sigma = c_3/(2\zeta)$.
4. $s^2$: $c_2 = \Sigma^2 - (2 - 4\zeta^2)\Pi$ → one equation for $k_{xd}$.

![[R7026R L13 s30 matching two damped modes.png]]
*The control moves the undamped orbital and nutation poles onto the $\zeta = 0.7$ lines (not to scale: $\omega_1/\omega_2 \approx 270$) ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30]]).*

Example ($h = 20$, $\zeta = 0.7$, disturbances 5·10⁻⁶ N m, 0.05° roll, 0.2° yaw): $k_x = 0.00427$, $k_z = -2.6\cdot10^{-5}$ (≈ 0: the bias alone almost meets the yaw requirement), $\Pi = 3.20\cdot10^{-6}$, $k_{xd} = 22.9$, $k_{zd} = 12.8$, $\omega_{1,2} = 0.0295$ and $1.09\cdot10^{-4}$ rad/s.

> [!tip] Why not LQR?
> LQR gives a full gain matrix (every torque uses every state). Here the hardware fixes the structure (each torque uses its own angle and rate): two free numbers, found in closed form by matching.

### Active control without a yaw measurement ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31–35]])
Only roll measured; tie the yaw torque to the roll torque:
$$
T_{cx} = -(k_x\phi + k_{xd}\dot\phi), \qquad T_{cz} = a\,T_{cx}
$$
**Why it works:** the wheel keeps $\mathbf h$ fixed in inertial space while the orbit frame turns. A yaw error at point 1 is the same tilt of $\mathbf h$ seen as a **roll error a quarter orbit later** at point 2, where it's measured and corrected.
![[R7026R L13 s31 yaw becomes roll.png]]
*At point 1 the spacecraft has a yaw error $\psi$; a quarter of an orbit later the same tilt of $\mathbf h$ is a roll error $\phi$ (Sidi Fig. 8.3.1, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]]).*

Question 12: saves the yaw sensor; costs slow yaw correction (a quarter orbit = 6 h at GEO).

**Sizing ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32]]):** the yaw requirement sets the bias:
$$
|\psi_{ss}| \le \frac{|T_{dz}| + |a||T_{dx}|}{\omega_oh}
$$
Example: GEO, $I_x = 800$, $I_z = 1000$, disturbances ≤ 5·10⁻⁶ N m, 0.05° roll, 0.4° yaw. Assume $|a| < 1$: $h \ge 2T_d/(\omega_o\psi_{ss}) = 19.6$ → $h = 20$ N m s; $k_x = 0.00427$, $k_{xd} = 42.8$, $a = -0.888$.
![[R7026R L13 s32 momentum bias loop.png]]
*The loop: Earth-sensor roll and pitch with noise (ESN), PD gains, low-pass filters, and $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$ (Sidi Fig. 8.3.2, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32]]).*

**The sign of $a$ ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]):** with $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$:
$$
\Delta(s) = I_xI_zs^4 + I_zk_{xd}s^3 + \left(h^2 + \omega_oh(I_x + I_z) + I_zk_x - ahk_{xd}\right)s^2 + h(\omega_ok_{xd} - ak_x)s + \omega_oh\kappa_x
$$
Step 2 gives $a = -k_{xd}\frac{I_z\Pi - \omega_oh}{hk_x}$; since $I_z\Pi = 0.0032 > \omega_oh = 0.0015$, **$a < 0$**. The $s^1$ coefficient $h(\omega_ok_{xd} - ak_x)$ damps the slow mode: with $a = +0.888$ it's $h(0.0031 - 0.0038) < 0$ → unstable.
![[R7026R L13 s33 sign of a.png]]
*Constant 5·10⁻⁶ N m on both axes: with $a = -0.888$, roll 0.05°, yaw 0.33°; with $a = +0.888$, divergence within minutes ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]).*

**Why can roll reveal yaw? (Question 13, [[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]])** `rank(obsv(A, [1 0 0 0])) = 4`: the bias couples $\dot\psi$ into the roll equation, so yaw leaves a trace in roll (L9's principle, now with $h$ instead of $\omega_o$). The law $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$ uses it implicitly; explicitly: an observer/Kalman filter estimating $\psi$ from $\phi$, then state feedback or LQG.
![[R7026R L13 s34 yaw observer loop.png]]

**Sensor noise ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35]]):** no rate sensor → $\dot\phi$ from differentiating Earth-sensor roll (~0.03° rms) → low-pass filters at 0.2 rad/s (~10× nutation). Lowering $k_{xd}$ reduces noise but also nutation damping: 42.8 → 0.428 drops $\zeta$ from 0.52 to 0.004; at 0.214 unstable. **Damping costs noise, filtering costs damping** (L6–L7 again).

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**PD per axis:** $K = I\omega_n^2$, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI$; ramp error $\dot\theta_{com}K_d/K$, removed by feed-forward $K_d\dot\theta_{com} + I\ddot\theta_{com}$.

**Quaternion law:** $T_{ci} = 2K_iq_{iE}q_{4E} - K_{id}\omega_i$, $2q_{iE}q_{4E} = e_i\sin\alpha$.

**Rates from attitude:** $\boldsymbol\omega_{BR} = 2[Q]^T\dot q_S$.

**Dumping:** $h_{w,ss} = T_d/K_w$; magnetic: $\mathbf M = \frac{K_w}{B^2}\Delta\mathbf h_w\times\mathbf B$, $\Delta h_{w,ss} \approx \frac{T_d}{\lambda_yK_w}$.

**Four wheels / CMG steering:** $\mathbf T_w = A^T(AA^T)^{-1}\mathbf T$; CMG $\mathbf T = -\dot\delta\,\hat g\times\mathbf h$, $|\mathbf T| = h_0\dot\delta$.

**Momentum bias**
$$
\omega_{nut} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{I_xI_z}}, \qquad \phi_{av} = \frac{T_{dx}}{\omega_oh}, \qquad \psi_{av} = \frac{T_{dz}}{\omega_oh}
$$
**With yaw sensor:** $\kappa = k + \omega_oh$, $\phi_{ss} = T_{dx}/\kappa_x$, $\psi_{ss} = T_{dz}/\kappa_z$.

**Without yaw sensor:** $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$ ($a < 0$); $|\psi_{ss}| \le \frac{|T_{dz}| + |a||T_{dx}|}{\omega_oh}$.

**Two damped modes:** $s^4 + 2\zeta\Sigma s^3 + (\Sigma^2 - (2 - 4\zeta^2)\Pi)s^2 + 2\zeta\Sigma\Pi s + \Pi^2$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_13_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27–29, 32]]) A GEO comsat ($\omega_o = 7.29\cdot10^{-5}$ rad/s, $I_x = 800$, $I_z = 1000$ kg m²) has disturbances up to $5\cdot10^{-6}$ N m on roll and yaw. (a) With a passive bias $h = 10$ N m s, what is the average roll error under $T_{dx} = 10^{-5}$ N m, and the nutation period? (b) With $h = 20$ N m s and a yaw sensor, find $k_x$ and $k_z$ for 0.05° roll and 0.2° yaw. (c) Without a yaw sensor and 0.4° yaw allowed, what minimum bias is needed if $|a| < 1$?

**Solution:**
1. *Passive bias:* $\omega_oh = 7.29\cdot10^{-4}$ N m/rad. $\phi_{av} = 10^{-5}/7.29\cdot10^{-4} = 0.0137$ rad $= 0.79°$ (16× the 0.05° budget). $\omega_{nut} = 10/\sqrt{800\cdot1000} = 0.0112$ rad/s → period $2\pi/0.0112 = 562$ s.
2. *With yaw sensor:* $\omega_oh = 7.29\cdot10^{-5}\cdot20 = 1.458\cdot10^{-3}$.
   - $\phi_{ss} = 0.05° = 8.73\cdot10^{-4}$ rad: $k_x = \frac{5\cdot10^{-6}}{8.73\cdot10^{-4}} - 1.458\cdot10^{-3} = 5.73\cdot10^{-3} - 1.458\cdot10^{-3} = 4.27\cdot10^{-3}$ N m/rad
   - $\psi_{ss} = 0.2° = 3.49\cdot10^{-3}$ rad: $k_z = \frac{5\cdot10^{-6}}{3.49\cdot10^{-3}} - 1.458\cdot10^{-3} = 1.432\cdot10^{-3} - 1.458\cdot10^{-3} = -2.6\cdot10^{-5}$ ≈ 0. The bias alone nearly does the yaw job.
   - Check $\Pi$: $c_0 = \kappa_x\kappa_z/(I_xI_z) = (5.73\cdot10^{-3})(1.432\cdot10^{-3})/(8\cdot10^5) = 1.03\cdot10^{-11}$, $\Pi = \sqrt{c_0} = 3.20\cdot10^{-6}$ ✓.
3. *Without yaw sensor:* $\psi_{ss} = 0.4° = 6.98\cdot10^{-3}$ rad. With $|a| < 1$ the worst case is $|T_{dz}| + |a||T_{dx}| < 2T_d$:
$$
h \ge \frac{2T_d}{\omega_o\psi_{ss}} = \frac{10^{-5}}{7.29\cdot10^{-5}\cdot6.98\cdot10^{-3}} = 19.6\ \text{N m s} \ \Rightarrow\ h = 20\ \text{N m s}
$$
   With the final $|a| = 0.888$: $(1 + 0.888)T_d/(\omega_o\psi_{ss}) = 18.5 \le 20$ ✓.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Choose PD gains for a 500 kg m² axis with $\omega_n = 0.2$ rad/s, $\zeta = 0.7$.

> [!example]- Answer
> $K = I\omega_n^2 = 500\cdot0.04 = 20$ N m/rad, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI = 2\cdot0.7\cdot0.2\cdot500 = 140$ N m s/rad.

- Q: Why is the quaternion law $2q_{iE}q_{4E}$ better than Euler-angle errors for a large slew?

> [!example]- Answer
> $2q_{iE}q_{4E} = e_i\sin\alpha$, so the torque points along the Euler axis of the error: the shortest rotation, no trig, no gimbal lock. Euler-angle errors take longer paths and near gimbal lock can even turn the wrong way first.

- Q: A wheel controller fights $T_d = 2$ mN m. What dumping gain keeps $h_w$ at 1 N m s with thrusters? With magnetorquers at 20° inclination (pitch momentum)?

> [!example]- Answer
> Thrusters: $K_w = T_d/h_w = 0.002$ s⁻¹. Magnetorquers: only the share $\lambda_y = 0.24$ acts: $K_w = T_d/(\lambda_yh_w) = 0.002/0.24 = 0.0083$ s⁻¹ (then check the dipole limit).

- Q: Kepler lost two of four wheels. Which test shows it can't hold attitude, and why might a CMG cluster recover from a rank loss when Kepler couldn't?

> [!example]- Answer
> Controllability: with no stored momentum the axis without wheel torque is a free double integrator, rank$[B\ AB] < 6$. A CMG singularity depends on the gimbal angles, so moving the gimbals (null motion) can restore rank 3; dead wheels can't be moved.

- Q: How does a momentum-bias satellite control yaw with only an Earth sensor? What sign must $a$ have here and why?

> [!example]- Answer
> $\mathbf h$ is fixed inertially while the orbit frame turns, so a yaw error becomes a roll error a quarter orbit later; roll is measured and $T_{cz} = aT_{cx}$ corrects both. Yaw is observable from roll. $a < 0$: the $s^1$ coefficient $h(\omega_ok_{xd} - ak_x)$ must be positive; with $a > 0$ (and the numbers here) it goes negative and the slow mode is unstable.

- Q: What limits a wheel slew and what limits a CMG slew?

> [!example]- Answer
> Wheels are torque-limited (≈ 0.2 N m each). CMGs give huge torque but are momentum-limited (the total rotor momentum caps the body rate), and their steering must avoid singular gimbal sets.
