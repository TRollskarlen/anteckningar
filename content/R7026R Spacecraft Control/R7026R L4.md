**Question:** If every state is measured, how do we choose the feedback gain $K$ in $u = -Kx$ so the closed-loop poles go where we want (pole placement), how do we add a reference, and how can a cost function choose the poles for us (LQR)?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L3]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L5]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
State feedback $u = -Kx$ gives $\dot x = (A - BK)x$, and if $(A, B)$ is controllable $K$ can put the closed-loop poles anywhere: pick a desired polynomial $\alpha(s)$ from the specs and use Ackermann's formula $K = [0 \cdots 0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\alpha(A)$ (for one spacecraft axis this is exactly the PD law $K_p = I\omega_n^2$, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI$). A reference is added as $u = -Kx + Nr$ with $N = (-C(A-BK)^{-1}B)^{-1}$, which gives zero steady-state error without moving the poles. LQR instead minimizes $\int(x^TQx + u^TRu)\,dt$; the answer is still a state feedback $K = R^{-1}B^TP$ from the Riccati equation, and the weights trade error against actuator effort with one knob.

## Roadmap
<!-- Read this first. ⭐ = core: you must be able to do it by hand (exam, projects). No star = understand the idea. (extra) = example or background, skim it. -->
**Why this lecture?** Now we design. If every state is measured, one gain matrix $K$ can put *every* closed-loop pole where the specs from L1 want it. The classical PD attitude law turns out to be exactly this. LQR then chooses the poles for you, from a cost that trades pointing error against actuator effort.

**The storyline**
1. ⭐ *State feedback*: $u = -Kx$ gives the closed loop $A - BK$, with poles from $\det(sI - A + BK) = 0$.
2. ⭐ *Pole placement*: pick the poles, multiply out the "dream polynomial", match coefficients. In control canonical form it is trivial, $K_i = \alpha_i - a_i$.
3. ⭐ *Ackermann's formula*: the same result without changing coordinates, $K = [0 \cdots 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\alpha(A)$.
4. ⭐ *PD attitude control*: for one axis, pole placement gives $K_p = I\omega_n^2$ and $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI$.
5. ⭐ *Reference input*: $u = -Kx + Nr$ makes the output end at $r$. Without $N$, the 30° slew becomes 300°.
6. *Where to put the poles*: faster poles cost more torque ($\propto \omega_n^2$), and the dominant-pair rule from L1.
7. *Damping the wobble of a spinning satellite*: the starting point of Project 1 (model, gains, voltage limit, why dissipation helps a disc and hurts a cylinder).
8. ⭐ *LQR*: minimize $\int(x^TQx + u^TRu)\,dt$; Riccati equation, $K = R^{-1}B^TP$, Bryson's rule. For one axis by hand: $\zeta = 0.707$ for every $q/r$.
9. *(extra) Launcher thrust-vector control*: an unstable plant (an inverted pendulum on its engine) stabilized both ways.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **State feedback $u = -Kx$:** Every state is multiplied by a gain and fed back. Closed loop $\dot x = (A - BK)x$.
- **Pole placement:** Choosing $K$ so that $\det(sI - A + BK)$ equals a desired polynomial. Possible for all poles ⇔ $(A, B)$ controllable.
- **Desired ("dream") polynomial $\alpha(s)$:** $\prod(s - p_i)$ for the poles you want. You design by matching the closed-loop characteristic polynomial to it.
- **Ackermann's formula:** $K = [0 \cdots 0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\alpha(A)$. Pole placement without going through the canonical form.
- **Reference gain $N$:** Scales the reference so the output ends exactly at $r$. Doesn't move poles or zeros.
- **Dominant pole design:** Choose the slowest pair from $M_p$, $t_s$; push the rest 3–5× further left.
- **Control effort:** Moving poles further costs actuator torque/voltage. Doubling $\omega_n$ ≈ 4× torque.
- **LQR:** Linear Quadratic Regulator. Minimizes $J = \int_0^\infty (x^TQx + u^TRu)\,dt$. The optimal input is a state feedback.
- **$Q$, $R$:** Weights: how much state errors cost vs how much actuator effort costs. Only their ratio matters.
- **Riccati equation:** $A^TP + PA - PBR^{-1}B^TP + Q = 0$. Solve for $P$, then $K = R^{-1}B^TP$.
- **Bryson's rule:** $Q_{ii} = 1/x_{i,\max}^2$, $R_{jj} = 1/u_{j,\max}^2$: pick weights from what's allowed.

### State feedback: what happens to the poles ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6–7]])
$$
\dot x = Ax + Bu, \quad u = -Kx = -[K_1 \cdots K_n]\,x \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot x = (A - BK)\,x
$$
$BK$ is an $n \times n$ matrix (column times row). The closed-loop poles are the roots of
$$
\det\big(sI - (A - BK)\big) = 0
$$
→ **choose $K$ so the closed-loop poles are where we like.**

**Pole placement algorithm ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]):**
1. From the time-domain specs, choose $n$ poles $p_1, \dots, p_n$.
2. Compute the desired polynomial $\alpha(s) = \prod_{i=1}^n (s - p_i)$.
3. Compute $\det(sI - A + BK)$ as a function of $K_1, \dots, K_n$.
4. Find $K$ by **matching coefficients** of the two polynomials.

> [!tip] The "dream polynomial"
> $\alpha(s)$ is the characteristic polynomial you *wish* the closed loop had: you pick the poles you want, multiply out $(s - p_1)(s - p_2)\cdots$, and then find the $K$ that makes $\det(sI - A + BK)$ equal to it. Example: wanted poles $-2, -3$ → $\alpha(s) = (s+2)(s+3) = s^2 + 5s + 6$.

### Why the control canonical form makes it trivial ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9–12]])
In control canonical form, $A$ has the denominator coefficients in the top row and $B = [1, 0, \dots, 0]^T$. Then $BK$ only has a top row, $[K_1\ K_2 \cdots K_n]$, so
$$
A - BK = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 - K_1 & -a_2 - K_2 & \cdots & -a_n - K_n \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
\det(sI - A + BK) = s^n + (a_1 + K_1)s^{n-1} + \dots + (a_n + K_n)
$$
Each gain $K_i$ shifts exactly one coefficient. To get $\alpha(s) = s^n + \alpha_1s^{n-1} + \dots + \alpha_n$:
$$
K_i = \alpha_i - a_i
$$

> [!important] Result ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]])
> State feedback in a **fully controllable** system can place **all** closed-loop poles wherever you want.
> Caveats: weak controllability (huge gains), the more you move poles the more actuator you use (**saturation!**), and zeros are not moved.

### Ackermann's formula ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–16]])
No need to transform to canonical form first:
$$
K = [0\ \cdots\ 0\ 1]\;\mathcal{C}^{-1}\;\alpha(A)
$$
where $\alpha(A)$ = the desired polynomial with the **matrix** $A$ plugged in ($s^2 \to A^2$, constant $c \to cI$). It's the canonical-form trick with the transformation $T$ from L3 built in. Needs $\mathcal{C}^{-1}$, i.e. controllability.

**Example ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]), uncontrollable case:** $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$: $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\det\mathcal{C} = 3 - 3 = 0$ → $\mathcal{C}^{-1}$ doesn't exist ("NEJ!"). Not controllable, no $K$ can place both poles. (The full worked example with $B = [0, 1]^T$ is below.)

### Spacecraft: PD attitude control is pole placement ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17–18]])
![[R7026R L4 s17 camera satellite.png]]
A small Earth-observation satellite turns its camera about one axis, $I\ddot\theta = T$. A reaction wheel gives $T$, a star tracker measures $\theta$, a gyro measures $\dot\theta$ → both states measured, so $u = -Kx$ can be built directly. Data: $I = 10$ kg m². Wanted: overshoot below 5 %, settled in about a minute ⇒ $\zeta = 0.7$, $\omega_n = 0.1$ rad/s (L1).

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1/I \\ 1/I & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathcal{C}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ I & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Desired $\alpha(s) = s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_ns + \omega_n^2$, and since $A^2 = 0$: $\alpha(A) = 2\zeta\omega_nA + \omega_n^2I = \begin{bmatrix} \omega_n^2 & 2\zeta\omega_n \\ 0 & \omega_n^2 \end{bmatrix}$.
$$
K = [0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\alpha(A) = [I\ \ 0]\begin{bmatrix} \omega_n^2 & 2\zeta\omega_n \\ 0 & \omega_n^2 \end{bmatrix} = [I\omega_n^2\ \ \ 2\zeta\omega_nI]
$$
$$
T = -K_p\theta - K_d\dot\theta, \qquad K_p = I\omega_n^2, \qquad K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI
$$
**The classical PD attitude loop**: torque from the angle (spring) and from the rate (damper). Numbers: $K_p = 0.1$ N m/rad, $K_d = 1.4$ N m s/rad, $M_p = 4.6\,\%$, $t_s \approx 66$ s.

→ The PD gains you already know **are** a state feedback, and now we know where they put the poles.

### Reference inputs ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20–25]])
Goal: make the output settle at a desired value, $y_{ss} = r_{ss}$.

**Wrong way ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]):** $u = -Kx + r$. At steady state $\dot x = 0$:
$$
0 = (A - BK)x_{ss} + Br_{ss} \;\Rightarrow\; x_{ss} = -(A - BK)^{-1}Br_{ss} \;\Rightarrow\; y_{ss} = -C(A - BK)^{-1}B\,r_{ss} \neq r_{ss}
$$
The output ends at the wrong value (scaled by the DC gain of the closed loop).

**Correct way ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]):** scale the reference with $N$:
$$
u = -Kx + Nr, \qquad N = \big(-C(A - BK)^{-1}B\big)^{-1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad y_{ss} = r_{ss}
$$
$N$ is just "1 / the closed-loop DC gain". (Discrete time: same idea but the steady state is $x(k+1) = x(k)$, so $(A - BK)$ is replaced by $(A - BK - I)$; left as HW.)
![[R7026R L4 s23 reference input with N.png]]

**What happens to poles and zeros? ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24–25]])** $\dot x = (A - BK)x + BNr$: the **poles stay** where $K$ put them, and the **zeros stay** the open-loop zeros. So $N$ doesn't disturb the design of $K$ (nor of the observer $L$ in L5), but it also gives no way to choose the zeros.

Do we like it? **Depends:** fine when the plant's zeros are harmless (one spacecraft axis has none). But $N$ comes from the model: a wrong $I$ or a constant disturbance torque leaves a **steady-state error** → integral action (L7).

> [!tip] Plain words
> Without a reference, state feedback drives everything to **zero**. $N$ tells it "zero is now over there at $r$". It works by feed-forward, so if the model is wrong, it ends up slightly off.

### Spacecraft: a 30° slew ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26–27]])

Same axis and gains $K = [0.1, 1.4]$, $y = \theta$, $r = 30° = 0.524$ rad.

- **Wrong way** $T = -Kx + r$: $\theta_{ss} = r/K_p = 10r$, a **300° turn** instead of 30°.

### Where to put the poles ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29–31]])
**Main issues ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]):**
- The more you move the poles, the more you use the actuators.
- A closed-loop pole next to a closed-loop zero makes control difficult.

![[R7026R L4 s29 pole speed vs torque.png]]
*30° slew with $\zeta = 0.7$ ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]): doubling $\omega_n$ halves the settling time but needs **four times** the torque ($K_pr = I\omega_n^2r$: 0.013, 0.052, 0.21 N m).*

**Dominant second-order poles ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30–31]]), recap from L1:**
![[R7026R L4 s30 dominant pole region.png]]
1. Define the time-domain specs ($M_p$, $t_s$, $t_r$).
2. Select the dominant pair inside the allowed region.
3. Design $K$ so the other poles are several (3–5) times further left.
4. Check that zeros don't disturb the dominant pair.

Problem: this method **ignores how much control effort** is needed. → LQR.

### Spacecraft: damping the wobble of a spinning satellite ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32–37]])
![[R7026R L4 s32 spinning disc with damper wheel.png]]
A disc satellite spins about its symmetry axis $Z_B$ at $\omega_S = 12.6$ rad/s. A disturbance left transverse rates $\omega_x, \omega_y$: it **wobbles** (nutation). A damper wheel on $Y_B$ ($I_w = 0.5$ kg m²) driven by a DC motor ($K_m = 0.1$ N m/A, $R_m = 1\ \Omega$, $|e_M| \le 10$ V). Disc: $m = 50$ kg, $r = 1$ m, $\ell = 0.5$ m ⇒ $I_S = \frac12mr^2 = 25$ kg m², $I_T = \frac{m}{12}(3r^2 + \ell^2) = 13.54$ kg m². **Goal: wobble gone within a minute, without exceeding 10 V.**

**Model ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33–34]], derived in L12):** with $\alpha_S = \frac{I_T - I_S}{I_T}\omega_S$, $\epsilon = \frac{I_w}{I_T}$, $\gamma = \frac{K_m^2}{R_mI_w}$, $\beta = \frac{K_m}{R_mI_w}$:
$$
\dot\omega_x = \alpha_S\omega_y + \epsilon\omega_S\Omega, \qquad \dot\omega_y = -\alpha_S\omega_x - \epsilon\dot\Omega, \qquad \dot\Omega + \dot\omega_y = -\gamma\Omega + \beta e_M
$$
- $\alpha_S$ terms: Euler's equations, the transverse rates turning into each other (the wobble).
- $\epsilon\omega_S\Omega$: the spin carries the wheel's momentum around → a torque on $X_B$.
- $-\epsilon\dot\Omega$: the wheel's reaction torque on the body.
- Motor line: back-emf $K_m\Omega$ gives the damping $\gamma$, the voltage gives the input gain $\beta$.

With $x = [\omega_x, \omega_y, \eta]^T$ ($\eta = \omega_y + \Omega$, the wheel's inertial rate), $u = e_M$:
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -11.127 & 0.465 \\ 11.070 & -0.0008 & 0.0008 \\ 0 & 0.020 & -0.020 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -0.0077 \\ 0.200 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Open loop ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35]]):** poles $-0.0008 \pm 11.10j$ and $-0.019$. Stable, but the wobble decays with time constant $1/0.0008 \approx 1250$ s ≈ **20 minutes**. Far too slow. $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = 3$ ⇒ the wheel can move all three poles.

> [!tip] Why is it stable at all?
> At fixed angular momentum $h$, spinning about inertia $I$ has energy $h^2/(2I)$. The motor's resistance dissipates energy, so the motion drifts toward the **largest** inertia. The disc already spins about its largest ($I_S > I_T$) → stable. A tall cylinder spinning about its **smallest** inertia ($I_S < I_T$): the same dissipation makes the wobble **grow** → unstable (Project 1's satellite!).

**Pole placement ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]):** keep the wobble frequency, move the pair left by $\sigma$, wheel pole at $-0.3$: $p = -\sigma \pm 11.1j,\ -0.3$, `K = place(A, B, p)`.

| $\sigma$ | $t_s \approx 4.6/\sigma$ | peak $\lvert e_M\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 92 s | 5.4 V |
| 0.10 | 46 s | 9.5 V |
| 0.20 | 23 s | 17 V ✗ |

![[R7026R L4 s36 nutation pole placement.png]]
*Faster poles = more voltage ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]). $\sigma = 0.1$ is the fastest design inside 10 V: $K = [0.39\ \ -12.4\ \ 1.92]$.*

**What the gains say ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]]):** almost all gain is on $\omega_y$, because the wheel sits on $Y_B$: its torque only pushes directly on $\omega_y$. Opposing $\omega_y$ removes energy from the wobble every cycle = rate damping on the axis we can reach. Alone, though, it lets the wheel speed drift (pole at $+0.08$); the $\eta$ gain holds the wheel (pole at $-0.3$); the $\omega_x$ gain hardly matters.

**Project 1:** the tall cylinder ($r = 0.5$ m, $\ell = 1$ m), `geometry = 'cylinder'`. Only a noisy gyro measures $\omega_x$ → the state comes from a Kalman filter (L7). Careful: there dissipation makes the wobble **grow**; check the sign of the $\omega_y$ gain.

### LQR: let a cost choose the poles ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39–42]])
**Idea ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]]):**
$$
J = \rho\|y\|_2^2 + \|u\|_2^2, \qquad \|\chi\|_2^2 = \int_0^\infty \chi^2(t)\,dt
$$
- $\|y\|^2$: how long and how far the output stays away from zero.
- $\|u\|^2$: how much actuator effort (fuel, wheel torque) we spend.
- $\rho$ large: pay mostly for errors ⇒ **fast and aggressive**.
- $\rho$ small: pay mostly for effort ⇒ **slow and gentle**.
- $\rho \to 0$ (effort infinitely expensive): stable open-loop poles stay, unstable ones move to their mirror images. Just enough to stabilize.

> [!important] Theorem 1 ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40]])
> For a controllable system, the $u$ that minimizes $J$ is a **state feedback** $u = -Kx$.
> LQR only chooses **where the poles go**; the structure is the same as pole placement.

**Symmetric root locus ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=41|s. 41]]):** with $N(s) = \det\begin{bmatrix} sI - A & -B \\ C & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (zeros) and $D(s) = \det(sI - A)$ (poles), the optimal closed-loop poles are the $n$ **stable** roots of
$$
D(-s)D(s) + \rho N(-s)N(s) = 0
$$
The roots come in pairs $\pm p$ (symmetric about the imaginary axis). Plot vs $\rho$, pick the stable ones, then use Ackermann.

**General LQR and the Riccati equation ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=42|s. 42]]):**
$$
J = \int_0^\infty \big(x^TQx + u^TRu\big)\,dt, \qquad Q \text{ s.d.p.},\ R \text{ d.p.}
$$
($\rho\|y\|^2 + \|u\|^2$ is the case $Q = \rho C^TC$, $R = 1$.)
1. Solve the **algebraic Riccati equation** for $P = P^T$ d.p.:
$$
A^TP + PA - PBR^{-1}B^TP + Q = 0
$$
2. Optimal gain: $K = R^{-1}B^TP$, $u = -Kx$.

If $(A, B)$ stabilizable and $(A, Q^{1/2})$ detectable (L3), $A - BK$ is stable. Compare the Lyapunov equation of L2: $-PBR^{-1}B^TP$ is the extra term the control adds. MATLAB: `K = lqr(A, B, Q, R)`.

> [!tip] Plain words: $Q$ and $R$
> $Q$ = "how much do I hate each state error", $R$ = "how much do I hate using each actuator". A diagonal $Q = \operatorname{diag}(1, 10)$ says the second state's error is 10× worse. Only the **ratio** $Q/R$ matters. For a $2\times2$ $P$ to be d.p. (needed for the Riccati solution): $p_{11} > 0$ and $p_{11}p_{22} > p_{12}^2$ (Sylvester, L2).

**Discrete-time LQR ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43]]):** $J = \sum_k (x^TQx + u^TRu)$,
$$
P = A^TPA - A^TPB(R + B^TPB)^{-1}B^TPA + Q, \qquad K = (R + B^TPB)^{-1}B^TPA
$$
MATLAB: `K = dlqr(A, B, Q, R)`.

### Spacecraft: LQR on one axis ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44–45]])
$I\ddot\theta = T$, $Q = \begin{bmatrix} q & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (only the angle error costs), $R = r$. The Riccati equation can be solved by hand:
$$
K = \left[\sqrt{q/r},\ \ \sqrt{2I\sqrt{q/r}}\right]
$$
Closed loop $s^2 + \frac{K_2}{I}s + \frac{K_1}{I}$ ⇒
$$
\omega_n = \frac{(q/r)^{1/4}}{\sqrt I}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = 0.707 \text{ for every } q/r
$$
Why: $D(s) = s^2$, $N(s) = 1/I$, so the symmetric root locus is $I^2s^4 + q/r = 0$. Its four roots lie on the ±45° lines; LQR keeps the two stable ones.
![[R7026R L4 s44 lqr symmetric root locus.png]]
*A larger weight moves the poles out along the 45° line: one weight sets the speed, the damping stays 0.707 ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44]]).*

**Choosing $q/r$ for the 30° slew ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=45|s. 45]]):**

| $q/r$ | $K$ | $\omega_n$ [rad/s] | $T_{peak}$ [N m] |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $[1, 4.47]$ | 0.316 | 0.52 |
| 0.1 | $[0.316, 2.51]$ | 0.178 | 0.17 |
| 0.01 | $[0.1, 1.41]$ | 0.1 | 0.052 |

The wheel gives about 0.05 N m. **Bryson's rule** ($q = 1/\theta_{max}^2$, $r = 1/T_{max}^2$) with $\theta_{max} = 30°$: $q/r = (0.05/0.524)^2 \approx 0.01$. → $q/r = 0.01$ gives back the PD design $[0.1, 1.4]$, but with **one** weight instead of two pole positions.

### Space example: thrust-vector control of a launcher ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=46|s. 46–48]])
![[R7026R L4 s46 launcher.png]]
A rigid launcher in the atmosphere, pitch plane. The center of pressure (CP) is **ahead of** the center of mass (CM): the aerodynamic moment turns it away from the flight path. The engine is gimballed by $\delta$:
$$
\ddot\theta = \mu_\alpha\theta + \mu_c\delta, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \mu_\alpha & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \mu_c \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{eigenvalues } \pm\sqrt{\mu_\alpha}
$$
$\mu_\alpha > 0$: aerodynamic instability; $\mu_c$: control effectiveness. **An inverted pendulum balanced on its own engine.**

Values: $\mu_\alpha = 1$ s⁻², $\mu_c = 4$ s⁻² (open-loop poles $\pm1$: the error doubles every 0.69 s).

**Pole placement** with $\omega_n = 3$ rad/s, $\zeta = 0.7$:
$$
\det(sI - A + BK) = s^2 + \mu_cK_2s + (\mu_cK_1 - \mu_\alpha) \;\Rightarrow\; K = \left[\frac{\omega_n^2 + \mu_\alpha}{\mu_c},\ \frac{2\zeta\omega_n}{\mu_c}\right] = [2.5,\ 1.05]
$$
A 1° pitch error commands 2.5° of gimbal; part of $K_1$ only cancels the instability $\mu_\alpha$.

**LQR**, $Q = \operatorname{diag}(q, 0)$, $R = r$:

| $q/r$ | $K$ | closed-loop poles | $\omega_n$, $\zeta$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $[1.28, 0.80]$ | $-1.60 \pm 1.25j$ | 2.03 rad/s, 0.79 |
| 10 | $[3.42, 1.31]$ | $-2.62 \pm 2.42j$ | 3.56 rad/s, 0.73 |

![[R7026R L4 s48 launcher simulation.png]]
*Start at $\theta = 1°$ ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=48|s. 48]]). Without control the error grows as $\cosh t$: almost 4° after 2 s. Bryson's rule with 2° allowed pitch error and 6° gimbal travel: $q/r = (6/2)^2 = 9 \approx 10$. More weight on the angle: faster recovery, paid in gimbal travel.*

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**State feedback**
$$
u = -Kx, \qquad \dot x = (A - BK)x, \qquad \text{poles: } \det(sI - A + BK) = 0
$$
**Desired polynomial and Ackermann**
$$
\alpha(s) = \prod_{i=1}^n(s - p_i), \qquad K = [0\ \cdots\ 0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}\,\alpha(A)
$$
where $p_i$ = wanted poles, $\mathcal{C}$ = controllability matrix, $\alpha(A)$ = $\alpha$ with $A$ plugged in.

**Canonical form:** $K_i = \alpha_i - a_i$.

**One axis PD**
$$
K_p = I\omega_n^2, \qquad K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI
$$
**Reference**
$$
u = -Kx + Nr, \qquad N = \big(-C(A - BK)^{-1}B\big)^{-1}
$$
**LQR**
$$
J = \int_0^\infty (x^TQx + u^TRu)\,dt, \qquad A^TP + PA - PBR^{-1}B^TP + Q = 0, \qquad K = R^{-1}B^TP
$$
where $Q$ = state weight (s.d.p.), $R$ = input weight (d.p.), $P$ = Riccati solution (d.p.).

**LQR one axis** ($Q = \operatorname{diag}(q, 0)$, $R = r$)
$$
K = \left[\sqrt{q/r},\ \sqrt{2I\sqrt{q/r}}\right], \qquad \omega_n = \frac{(q/r)^{1/4}}{\sqrt I}, \qquad \zeta = 0.707
$$
**Bryson's rule:** $q_i = 1/x_{i,\max}^2$, $r_j = 1/u_{j,\max}^2$.

## Common mistakes
- **Sign of the feedback.** The course uses $u = -Kx$, so the closed loop is $A - BK$. If you write $u = Kx$, every sign in $K$ flips.
- **Complex poles without their partner.** Wanted poles must come in conjugate pairs ($-\sigma \pm j\omega_d$), otherwise $\alpha(s)$ has complex coefficients and so does $K$.
- **Ackermann details.** Use the *last row* of $\mathcal{C}^{-1}$, and in $\alpha(A)$ the constant term becomes a matrix: $6 \to 6I$.
- **Not checking the answer.** Put $K$ back and compute $\det(sI - A + BK)$. It takes a minute and catches most errors (step 5 of the worked example).
- **$N$ from the wrong matrix.** $N = (-C(A - BK)^{-1}B)^{-1}$ uses the *closed-loop* matrix. And $N$ is model based: a wrong $I$ or a constant disturbance torque still leaves a steady-state error (integral action, L7).
- **Degrees in $u = -Kx$.** $K_p$ is in N m/rad. Angles go in as radians.
- **Bryson with the wrong power.** The weights are $1/x_{\max}^2$ and $1/u_{\max}^2$, not $1/x_{\max}$.
- **LQR does not see what $Q$ does not weight.** It stabilizes the loop only if $(A, B)$ is stabilizable and $(A, Q^{1/2})$ is detectable. An unstable mode that $Q$ does not "see" and that costs effort to move is left alone.
- **Forgetting saturation.** A design that is fast on paper can need more than the actuator gives (17 V instead of 10 V for the disc). Always check the peak input.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_4_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15–16]]) $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Open-loop poles $-1, -3$. Find $K$ that places the closed-loop poles at $-2$ and $-3$, using Ackermann. Check the answer.

**Solution:**
1. *Controllability:* $AB = \begin{bmatrix} -1\cdot0 + 2\cdot1 \\ 0 - 3\cdot1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathcal{C} = [B\ \ AB] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\det\mathcal{C} = 0\cdot(-3) - 2\cdot1 = -2 \neq 0$ ✓ controllable.
2. *Inverse:*
$$
\mathcal{C}^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2}\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -2 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & 1 \\ 0.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad [0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1} = [0.5\ \ 0]
$$
3. *Dream polynomial:* $\alpha(s) = (s+2)(s+3) = s^2 + 5s + 6$, so $\alpha(A) = A^2 + 5A + 6I$:
$$
A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -8 \\ 0 & 9 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \alpha(A) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 5 + 6 & -8 + 10 \\ 0 & 9 - 15 + 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
4. *Ackermann:* $K = [0.5\ \ 0]\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = [1,\ 1]$.
5. *Check:* $BK = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, so $A - BK = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ -1 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$ and
$$
\det(sI - A + BK) = \det\begin{bmatrix} s+1 & -2 \\ 1 & s+4 \end{bmatrix} = (s+1)(s+4) - (-2)(1) = s^2 + 5s + 6 \ ✓
$$

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: One spacecraft axis, $I = 20$ kg m², wanted $\zeta = 0.8$, $\omega_n = 0.05$ rad/s. Find $K_p$, $K_d$ and the settling time.

> [!example]- Answer
> $K_p = I\omega_n^2 = 20 \cdot 0.0025 = 0.05$ N m/rad. $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI = 2 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 0.05 \cdot 20 = 1.6$ N m s/rad. $t_s \approx 4.6/(\zeta\omega_n) = 4.6/0.04 = 115$ s.

- Q: You use $u = -Kx + r$ (no $N$) on the axis above with a 10° reference. Where does it end up?

> [!example]- Answer
> $\theta_{ss} = r/K_p = 10°/0.05 = 200°$. The DC gain of the closed loop is $1/K_p$, which is why you need $N = K_p$.

- Q: Does adding $N$ change the closed-loop poles? The zeros?

> [!example]- Answer
> Neither. $\dot x = (A - BK)x + BNr$: the poles are set by $A - BK$ and the zeros stay the open-loop zeros. $N$ only rescales the input.

- Q: In LQR for one axis, what happens to $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$ if you multiply $q/r$ by 16?

> [!example]- Answer
> $\zeta$ stays 0.707. $\omega_n \propto (q/r)^{1/4}$, so it doubles. Torque roughly scales with $\omega_n^2$, so ~4× more torque.

- Q: Why is pole placement for the disc satellite limited to $\sigma = 0.1$?

> [!example]- Answer
> Faster poles need more motor voltage. $\sigma = 0.2$ needs 17 V, but the motor is limited to 10 V. Moving poles always costs actuator effort.
