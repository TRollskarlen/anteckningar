**Question:** Before designing anything: can the actuators move every state (controllability), and can the sensors see every state (observability)? And what if they can't?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L2]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L4]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
A system is controllable if $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = n$ with $\mathcal{C} = [B\ AB\ \cdots\ A^{n-1}B]$, and observable if $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O} = n$ with $\mathcal{O}$ stacking $C, CA, \dots, CA^{n-1}$; these two rank tests tell whether a controller or observer can work at all (a radial thruster alone cannot stop an along-track drift, a gyro alone can never give the angle). Duality says $(A, C)$ observable ⇔ $(A^T, C^T)$ controllable, so every controller tool is also an observer tool, and the control/observable canonical forms exist exactly when the system is controllable/observable. What cannot be steered or seen must die out by itself (stabilizable/detectable), otherwise no controller or observer can fix it.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Controllability:** The input can bring the state from anywhere to anywhere. Needed for state feedback (L4).
- **Controllability matrix $\mathcal{C} = [B\ AB\ \cdots\ A^{n-1}B]$:** Its columns are the directions the input can push the state. Full rank ($= n$) ⇔ controllable.
- **Reachable subspace:** $\operatorname{range}\mathcal{C}$, all states the input can reach from 0.
- **Observability:** From the output $y$ (and known $u$) you can recover the state $x$. Needed for observers/Kalman (L5, L7).
- **Observability matrix $\mathcal{O} = [C;\ CA;\ \cdots;\ CA^{n-1}]$:** Full rank ⇔ observable. Its null space = the invisible states.
- **State transformation $x = T\tilde x$:** Same system in new coordinates: $\tilde A = T^{-1}AT$, $\tilde B = T^{-1}B$, $\tilde C = CT$. Doesn't change eigenvalues, controllability or observability.
- **Canonical forms:** Special coordinates where a design step becomes trivial (control form → pole placement, observable form → observers, modal form → read off modes).
- **Duality:** $\Sigma = (A, B, C)$ ↔ $\Sigma_d = (A^T, C^T, B^T)$. Controllable ⇔ dual observable.
- **Stabilizable / detectable:** All uncontrollable / unobservable modes are asymptotically stable. The minimum requirement for LQR (L4) / Kalman (L7).
- **Kalman decomposition:** Splits the state into four parts (controllable/observable or not). The transfer function only sees the controllable *and* observable part.

### From L2 to this lecture ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3]])
We have state-space models $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx + Du$ and know stability = eigenvalues of $A$. Today two new questions **before** designing a controller or an observer:
- **Controllability:** can the actuators move every state?
- **Observability:** can the sensors see every state?

Each is answered by **one rank test**. Spoiler: a radial thruster alone cannot stop a drift, and a gyro alone cannot give the angle.

### Controllability: an example where it fails ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–5]])
![[R7026R L3 s4 circuit example.png]]
Two RC branches driven by the same source $e_0$. Look at the voltage across $R_3$: $v_3 = v_1 - v_2$. Its equation contains $e_0$ with the factor $(R_2C_2 - R_1C_1)$. If $R_1C_1 = R_2C_2$ (the two branches are identical) the input **disappears**:
$$
\dot v_3 = -\frac{R_1 + R_2 + R_3}{C_1R_1R_3}\,v_3
$$
Whatever we apply, $v_3$ just decays on its own. Intuition: the source pushes both sides exactly the same, so their *difference* can never be changed.

### Where the rank test comes from ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6–9]])
Easiest in discrete time, $x(k+1) = Ax(k) + Bu(k)$, starting from $x(0) = 0$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x(1) &= Bu(0) \\
x(2) &= ABu(0) + Bu(1) \\
x(3) &= A^2Bu(0) + ABu(1) + Bu(2) \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
x(k+1) &= \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^2B & \cdots & A^kB \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} u(k) \\ u(k-1) \\ \vdots \\ u(0) \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
The reachable states are **linear combinations of the columns** $B, AB, A^2B, \dots$ If those columns span all $n$ directions (rank $n$), we can choose the inputs to reach **any** state.

Why stop at $A^{n-1}B$? **Cayley–Hamilton:** $A^n$ is a combination of $I, A, \dots, A^{n-1}$, so $A^nB$ and later columns add no new directions.

> [!tip] Plain words
> $B$ = the directions the input pushes *directly*. $AB$ = where the system's own dynamics carries that push one step later. $A^2B$ = two steps later, and so on. If together they point in every direction, you can steer anywhere.

### The rank test ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]])
> [!important] Controllability
> $$\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^2B & \cdots & A^{n-1}B \end{bmatrix}$$
> - Reachable subspace = $\operatorname{range}\mathcal{C}$, with dimension $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C}$.
> - The system is **controllable** ⇔ $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = n$.
>
> (The directions you *can't* reach are those orthogonal to $\operatorname{range}\mathcal{C}$, i.e. the null space of $\mathcal{C}^T$.)

Same test in continuous time.

**One spacecraft axis with a reaction wheel ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):**
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I \end{bmatrix}, \quad AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1/I \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1/I \\ 1/I & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Rank 2 ⇒ controllable: a torque can bring angle *and* rate anywhere. The torque acts on the rate directly ($B$), and the rate then integrates into the angle ($AB$).
![[R7026R L3 s11 reaction wheel axis.png]]

**Question 1 & 2 ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12–13]]):** draw an arrow $x_j \to x_i$ for every $a_{ij} \neq 0$. If the input can reach every state by following arrows, it's (usually) controllable.

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\0\\0 \end{bmatrix}$: $AB = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}$, $A^2B = \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}$, $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&2 \\ 0&1&1 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}$ → triangular with ones on the diagonal, rank 3, **controllable**.
![[R7026R L3 s12 controllable graph.png]]

Change the last row of $A$ to $[0\ 0\ 1]$: $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&2 \\ 0&1&1 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}$ → rank 2, **not controllable**. The third row says $\dot x_3 = x_3$: nobody pushes on $x_3$.
![[R7026R L3 s13 uncontrollable graph.png]]

### State transformations ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14–15]])
The state-space description is **not unique**. With an invertible $T$ and $x = T\tilde x$:
$$
\tilde A = T^{-1}AT, \qquad \tilde B = T^{-1}B, \qquad \tilde C = CT, \qquad \tilde D = D
$$
Same physical system, different coordinates (like describing a vector in a rotated basis). The eigenvalues don't change.

**Does it change controllability? No ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]):**
$$
\tilde{\mathcal{C}} = [\tilde B\ \ \tilde A\tilde B\ \cdots] = [T^{-1}B\ \ T^{-1}ATT^{-1}B\ \cdots] = T^{-1}[B\ \ AB\ \cdots] = T^{-1}\mathcal{C}
$$
Multiplying by an invertible matrix doesn't change rank. Same for observability.

### Control canonical form ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17–22]])
A transfer function has **infinitely many** state-space realizations (any $T$). Some have a structure that makes a design step trivial: **canonical forms**.

For
$$
G(s) = \frac{b(s)}{a(s)} = \frac{b_1s^{n-1} + \dots + b_n}{s^n + a_1s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n}
$$
split it in two: first $x_n = \frac{1}{a(s)}u$ (only poles), then $y = b(s)x_n$ (only zeros). Drawing the first part as a chain of integrators gives:
![[R7026R L3 s19 chain of integrators.png]]
$$
A_c = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & -a_2 & \cdots & -a_n \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
C_c = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & \cdots & b_n \end{bmatrix}
$$
- **Top row** = the denominator coefficients (with minus sign) → the poles.
- **$C$** = the numerator coefficients → the zeros.
- The ones below the diagonal = the chain of integrators (each state is the integral of the one above).
- MATLAB: `tf2ss`.

Why it's useful: in L4, state feedback $u = -Kx$ just **adds $K$ to the top row**, so you can choose the characteristic polynomial directly.

> [!important] Theorem 4 ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]])
> A system can be written in control canonical form ⇔ the system is **controllable**.
> Reason: the algorithm below needs $\mathcal{C}^{-1}$, which exists exactly when $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = n$.

**Algorithm ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]):**
1. Compute $\mathcal{C}$ from $A, B$.
2. Check that it has full rank.
3. Compute $\mathcal{C}^{-1}$.
4. $t_n = [0, \dots, 0, 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}$ (the last row of $\mathcal{C}^{-1}$).
5. $t_i = t_n A^{n-i}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$.
6. $T^{-1}$ = the matrix with **rows** $t_1, \dots, t_n$.
7. $T = (T^{-1})^{-1}$.
8. $\tilde A = T^{-1}AT$, $\tilde B = T^{-1}B$, $\tilde C = CT$.

### Space example: thrusting in orbit ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23–24]])
Clohessy–Wiltshire in-plane (L2), $x = [x, y, \dot x, \dot y]^T$:
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 3n^2 & 0 & 0 & 2n \\ 0 & 0 & -2n & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
![[R7026R L3 s23 thrusting in orbit.png]]
- **Radial thruster only**, $B_r = [0, 0, 1, 0]^T$: $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = 3$ ⇒ **not controllable**.
- **Along-track thruster only**, $B_t = [0, 0, 0, 1]^T$: $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = 4$ ⇒ **controllable**.

**Why a radial thruster can't stop a drift ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]):** from the CW equations,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\dot y + 2nx) = \ddot y + 2n\dot x = (-2n\dot x + u_y) + 2n\dot x = u_y
$$
The quantity $\dot y + 2nx$ measures the difference in **orbital energy** (semi-major axis) between chaser and target, and it sets the along-track drift. Only $u_y$ changes it. A radial push is perpendicular to the velocity, so to first order it does **no work** on the orbit and can't change the energy.
![[R7026R L3 s24 radial drift.png]]
*A lower orbit is faster: the chaser drifts ahead. A radial push does no work; an along-track push changes the orbit's energy ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]).*

### Observability ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26–27]])
**Question 3:** we measure $y$ (and know $u$). Can we recover $x$?

Discrete time, $u = 0$ for simplicity (a known $u$ can be subtracted):
$$
y(0) = Cx(0), \quad y(1) = CAx(0), \quad \dots, \quad y(n-1) = CA^{n-1}x(0)
$$
$$
\begin{bmatrix} y(0) \\ y(1) \\ \vdots \\ y(n-1) \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathcal{O}} x(0)
$$
A linear system of equations for $x(0)$. Unique solution ⇔ $\ker\mathcal{O} = \{0\}$ ⇔ $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O} = n$. If $\mathcal{O}x^* = 0$ for some $x^* \neq 0$, starting from $x^*$ gives $y \equiv 0$: **that state is invisible**.

> [!important] Observability
> $$\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}$$
> $(A, C)$ is **observable** ⇔ $\ker\mathcal{O} = \{0\}$ ⇔ $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O} = n$.
> The **unobservable subspace** is $\ker\mathcal{O}$ (the null space).

- $n$ samples are enough (Cayley–Hamilton again).
- Continuous time: same test, using $y, \dot y, \dots, y^{(n-1)}$ instead of samples.

> [!tip] Plain words
> $C$ = what the sensor sees now. $CA$ = how what it sees *changes* (one step later). If watching the sensor over time reveals every state, it's observable. Geometrically: a state vector can be split into a part in the null space (invisible) and a part orthogonal to it (fully visible).

**Weak vs strong (from the lecture):** rank is yes/no, but in practice "almost singular" matters too. **Weak observability** → noisy estimates (noise gets amplified when inverting $\mathcal{O}$). **Weak controllability** → large inputs needed.

### Example: star tracker or gyro? ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28]])
One axis, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $x = [\theta, \dot\theta]^T$.

| | Star tracker (angle) | Gyro (rate) |
|---|---|---|
| $C$ | $[1\ 0]$ | $[0\ 1]$ |
| $CA$ | $[0\ 1]$ | $[0\ 0]$ |
| $\mathcal{O}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Rank | 2 ⇒ **observable** | 1 ⇒ **not observable** |
| Why | the rate shows in how fast the angle changes | $x^* = [1, 0]^T$ (a constant angle offset) gives $y \equiv 0$ |

Integrating a gyro gives the angle only up to the unknown $\theta(0)$. Measuring the derivative never gives you the value itself. → **Gyros fly together with star trackers** (L7).

### Space example: measuring relative position ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]])
CW in-plane again.
- **Radial offset measured**, $C_r = [1\ 0\ 0\ 0]$:
$$
\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 3n^2 & 0 & 0 & 2n \\ 0 & 0 & -n^2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
  Rank 3 ⇒ **not observable**. The column of zeros: $x^* = [0, 1, 0, 0]^T$ is invisible.
- **Along-track offset measured**, $C_t = [0\ 1\ 0\ 0]$: rank 4 ⇒ **observable**.

![[R7026R L3 s29 along-track offset.png]]
*A chaser shifted along the target's own orbit ($y \neq 0$, $x = 0$) never moves radially: a pure along-track offset can't be seen in $x$ ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]).*

### Preview of Part 3: yaw seen through roll ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30]])
Linearized roll $\phi$ and yaw $\psi$ of an Earth-pointing spacecraft (derived in L9) are **coupled** through the orbital rate $\omega_o$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_x\ddot\phi + 4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi + \omega_o(I_y - I_z - I_x)\dot\psi &= T_x \\
I_z\ddot\psi + \omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)\psi + \omega_o(I_z + I_x - I_y)\dot\phi &= T_z
\end{aligned}
$$
![[R7026R L3 s30 roll and yaw.png]]
An Earth sensor measures **roll only** (roll tilts the Earth in its view, yaw doesn't). Still:
$$
\det\mathcal{O} = \frac{\omega_o^4(I_x - I_y)(I_x + I_z - I_y)^2}{I_x^2I_z}
$$
→ **observable** as long as $\omega_o \neq 0$, $I_x \neq I_y$ and $I_x + I_z \neq I_y$. The orbital rate couples yaw into roll, so yaw can be estimated from roll measurements alone. An inertially pointing spacecraft ($\omega_o = 0$) would lose it.

### Duality ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]])
Intuition: controllability and observability "are the same thing" seen from opposite ends.

Dual system: $\Sigma = (A, B, C, 0)$ → $\Sigma_d = (A^T, C^T, B^T, 0)$. Then
$$
\mathcal{C}_{\Sigma_d} = [C^T\ \ A^TC^T\ \cdots] = \mathcal{O}_\Sigma^T, \qquad \mathcal{O}_{\Sigma_d} = \mathcal{C}_\Sigma^T
$$
so
- $\Sigma$ controllable ⇔ $\Sigma_d$ observable
- $\Sigma$ observable ⇔ $\Sigma_d$ controllable

![[R7026R L3 s31 duality.png]]
*Reverse every arrow, transpose every gain ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]]). In the dual, $u_d$ enters through $C^T$ and $y_d$ comes out through $B^T$.*

> [!warning] Check the dual diagram
> In the lecture the dual block diagram was said to have $B^T$ and $C^T$ in the wrong places. In this handout it reads correctly: $u_d \to C^T \to \int \to B^T \to y_d$. If you see a version with $B^T$ at the input, it's the old wrong one.

**Why it matters:** $(A, C)$ observable ⇔ $(A^T, C^T)$ controllable → every tool for designing controllers (L4) gives a tool for designing observers (L5).

### Observable canonical form ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32–33]])
Both forms realize the same $G(s) = \frac{b_1s^2 + b_2s + b_3}{s^3 + a_1s^2 + a_2s + a_3}$:

| Control canonical form | Observable canonical form |
|---|---|
| $A_c = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & -a_2 & -a_3 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ | $A_o = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & 1 & 0 \\ -a_2 & 0 & 1 \\ -a_3 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| $B_c = [1, 0, 0]^T$ | $B_o = [b_1, b_2, b_3]^T$ |
| $C_c = [b_1\ b_2\ b_3]$ | $C_o = [1\ 0\ 0]$ |

$A_o = A_c^T$, $B_o = C_c^T$, $C_o = B_c^T$: the observable form **is the dual** of the control form. Same transfer function because $G(s)$ is a scalar, so it equals its own transpose $B^T(sI - A^T)^{-1}C^T$.

> [!important] Theorem 8 ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]])
> A system can be written in observable canonical form ⇔ the system is **observable**.

**Algorithm (only one needed):** take the dual $\Sigma_d = (A^T, C^T, B^T)$ → check $\mathcal{O}$ full rank (⇔ $\Sigma_d$ controllable) → put $\Sigma_d$ in control canonical form with $T_d$ → transpose back. $T = T_d^{-T}$.

### Quiz: TF ↔ state space ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34–35]])
1. From any $(A, B, C, 0)$, can you always compute the TF $\frac{b(s)}{a(s)}$? **Yes** (but it may have pole-zero cancellations).
2. From any TF, can you always write a state-space system in control or observable canonical form? **Yes**, always.
3. Can any given $(A, B, C, 0)$ be *transformed* into control/observable canonical form? **Only if it is controllable (observable).**

> [!warning] Careful with wording
> "**Write** a TF in canonical form" (always possible) ≠ "**transform** a given system into canonical form" (only if controllable/observable). Going $\Sigma \to$ TF $\to \Sigma_c$ can give a *different, smaller* system if the TF had cancellations.

### Systems that are not controllable / not observable ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36–37]])
**Not controllable**, $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = n' < n$. Choose a basis where the first $n'$ vectors span $\operatorname{range}\mathcal{C}$:
$$
\tilde A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ 0 & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \tilde B = \begin{bmatrix} B_1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \tilde C = [C_1\ \ C_2]
$$
$$
\dot z_1 = A_{11}z_1 + A_{12}z_2 + B_1u, \qquad \dot z_2 = A_{22}z_2
$$
$u$ never affects $z_2$: the **eigenvalues of $A_{22}$ are the uncontrollable modes**.
![[R7026R L3 s36 uncontrollable decomposition.png]]

**Not observable**, $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O} = n' < n$:
$$
\tilde A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & 0 \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \tilde C = [C_1\ \ 0]
$$
$$
\dot z_1 = A_{11}z_1 + B_1u, \qquad \dot z_2 = A_{21}z_1 + A_{22}z_2 + B_2u, \qquad y = C_1z_1
$$
$z_2$ never affects $y$: the **eigenvalues of $A_{22}$ are the unobservable modes**.
![[R7026R L3 s37 unobservable decomposition.png]]

### Stabilizability and detectability ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]])
> [!important] Definition 9
> - $(A, B)$ is **stabilizable** if all its uncontrollable modes are asymptotically stable.
> - $(A, C)$ is **detectable** if all its unobservable modes are asymptotically stable.

We can live with what we can't steer or see, **as long as it dies out by itself**.

| Example | Uncontrollable / unobservable mode | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Circuit with $R_1C_1 = R_2C_2$ | $v_3$, decays | stabilizable |
| CW, radial thrust only | the drift, $\lambda = 0$ | **not** stabilizable |
| CW, radial offset measured only | along-track offset, $\lambda = 0$ | **not** detectable |
| One axis, gyro only | angle offset, $\lambda = 0$ | **not** detectable |

State feedback and LQR need **stabilizability** (L4); observers and the Kalman filter need **detectability** (L5, L7).

### Kalman decomposition ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]])
Combining both splits gives **four kinds of states**: controllable & observable ($\Sigma_{co}$), controllable & not observable ($\Sigma_{c\bar o}$), not controllable & observable ($\Sigma_{\bar co}$), neither ($\Sigma_{\bar c\bar o}$).
![[R7026R L3 s39 kalman decomposition.png]]
- The TF doesn't depend on the basis ⇒ **the TF only accounts for $\Sigma_{co}$**.
- A TF without pole-zero cancellations → its state-space realization is fully controllable and observable.
- If a state-space system is not fully controllable or observable, its TF **has pole-zero cancellations** (hidden modes). This is why "BIBO stable = asymptotically stable" in L2 needed "no hidden modes".

### Canonical forms at a glance ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40]])

| Form | What it looks like | What we use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Controllable | companion $A$ (top row $-a_i$), $B = [1, 0, \dots, 0]^T$ | pole placement, Ackermann (L4); realizing a TF |
| Observable | $A_c^T$, $C = [1, 0, \dots, 0]$ | observer design by duality (L5) |
| Modal | $A$ diagonal (the eigenvalues) | decoupled modes $e^{\lambda_it}$; flexible spacecraft, one mode per bending shape |
| Jordan | blocks $\lambda I + N$, ones above the diagonal | repeated eigenvalues: a rigid axis $\frac{1}{Is^2}$ is one $2\times2$ Jordan block at 0 (the "rigid-body mode") |
| Decomposition | four blocks $co, c\bar o, \bar co, \bar c\bar o$ | what can never be stabilized or seen |

Same system, different coordinates: pick the form that makes the job easy.

### Modal form: read controllability off directly ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=41|s. 41]], backup [[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=68|s. 68–92]])
If $A$ is diagonalizable ($n$ independent eigenvectors), there is a $T$ such that
$$
\dot z = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \lambda_n \end{bmatrix}z + \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}u, \qquad y = [c_1\ \cdots\ c_n]\,z
$$
Each state obeys its own equation $\dot z_i = \lambda_iz_i + b_iu$: **one mode $e^{\lambda_it}$ per state**, and $e^{At} = \operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1t}, \dots, e^{\lambda_nt})$. The free output is $y(t) = \sum c_iz_i(0)e^{\lambda_it}$ → the $\lambda_i$ with $b_ic_i \neq 0$ are the poles of the TF.

With one input/output, the modal form is controllable and observable ⇔
- all $b_i \neq 0$ (the input reaches every mode),
- all $c_i \neq 0$ (every mode shows in $y$),
- all $\lambda_i$ **different** (two equal modes driven by the same $u$ move together).

![[R7026R L3 s41 solar array mode node.png]]
**Spacecraft:** a force at a **node** of mode $i$ (where its shape is zero) has $b_i = 0$: it can neither excite nor damp that mode. A sensor there has $c_i = 0$. → Place actuators and sensors **away from the nodes** of the modes you must control.

**Jordan form (backup):** if $A$ can't be diagonalized (repeated eigenvalues without enough eigenvectors), it can be brought to blocks $\lambda I + N$ with ones above the diagonal. Then $e^{At} = e^{\lambda t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & t & t^2/2! & \cdots \\ & 1 & t & \cdots \\ & & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$, which is where the $t^{k}e^{\lambda t}$ terms of L1 come from. For a single Jordan block: controllable ⇔ the **last** $b_n \neq 0$; observable ⇔ the **first** $c_1 \neq 0$.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Controllability**
$$
\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & \cdots & A^{n-1}B \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{controllable} \Leftrightarrow \operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = n
$$
**Observability**
$$
\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{observable} \Leftrightarrow \operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O} = n, \qquad \text{unobservable states} = \ker\mathcal{O}
$$
where $n$ = number of states.

**Change of coordinates** ($x = T\tilde x$)
$$
\tilde A = T^{-1}AT, \quad \tilde B = T^{-1}B, \quad \tilde C = CT, \quad \tilde D = D, \quad \tilde{\mathcal{C}} = T^{-1}\mathcal{C}
$$
**Duality**
$$
(A, B, C) \leftrightarrow (A^T, C^T, B^T), \qquad \mathcal{C}_{\Sigma_d} = \mathcal{O}_\Sigma^T
$$
**Canonical forms** for $G(s) = \frac{b_1s^{n-1} + \dots + b_n}{s^n + a_1s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n}$
$$
A_c = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & \cdots & -a_n \\ 1 & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \vdots \end{bmatrix},\ B_c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix},\ C_c = [b_1 \cdots b_n]; \qquad A_o = A_c^T,\ B_o = C_c^T,\ C_o = B_c^T
$$
**Control canonical transformation:** $t_n = [0 \cdots 0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}$, $t_i = t_nA^{n-i}$, $T^{-1}$ = rows $t_1, \dots, t_n$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_3_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]) Chaser near a target, Clohessy–Wiltshire in-plane with $x = [x, y, \dot x, \dot y]^T$. Is the system controllable with only a radial thruster, $B_r = [0, 0, 1, 0]^T$? Which state direction can't be reached, and is the system stabilizable?

**Solution:**
1. *Columns of $\mathcal{C}$* (multiply by $A$ each time; $A$ picks: row 1 = 3rd entry, row 2 = 4th entry, row 3 = $3n^2\cdot$1st $+ 2n\cdot$4th, row 4 = $-2n\cdot$3rd):
$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ -2n \end{bmatrix}, \quad
A^2B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2n \\ 3n^2 - 4n^2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2n \\ -n^2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
A^3B = \begin{bmatrix} -n^2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 2n^3 \end{bmatrix}
$$
2. *Rank:* $A^3B = -n^2 \cdot AB$, so the 4th column adds nothing. The first three are independent ⇒ $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{C} = 3 < 4$ ⇒ **not controllable**.
3. *What can't be reached:* the quantity $\dot y + 2nx$ (orbital energy). Check: for every column, $(\text{4th entry}) + 2n(\text{1st entry}) = 0$:
   $B$: $0 + 0 = 0$, $AB$: $-2n + 2n = 0$, $A^2B$: $0 + 0 = 0$, $A^3B$: $2n^3 - 2n^3 = 0$ ✓. So the input can never change $\dot y + 2nx$.
4. *Stabilizable?* The uncontrollable mode is the drift with $\lambda = 0$ (not asymptotically stable) ⇒ **not stabilizable**. A radial thruster alone can never stop an along-track drift; you need along-track thrust.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ controllable? Stabilizable?

> [!example]- Answer
> $AB = [-1, 0]^T$, $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, rank 1 ⇒ not controllable (the input never reaches the second state; in modal form $b_2 = 0$). The uncontrollable mode is $\lambda = -2$, stable ⇒ **stabilizable**.

- Q: Why can't a gyro alone give the attitude angle? Which state is invisible?

> [!example]- Answer
> $C = [0\ 1]$, $\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, rank 1. $\ker\mathcal{O}$ is spanned by $[1, 0]^T$: a constant angle offset produces zero output. Integrating the rate gives the angle only up to the unknown $\theta(0)$. Not detectable either ($\lambda = 0$).

- Q: What is the dual of $(A, B, C)$ and why is it useful?

> [!example]- Answer
> $(A^T, C^T, B^T)$. $\Sigma$ observable ⇔ $\Sigma_d$ controllable, so observer design (L5) can reuse every controller-design tool (pole placement, LQR) on the dual system.

- Q: A transfer function computed from $(A, B, C)$ has order 2 but $A$ is $3\times3$. What does that tell you?

> [!example]- Answer
> There is a pole-zero cancellation: one mode is uncontrollable and/or unobservable (a hidden mode). The TF only shows the controllable-and-observable part.

- Q: Where on a flexible solar array should you NOT put an actuator for mode 2?

> [!example]- Answer
> At a node of mode 2 (where the mode shape is zero). There $b_2 = 0$, so the actuator can neither excite nor damp that mode.
