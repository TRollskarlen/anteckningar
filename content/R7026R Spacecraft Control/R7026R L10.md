**Question:** Which sensors measure the attitude and which actuators change it, what can each of them *not* do, and how are their measurements turned into an attitude estimate (TRIAD, gyro + star tracker Kalman filter)?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L6]], [[R7026R L7]], [[R7026R L8]], [[R7026R L9]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L11]], [[R7026R L13]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Every sensor delivers truth + bias + noise: vector sensors (star tracker, Sun, Earth, magnetometer) each give one direction, and gyros give rates that drift when integrated, so the attitude always comes from combining sensors: TRIAD turns two directions into $[A] = [M_b][M_r]^T$ (most accurate vector first), and a Kalman filter lets the gyro drive the prediction while the star tracker corrects it and estimates the gyro bias as a state. Wheels only move momentum between wheel and body (so they saturate under a constant disturbance), while magnetorquers and thrusters change the total momentum and can dump it. A magnetorquer gives $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$, never along $\mathbf B$ (only two axes at a time), and the B-dot law $\mathbf M = -k\dot{\mathbf B}_b$ detumbles a satellite with a one-line Lyapunov proof.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **ADCS:** Attitude Determination and Control System. Determination estimates the attitude; control changes or holds it with actuators.
- **Passive vs active control:** passive uses nature (gravity gradient, magnetic field, drag): cheap, limited accuracy. Active uses sensors + actuators + computer: accurate, flexible, expensive.
- **Absolute vs rate sensors:** stars, Sun, Earth, magnetic field give *directions*; gyros give *rates*. Combine them for an accurate, continuous estimate.
- **Star tracker:** camera + star catalog → full attitude to arcseconds. Blinded by Sun, Moon, Earth.
- **Sun sensor:** photocell current $I \approx I_0\cos\alpha$ → Sun direction. Simple; nothing in eclipse. Used for Sun acquisition and safe mode.
- **Magnetometer:** measures $\mathbf B$; compared with a field model (IGRF). ~1°, coarse. Also needed for magnetorquer control.
- **Earth horizon sensor:** finds the Earth's limb → roll and pitch relative to nadir. **No yaw.**
- **Gyro model:** $\omega_m = \omega + b + v_g$, $\dot b = v_b$. The bias integrates to an angle error that grows linearly.
- **TRIAD:** Attitude from two vector measurements. Trusts the first vector fully.
- **Bias as a state:** The gyro bias is added to the filter state; it is observable through the star tracker.
- **Momentum exchange vs momentum change:** wheels exchange momentum with the body; magnetorquers and thrusters push against something outside and change the total.
- **Saturation / momentum dumping:** a constant disturbance spins the wheels up to their limit; an external torque must unload them.
- **B-dot:** $\mathbf M = -k\dot{\mathbf B}_b$; detumbling with only a magnetometer and magnetorquers.

### The ADCS loop ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–7]])
Attitude control tasks: point for a ΔV burn, keep a spin axis, hold Euler angles near zero (nadir pointing), track ground targets, point at celestial targets.
![[R7026R L10 s4 ADCS loop.png]]
*Target → control law → actuators → spacecraft dynamics (+ disturbance torques) → attitude → sensors → attitude determination → estimate fed back ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4]]).*

- **Passive vs active ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]):** a gravity-gradient satellite hangs its long axis toward the Earth by itself; an active one measures and corrects. Many missions mix the two.
- **Maneuvering vs nadir-pointing ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]):** maneuvering spacecraft slew often → strong, agile actuators. Nadir-pointing (comms, Earth observation) turn once per orbit → emphasis on stability and disturbance rejection.
- **Why determine attitude ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]):** pointing cameras/instruments, antennas, solar arrays to the Sun, radiators into shade (thermal), formation flying and rendezvous.

### Attitude sensors ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8–14]])
![[R7026R L10 s8 attitude sensors.png]]
*Typical sensor suite: star tracker, Sun sensor, magnetometer, Earth sensor, gyros inside the body ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]).*

**Key idea:** combine **absolute** sensors (stars, Sun, Earth, magnetic field) with **rate** sensors (gyros).

- **Star tracker ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]):** a camera images the star field, the processor matches the pattern to a catalog → full attitude (arcseconds to arcminutes). Stray-light shield, lens, CCD/CMOS, electronics. Used for precise pointing (EO, astronomy, laser comms), often paired with gyros.
- **Sun sensor ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]]):** a photocell's current falls with the Sun angle, $I(\alpha) \approx I_0\cos\alpha$. Two or more tilted cells → an angle. Coarse (wide FOV) and fine types. Low mass and power: ideal for initial Sun acquisition and safe mode.
- **Magnetometer ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):** measures the local field, compared with a model (IGRF). Coarse attitude, and needed for magnetorquer control and momentum dumping. Small, cheap, common on small satellites (usually on a boom, away from the spacecraft's own currents).
- **Gyros/IMUs ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]):** measure angular rate; IMU = gyros + accelerometers (+ magnetometers). Propagate the attitude between absolute fixes. **Main issue: drift.** Hubble: rate gyros for the fast motion, star trackers and fine guidance sensors to remove their drift.
- **Earth horizon sensor ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]):** detects the Earth's limb (IR or visible) → attitude relative to nadir.

![[R7026R L10 s13 horizon sensor scan.png]]
*A scanning sensor sweeps a cone that crosses the Earth twice per turn. The time on the Earth (pulse width $E$) gives roll $\phi = E - E_0$; the timing of the two crossings gives pitch $\theta = H_1 - H_2$ (Sidi Fig. B.2.3, [[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]).*

**Sensor fusion ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]):** no sensor is perfect (star trackers get obscured, Sun sensors give only one direction, magnetometers are coarse, gyros drift). Kalman filtering combines absolute fixes with high-rate gyro data. Designs trade accuracy, mass, power, cost and redundancy; small satellites often buy integrated ADCS modules.

### What a sensor really delivers ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]])
**measurement = true value + bias + noise**, at a limited rate, in a limited field of view.

| sensor | measures | typical accuracy | main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| star tracker | full attitude | arcseconds | blinded by Sun, Moon, Earth |
| fine Sun sensor | Sun direction | 0.01–0.1° | no Sun in eclipse |
| Earth sensor | nadir direction | 0.02–0.5° | noisy, **no yaw** |
| magnetometer | field direction | ~1° | field-model error |
| gyro | body rate | — | bias drifts, angle grows |

Orders of magnitude only (factor ~10 between cheap and expensive units). **Every vector sensor gives one direction; the gyro gives rates, never an attitude.**

### A noise model for the gyro ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]])
$$
\omega_m = \omega + b + v_g, \qquad \dot b = v_b
$$
- $v_g$: white noise on the rate → integrated it becomes the **angle random walk**.
- $b$: a slowly drifting bias, modeled as an integrator driven by white noise $v_b$ → the **bias random walk** (an L6 shaping filter).

![[R7026R L10 s16 gyro noise model.png]]
*Inside the gyro: white noise $v_b$ through $1/s$ gives the bias $b$; it and the white noise $v_g$ add to the true rate. Integrating $\omega_m$ gives the angle estimate ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]).*

> [!important] Question 1: integrate the gyro, $\hat\theta = \int\omega_m\,dt$. What happens?
> The bias integrates into an error that **grows linearly**: $b\,t$. With $b = 0.01°$/s (36°/h, a MEMS gyro): **36° after one hour**. So the bias must be **estimated as a state** (the shaping filter adds it to the state vector).

### One vector or two? ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]])
A Sun sensor can't fix all three angles alone: **any rotation about the Sun line leaves the reading unchanged**.
![[R7026R L10 s17 rotation about the sun line.png]]
*Rotating the spacecraft about the Sun line (from $Z_B$ to $Z_B'$) gives the same Sun reading ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]).*

A second, **non-parallel** direction is needed (magnetic field, Earth, a star). Two vectors fix the attitude; more vectors and a gyro, blended over time, improve it. That blending is the Kalman filter.

### TRIAD: attitude from two vectors ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18–19]])
Two directions known in the reference frame ($\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2$: Sun, field model) and measured in the body ($\mathbf b_1, \mathbf b_2$), with $\mathbf b_i = [A]\mathbf r_i$.
1. Build an orthonormal triad from each pair:
$$
\mathbf t_1 = \mathbf v_1, \qquad \mathbf t_2 = \frac{\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2}{|\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2|}, \qquad \mathbf t_3 = \mathbf t_1\times\mathbf t_2
$$
   giving $[M_b] = [\mathbf t_1^b\ \mathbf t_2^b\ \mathbf t_3^b]$ and $[M_r] = [\mathbf t_1^r\ \mathbf t_2^r\ \mathbf t_3^r]$ (columns).
2. They're the same physical axes, so $[M_b] = [A][M_r]$ ⇒
$$
[A] = [M_b][M_r]^T
$$
![[R7026R L10 s18 TRIAD triad.png]]
*$\mathbf t_1$ along the first vector, $\mathbf t_2$ normal to the plane of $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2$, $\mathbf t_3$ completes the triad ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]).*

- Exact for perfect measurements; always a proper rotation by construction.
- **The first vector is trusted completely**; the second only fixes the rotation *about* the first.

**Example ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]):** true $(\phi, \theta, \psi) = (10°, 20°, 30°)$, Sun $\mathbf r_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, field $\mathbf r_2 = (0, 0.6, 0.8)$. Perfect data → exact to $10^{-15}$°. Sun error 0.1°, magnetometer 1°: Sun first → 0.76° error; field first → 1.00°. **Put the more accurate vector first** (Question 3). Methods that weight all vectors by accuracy (Wahba's problem, q-method, QUEST) do better.

### From an attitude fix to the Kalman filter ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]])
- Between fixes the gyros **propagate** $\hat q^-$ with L8's $\dot q = \frac12[\Omega]q$, fed with gyro rates minus estimated biases $\hat{\mathbf b}$.
- A **fix** from TRIAD or a star tracker gives a measured attitude $[A_m]$.
- They differ by a small error DCM (L8), i.e. three small angles:
$$
[A_E] = [A_m][\hat A^-]^T \approx 1 - [\delta\boldsymbol\alpha\times], \qquad \mathbf y = \delta\boldsymbol\alpha = (\delta\phi, \delta\theta, \delta\psi) \approx 2(q_{1E}, q_{2E}, q_{3E})
$$
![[R7026R L10 s20 three-axis attitude filter.png]]
*Gyros → propagate $q$ → error angles against the star tracker/TRIAD fix → filter gain $L$ → correct $\hat q$ and $\hat b$ ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]).*

Each angle is the $y - \hat\theta^-$ of L7's single-axis filter, with one gyro bias per axis. At Earth-pointing rates the axes barely couple, so one axis is enough to study.

### Gyro + star tracker filter ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21–25]])
**Model** (single axis, $h = 0.1$ s, gyro reading as a known **input**):
$$
\begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ b \end{bmatrix}_{k+1} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A}\begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ b \end{bmatrix}_k + \begin{bmatrix} h \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\omega_{m,k} + \text{noise}, \qquad y = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{C}\begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ b \end{bmatrix} + v_2
$$
(Why $-h$: the true rate is $\omega_m - b$, so $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + h(\omega_m - b)$.)

> [!tip] Question 4: the tracker only measures the angle. Can the filter find the bias?
> $\begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -h \end{bmatrix}$, rank 2 → **observable** (L3). Physically: a bias makes the gyro-propagated angle drift away from the star tracker at a steady rate, and that drift reveals $b$. Without the tracker ($C = 0$) the bias is unobservable.

**The filter ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]):**

| Predict (every gyro sample, 10 Hz) | Update (every star tracker sample, 1 Hz) |
|---|---|
| $\hat\theta^- = \hat\theta + (\omega_m - \hat b)h$ | $L = P^-C^T(CP^-C^T + R_d)^{-1}$ |
| $\hat b^- = \hat b$ | $\hat x = \hat x^- + L(y - \hat\theta^-)$ |
| $P^- = APA^T + Q_d$ | $P = (1 - LC)P^-$ |

![[R7026R L10 s22 gyro star tracker kalman filter.png]]

> [!important] The gyro is not a measurement here
> It is the **input** that drives the prediction. Only the star tracker is a measurement. This is how most attitude filters are built.

**Noise covariances ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]):** angle random walk $10^{-5}$ rad/√s, bias random walk $10^{-7}$ rad/s/√s, so $R_g = 10^{-10}$ rad²/s, $R_b = 10^{-14}$ rad²/s³. Discretized exactly (L7):
$$
Q_d = \begin{bmatrix} R_gh + R_bh^3/3 & -R_bh^2/2 \\ -R_bh^2/2 & R_bh \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0\cdot10^{-11} & -5.0\cdot10^{-17} \\ -5.0\cdot10^{-17} & 1.0\cdot10^{-15} \end{bmatrix}
$$
Tracker noise is given per sample: $R_d = (5\cdot10^{-5})^2 = 2.5\cdot10^{-9}$ rad² (10 arcsec). Per gyro step only $\sqrt{Q_{d,11}} = 0.65$ arcsec of angle noise vs 10 arcsec per fix → **between fixes the filter trusts the gyro**.

**Results ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]):**
![[R7026R L10 s24 gyro star tracker results.png]]
*(a) gyro alone: ~36° error after an hour. (b) the filter: 4.6 arcsec rms, better than the tracker alone because the gyro smooths between fixes. (c) the bias is found within minutes and tracked as it wanders ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]).*

**Star tracker blinded, minutes 30–40 ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]):**
![[R7026R L10 s25 star tracker blinded.png]]
*The filter keeps propagating with the gyro minus the estimated bias. The error grows slowly (max 291 arcsec), and the filter knows it: $\sigma$ grows from 4.5 to 223 arcsec. At the first new fix the error collapses ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]).*

Without the bias estimate the same 10 minutes would cost $0.01°/\text{s}\times600\ \text{s} = 6°$. **Estimating the bias is what lets a spacecraft ride through sensor outages.**

### Actuators ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27–31]])
Purpose: rotate, reject disturbances, hold pointing, dump momentum.
- **Reaction wheels ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28]]):** electrically driven flywheels; changing wheel speed torques the body. Fine pointing (arcseconds). Most common on EO and science missions.
- **Control moment gyros ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]):** a spinning wheel is *gimballed*; turning its momentum vector gives a much larger torque. Agile slewing; ISS, large EO platforms. Steering laws and singularities: L13.
- **Thrusters ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30–31]]):** jets offset from the CM, $T = Fd$; a pair fired together gives a pure torque. Hydrazine on large spacecraft, cold gas on CubeSats. Momentum unloading and large slews. Thrust and efficiency:
$$
F = V_e\frac{dm}{dt} + A_e(P_e - P_a) = V_{ef}\frac{dm}{dt}, \qquad I_{sp} = \frac{F}{g_0\,dm/dt}\ [\text{s}]
$$
  Higher $I_{sp}$ → less fuel for the same impulse.
- **Magnetorquers ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32]]):** a coil with dipole $\mathbf M$ in the Earth's field: $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$. No fuel. Coarse control and wheel momentum dumping. The dipole grows with current, then saturates.

### Why more than one kind of actuator? ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33–34]])
**Question 6:** a constant disturbance acts on a wheel-only spacecraft. The wheels can only **exchange** momentum with the body, never remove it. To cancel the disturbance they keep accelerating until they hit max speed: **saturation**, and the disturbance wins.
![[R7026R L10 s33 wheel saturation.png]]
*Wheel momentum under a constant disturbance: it ramps up to $h_{max}$ and then the disturbance wins (red). With momentum dumping (blue) an external torque unloads it periodically ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]).*

**Actuator models for design ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]):**
- **Reaction wheel:** $T = -\dot h_w$, limited $|T| \le T_{max}$ and $|h_w| \le h_{max}$.
- **Magnetorquer:** $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$, $|M_i| \le M_{max}$, no torque along $\mathbf B$.
- **Thrusters:** on/off, a minimum impulse bit, finite fuel; external torque (and force).

![[R7026R L10 s34 wheel torque limit.png]]
*A real wheel: $T_{max} = 0.2$ N m at low momentum, falling at high momentum where the 80 W power limit is reached (Sidi Fig. C.4.3, [[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]).*

> [!important] Question 7: which actuators can change the **total** angular momentum?
> Only **magnetorquers and thrusters**: they push against something outside (the Earth's field, ejected mass). Wheels only move momentum between wheel and body. That's why every wheel-controlled spacecraft also carries one of the other two.

### Magnetorquer torque ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35–36]])
$$
\begin{bmatrix} T_{cx} \\ T_{cy} \\ T_{cz} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & B_z & -B_y \\ -B_z & 0 & B_x \\ B_y & -B_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} M_x \\ M_y \\ M_z \end{bmatrix}
$$
The torque is always perpendicular to both $\mathbf M$ and $\mathbf B$. **The matrix is singular** → you can't control all three axes at once magnetically.

![[R7026R L10 s36 no torque along B.png]]
*A dipole parallel to the field gives $\mathbf T = 0$ (null space); every possible torque lies in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf B$ ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]).*

Why singular (Question 8): $\mathbf M \parallel \mathbf B$ ⇒ $\mathbf T = 0$, a null space, so $\det = 0$. Equivalently, every 3×3 skew-symmetric matrix is singular. At any instant only **two** torque directions are available; which two changes as $\mathbf B$ turns along the orbit.

### Two-axis magnetic damping of a GG satellite ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37–38]])
Since no torque along $\mathbf B$: control two axes, let gravity gradient hold the third. For a nadir-pointing GG satellite in an equatorial orbit ($\mathbf B$ along $\mp Y_B$):
- $Y_B$ (pitch) stabilized passively by gravity gradient,
- $X_B$ and $Z_B$ damped with magnetorquers along $X_B$ and $Z_B$:
$$
M_z = -\frac{T_{cx}}{B_y}, \qquad M_x = \frac{T_{cz}}{B_y}
$$
(From the matrix with $B_x = B_z = 0$: $T_{cx} = -B_yM_z$, $T_{cz} = B_yM_x$.) Needs a 3-axis magnetometer (for $B_y$) and rate measurements (for $T_{cx}, T_{cz}$).

![[R7026R L10 s37 two-axis magnetic damping.png]]

**How strong must it be? (Question 9, [[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]])** $T_{dz} = 10^{-5}$ N m at 800 km. Dipole model: $B \approx B_0(R_E/r)^3 = 3.0\cdot10^{-5}(6378/7178)^3 \approx 2.1\cdot10^{-5}$ T. In an equatorial orbit $\mathbf B$ lies along the orbit normal, so $|M_x| = 10^{-5}/2.1\cdot10^{-5} \approx 0.5$ A m².
![[R7026R L10 s38 dipole field and orbits.png]]
*Dipole field lines. Equatorial orbit (blue, edge-on): $\mathbf B$ crosses the orbit plane. Polar orbit (red dashed): $\mathbf B$ lies almost in the orbit plane ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]]).*

**Question 10:** in a near-polar orbit $B_y$ is small and $M = T/B_y$ grows without bound. This scheme needs a field with a healthy component along $Y_B$.

### B-dot detumbling ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39–41]])
After separation a small satellite tumbles at several °/s. The first controller uses only a magnetometer and three magnetorquers:
$$
\mathbf M = -k\,\dot{\mathbf B}_b
$$
$\dot{\mathbf B}_b$ = derivative of the field measured in body axes. For a fast tumble the field barely moves in inertial space, so $\dot{\mathbf B}_b \approx -\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B_b$ ⇒ $\mathbf M \approx k\,\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B_b$. **No attitude estimate, no gyro**: the field's rotation in the body frame *is* the rate information.
![[R7026R L10 s39 bdot detumbling.png]]

**Why it works: a Lyapunov function (L2, [[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40]]).** $V = \frac12\boldsymbol\omega^T[I]\boldsymbol\omega$ (kinetic energy). With $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$, $\mathbf M = k\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B$:
$$
\dot V = \boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf T = k\,\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\big((\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B\big) = -k|\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B|^2 \le 0
$$
(Use $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) = (\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c$ with $\mathbf a = \boldsymbol\omega$, $\mathbf b = \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B$, $\mathbf c = \mathbf B$: $\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf B = -|\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B|^2$.) Energy can only go down. $\dot V = 0$ only when $\boldsymbol\omega \parallel \mathbf B$, but $\mathbf B$ turns along the orbit, so every axis gets damped over time. The gyroscopic term $\boldsymbol\omega\times[I]\boldsymbol\omega$ doesn't even appear ($\boldsymbol\omega\cdot(\boldsymbol\omega\times\cdot) = 0$).

**Simulation ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=41|s. 41]]):** 3U CubeSat, $I = \operatorname{diag}(0.03, 0.03, 0.01)$ kg m², $M_{max} = 0.2$ A m², 25–50 µT dipole field, 95 min polar orbit, initial $|\omega| = 14°$/s.
![[R7026R L10 s41 bdot simulation.png]]
*$|\omega|$: 2.1°/s after half an orbit, 0.49°/s after one orbit. It settles near $2\omega_o \approx 0.13°$/s, because once the tumble is slow the field's own rotation along the orbit dominates $\dot{\mathbf B}_b$ ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=41|s. 41]]). Good enough to hand over to the fine-pointing controller (L13).*

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Gyro:** $\omega_m = \omega + b + v_g$, $\dot b = v_b$; integrated bias error $= b\,t$.

**TRIAD**
$$
\mathbf t_1 = \mathbf v_1, \quad \mathbf t_2 = \frac{\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2}{|\mathbf v_1\times\mathbf v_2|}, \quad \mathbf t_3 = \mathbf t_1\times\mathbf t_2, \qquad [A] = [M_b][M_r]^T
$$
**Gyro + star tracker filter** (state $[\theta\ b]^T$, gyro = input)
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} h \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad Q_d \approx \begin{bmatrix} R_gh & 0 \\ 0 & R_bh \end{bmatrix}, \quad R_d = \sigma_{tracker}^2
$$
**Attitude error as the measurement:** $[A_m][\hat A^-]^T \approx 1 - [\delta\boldsymbol\alpha\times]$, $\delta\boldsymbol\alpha \approx 2\mathbf q_E$.

**Actuators:** wheel $T = -\dot h_w$; thruster $T = Fd$, $I_{sp} = F/(g_0\dot m)$; magnetorquer $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$.

**Two-axis magnetic (equatorial, $\mathbf B \parallel Y_B$):** $M_z = -T_{cx}/B_y$, $M_x = T_{cz}/B_y$. Dipole field $B \approx B_0(R_E/r)^3$, $B_0 \approx 3\cdot10^{-5}$ T.

**B-dot:** $\mathbf M = -k\dot{\mathbf B}_b \approx k\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B_b$; $\dot V = -k|\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B|^2 \le 0$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_10_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]) The spacecraft is yawed by exactly 90° from the reference frame. The Sun is at $\mathbf r_1 = (1, 0, 0)$ and the field model gives $\mathbf r_2 = (0, 0.6, 0.8)$ in the reference frame. The sensors measure $\mathbf b_1 = (0, -1, 0)$ and $\mathbf b_2 = (0.6, 0, 0.8)$. Use TRIAD to find $[A]$, and check it.

**Solution:**
1. *Reference triad:*
   - $\mathbf t_1^r = (1, 0, 0)$
   - $\mathbf r_1\times\mathbf r_2 = (0\cdot0.8 - 0\cdot0.6,\ 0\cdot0 - 1\cdot0.8,\ 1\cdot0.6 - 0) = (0, -0.8, 0.6)$, length 1 → $\mathbf t_2^r = (0, -0.8, 0.6)$
   - $\mathbf t_3^r = \mathbf t_1^r\times\mathbf t_2^r = (0, -0.6, -0.8)$
2. *Body triad:*
   - $\mathbf t_1^b = (0, -1, 0)$
   - $\mathbf b_1\times\mathbf b_2 = ((-1)(0.8) - 0,\ 0 - 0,\ 0 - (-1)(0.6)) = (-0.8, 0, 0.6)$ → $\mathbf t_2^b$
   - $\mathbf t_3^b = \mathbf t_1^b\times\mathbf t_2^b = ((-1)(0.6) - 0,\ 0 - 0,\ 0 - (-1)(-0.8)) = (-0.6, 0, -0.8)$
3. *Matrices (triad vectors as columns):*
$$
[M_b] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -0.8 & -0.6 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.6 & -0.8 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad [M_r]^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0 & -0.6 & -0.8 \end{bmatrix}
$$
4. *Product:* row 1 of $[M_b]$ times the columns of $[M_r]^T$: $(0,\ 0.64 + 0.36,\ -0.48 + 0.48) = (0, 1, 0)$; row 2: $(-1, 0, 0)$; row 3: $(0,\ -0.48 + 0.48,\ 0.36 + 0.64) = (0, 0, 1)$.
$$
[A] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = [A_\psi]_{\psi = 90°} \ ✓
$$
5. *Check:* $[A]\mathbf r_1 = (0, -1, 0) = \mathbf b_1$ ✓, $[A]\mathbf r_2 = (0.6, 0, 0.8) = \mathbf b_2$ ✓.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: A gyro has a bias of 0.005°/s. How large is the attitude error after 20 minutes of pure gyro propagation?

> [!example]- Answer
> $0.005°/\text{s}\times1200\ \text{s} = 6°$ (plus the angle random walk). That's why the bias is estimated as a state.

- Q: Why can't an Earth horizon sensor alone give a full attitude?

> [!example]- Answer
> It measures only the nadir direction: one vector. Rotation about nadir (yaw) leaves it unchanged. A second direction (Sun, magnetic field, star) is needed.

- Q: In TRIAD, which vector should go first and why?

> [!example]- Answer
> The more accurate one. TRIAD uses the first vector exactly; the second only fixes the rotation about it, so errors in the first vector go straight into the attitude.

- Q: In the gyro + star tracker filter, is the gyro a measurement? Is the bias observable?

> [!example]- Answer
> No, the gyro reading is the known **input** that drives the prediction; the star tracker is the only measurement. The bias is observable: $\begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -h \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 2. A bias makes the propagated angle drift away from the tracker at a steady rate.

- Q: A constant disturbance acts on a spacecraft with only reaction wheels. What happens and what is the cure?

> [!example]- Answer
> The wheels absorb the disturbance momentum, speed up and finally saturate; then the disturbance wins. An external torque (magnetorquers or thrusters) must dump the stored momentum periodically.

- Q: Why can't magnetorquers control all three axes at the same instant? What is the usual workaround?

> [!example]- Answer
> $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$ is always perpendicular to $\mathbf B$: the matrix is skew-symmetric and singular, with $\mathbf M \parallel \mathbf B$ in its null space. Workarounds: let gravity gradient hold the third axis (two-axis scheme), or rely on $\mathbf B$ rotating along the orbit so all axes are reached over time.

- Q: Show that B-dot never increases the kinetic energy.

> [!example]- Answer
> $V = \frac12\boldsymbol\omega^T[I]\boldsymbol\omega$, $\dot V = \boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf T = \boldsymbol\omega\cdot(\mathbf M\times\mathbf B)$ with $\mathbf M = k\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B$: $\dot V = k\,\boldsymbol\omega\cdot((\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B) = -k|\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf B|^2 \le 0$.
