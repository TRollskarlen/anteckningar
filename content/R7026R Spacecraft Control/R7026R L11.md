**Question:** How does a gravity-gradient satellite actually move under initial errors and constant disturbances, why is "stable" (L9) not good enough, and how much can passive dampers, magnetorquers and an active wheel with LQR improve it?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L9]], [[R7026R L10]], [[R7026R L1]], [[R7026R L4]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L12]], [[R7026R L14]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
A passive gravity-gradient satellite answers a constant disturbance with a **bias plus an undamped oscillation**: in pitch $\theta_{ss} = T_{dy}/(3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z))$ swinging between 0 and $2\theta_{ss}$ at $\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$, in roll–yaw at two coupled frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2$; yaw is the weak axis (stiffness only $\omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)$), so 10 µN m can push it far outside the linear model. A passive wheel damper barely helps, since its root locus runs from the pole to a zero only 0.6 % away ($\zeta \le 0.003$, ~73 orbits to decay tenfold), and two magnetorquers damp roll and yaw but leak a periodic torque into pitch. An active pitch wheel with LQR gives $\zeta = 0.707$ and arcsecond errors, but needs a sensor, a computer, power, and momentum dumping because the wheel absorbs the constant disturbance.

## Roadmap
<!-- Read this first. ⭐ = core: you must be able to do it by hand (exam, projects). No star = understand the idea. (extra) = example or background, skim it. -->
**Why this lecture?** In L9, "stable" only meant "does not diverge". Here we look at how a gravity-gradient satellite actually moves under disturbances: a bias plus an oscillation that never dies out, and a yaw axis that can run away completely. Then we try to add damping: a passive damper (barely helps), magnetorquers (help roll and yaw), and an active wheel with LQR (precise, but it costs hardware and momentum management).

**The storyline**
1. ⭐ *The control problem*: each axis is a double integrator, and gravity gradient is a spring around it. That is P control without D, so damping must be added.
2. ⭐ *Passive pitch response*: three cases by the sign of $\sigma_y$. Under a constant torque, $\theta = C(1 - \cos\omega_\theta t)$: a bias plus an endless swing.
3. ⭐ *Roll–yaw response*: two modes $\omega_1, \omega_2$, offsets under constant torques, and the singular case $I_x = I_y$.
4. ⭐ *Example 1, the boom satellite*: roll stays within a few degrees, yaw runs off to "267°". Yaw is the weak axis.
5. *Passive dampers*: the types, and why yaw is hard to damp.
6. ⭐ *Wheel damper model*: the characteristic polynomial, the two limits of $D$, and why the bias does not change.
7. ⭐ *Why the boom matters*: without it the linear result (88.6°) really means "it tumbles". Check against the maximum gravity-gradient torque.
8. ⭐ *Wheel damper root locus*: pole and zero only 0.6 % apart, so $\zeta \le 0.003$.
9. ⭐ *How slow is that?* $\zeta\omega_\theta T_{0.1} = 2.3$ gives about 73 orbits.
10. *Active magnetic damping*: roll and yaw are held, but a periodic torque leaks into pitch, and it fails in polar orbits.
11. ⭐ *Active wheel + LQR*: $\zeta = 0.707$ and arcsecond errors, but the wheel momentum grows without limit, so it must be dumped.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Attitude plant = double integrator + spring:** each axis is $\frac{1}{Is^2}$, and gravity gradient closes a "spring" loop around it. P-control alone → oscillation; rate feedback (damping) is essential.
- **Biased harmonic oscillation:** constant torque switched on → $\theta(t) = C(1 - \cos\omega_\theta t)$: mean $C$, swinging between 0 and $2C$ for ever.
- **Pitch cases:** $I_x < I_z$ diverges, $I_x = I_z$ drifts (no restoring torque), $I_x > I_z$ oscillates undamped.
- **Roll–yaw modes:** $\Delta(s) = (s^2 + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + \omega_2^2)$; two undamped natural frequencies, both appear in $\phi$ and $\psi$.
- **Yaw is the weak axis:** no gravity-gradient yaw torque, only orbital coupling: stiffness $\omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)$, ~100× less than pitch.
- **Inertia augmentation:** a deployable boom raises $I_x - I_z$ → smaller bias, stability at all.
- **Passive dampers:** point-mass damper, spring-boom damper, hysteresis rods, boom-hinge damper, viscous wheel damper. All turn libration energy into heat.
- **Viscous wheel damper:** free wheel in fluid, torque $D(\dot\theta - \omega_w)$. $D$ is the root-locus gain; max damping limited by $I_w/I_y$.
- **$T_{0.1}$:** time for an oscillation to fall to 1/10: $\zeta\omega_\theta T_{0.1} = 2.3$.
- **Two-axis magnetic damping:** magnetorquers along $X_B$, $Z_B$ with PD laws; pitch left to gravity gradient. Fails where $B_y \approx 0$ (polar orbits); leaks $T_{mby} = -B_zM_x + B_xM_z$ into pitch.
- **Active wheel + LQR:** precise and well damped, but the wheel's momentum grows without limit under a constant disturbance → dumping needed.

### The control problem ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–6]])
- The attitude dynamics are three nonlinear 2nd-order ODEs; the design tools of L1–L7 need linear models → linearize for small angles (L9). Attitude errors don't have to be Euler angles: e.g. a Sun-pointing satellite can use projections of the Sun vector, which can be linearized too.
- The L9 equations ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]) have three kinds of terms: inertial coupling (products of inertia), gravity gradient, wheel torque/momentum. **This lecture: no bias, principal axes.**

**Each axis is a double integrator ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]):**
![[R7026R L11 s6 pitch plant with gravity-gradient spring.png]]
*Pitch: torque through $\frac{1}{I_ys}$ and $\frac1s$ to $\theta$; gravity gradient feeds back $3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta$, a spring ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]).*

- Control torques are needed: active (wheels, CMGs, thrusters, magnetorquers) or passive (gravity gradient).
- Second order ⇒ **damping (rate feedback) is essential**; removing a steady-state error may need **integral action**.

> [!tip] Question 1: close a double integrator with pure P control
> $Is^2 + K = 0$ ⇒ $s = \pm j\sqrt{K/I}$: it **oscillates forever**. Only rate feedback moves the poles left. Gravity gradient is exactly such a spring, so a passive GG satellite behaves like P control without D.

### Passive GG satellite: pitch time response ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7–12]])
No wheels, no control; constant disturbances (drag off the CM, solar pressure). Laplace transform of $T_{dy} = I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta$ with a constant torque ($T_{dy}/s$) and initial conditions:
$$
\theta(s) = \frac{T_{dy}}{I_y\,s\,(s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y)} + \frac{s\,\theta(0) + \dot\theta(0)}{s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y}, \qquad \sigma_y = \frac{I_x - I_z}{I_y}
$$
Three cases by the sign of $\sigma_y$:

| case | inertia | roots | behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| A ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=9\|s. 9]]) | $I_x < I_z$ (long along the velocity) | one real positive | $\theta$ diverges exponentially |
| B ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=10\|s. 10]]) | $I_x = I_z$ | $s^2 = 0$ | $\theta = \theta_0 + \dot\theta_0t + \frac{T_{dy}}{2I_y}t^2$: drifts, no restoring torque |
| C ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=11\|s. 11]]) | $I_x > I_z$ (long along the vertical) | $\pm j\omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$ | stable, oscillatory, **undamped** |

![[R7026R L11 s9 case A pitch diverges.png]]
*Case A: gravity gradient turns the long axis away from the flight direction toward the vertical; any small $\theta$ grows ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]).*

**Case C solution** (zero initial conditions, partial fractions of $\frac{1}{s(s^2 + a^2)} = \frac{1}{a^2}\left(\frac1s - \frac{s}{s^2 + a^2}\right)$):
$$
\theta(t) = C\left[1 - \cos(\omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}\,t)\right], \qquad C = \frac{T_{dy}}{3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)}
$$
![[R7026R L11 s12 biased pitch oscillation.png]]
*A constant pitch torque switched on at $t = 0$: the satellite swings between 0 and $2C$ around the new mean $C$, forever ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]).*

- Mean = the bias $C$ (where GG torque balances $T_{dy}$). Frequency $\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$.
- $C \propto \frac{1}{I_x - I_z}$: a larger inertia difference → smaller bias and swing.
- Undamped because there's no damping term. **A passive GG satellite needs extra damping.**

### Roll–yaw time response ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–17]])
Laplace transform of the coupled pair (L9), with initial conditions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(s^2 + 4\omega_o^2\sigma_x)\phi - s\omega_o(1 - \sigma_x)\psi &= \frac{T_{dx}(s)}{I_x} + s\phi_0 + \dot\phi_0 - \omega_o(1 - \sigma_x)\psi_0 \\
(s^2 + \omega_o^2\sigma_z)\psi + s\omega_o(1 - \sigma_z)\phi &= \frac{T_{dz}(s)}{I_z} + \omega_o(1 - \sigma_z)\phi_0 + s\psi_0 + \dot\psi_0
\end{aligned}
$$
**Singular case (Question 2, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]):** symmetric about nadir, $I_x = I_y$, long boom (small $I_z$). Pitch is fine ($I_x > I_z$), but $\sigma_z = 0$ and $\Delta(s)$ gains a factor $s^2$: **a double integrator in roll–yaw**. Turning a body that's symmetric about nadir changes nothing, so nothing pulls the yaw back.
![[R7026R L11 s14 symmetric about nadir.png]]

**GG-stable case ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15–16]]):** no $s^1$ or $s^3$ terms (no damping), so the determinant factors as
$$
\Delta(s) = (s^2 + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + \omega_2^2) = s^4 + (\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2)s^2 + \omega_1^2\omega_2^2 \overset{!}{=} s^4 + \omega_o^2(1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z)s^2 + 4\omega_o^4\sigma_x\sigma_z
$$
For constant disturbances (Cramer's rule):
$$
\phi(s) = \frac{T_{dx}(s^2 + \omega_o^2\sigma_z)}{I_x\,s(s^2 + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + \omega_2^2)} + \frac{T_{dz}\,\omega_o(1 - \sigma_x)}{I_z(s^2 + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + \omega_2^2)}
$$
$$
\psi(s) = \frac{-T_{dx}\,\omega_o(1 - \sigma_z)}{I_x(s^2 + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + \omega_2^2)} + \frac{T_{dz}(s^2 + 4\omega_o^2\sigma_x)}{I_z\,s(s^2 + \omega_1^2)(s^2 + \omega_2^2)}
$$
Both angles = a constant bias + two undamped harmonics at $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$.

**Practical rule ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]):** GG stable needs $I_y > I_x > I_z$ (region I) or $I_x > I_z > I_y$ (region III). In practice region I: a boom along nadir makes $I_z$ smallest, the largest inertia is about the orbit normal $Y_B$. Even then, motion is oscillatory and needs magnetic damping, hysteresis rods or active control.
![[R7026R L11 s17 region I boom configuration.png]]

### Example 1: a gravity-gradient satellite ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18–26]])
Small satellite $I = (6, 8, 4)$ kg m² deploys a boom with tip mass along $Z_B$ → $I = (80, 82, 4)$. Circular 800 km orbit, $\omega_o = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s (101 min). Dominant disturbance: aerodynamic torque about $Z_B$. Initial yaw error 5°.
![[R7026R L11 s18 boom deployment.png]]
*The boom raises $I_x$ and $I_y$ by 74 kg m² and leaves $I_z$ unchanged ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]).*

- **Stable? ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]])** $\sigma_x = 78/80 = 0.975$, $\sigma_z = 2/4 = 0.5$ → region I ✓.
- **Frequencies ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]):** $\omega_1 = 1.9784\,\omega_o = 0.002054$ rad/s ($T_1 \approx 3059$ s), $\omega_2 = 0.7058\,\omega_o = 0.000733$ rad/s ($T_2 \approx 8575$ s). One fast mode, one slow.
- **Pitch (Question 3, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]):** $\sigma_y = 0.927$, $\omega_\theta = 1.667\,\omega_o$ (≈ 60 min), $C = 2.33°$ for $T_{dy} = 10^{-5}$ N m: swings 0–4.7° once an hour, forever.

**Initial yaw error only ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22–23]]):** with the numbers,
$$
\phi(t) = \left[0.001849\sin(1.9784\,\omega_ot) - 0.005186\sin(0.7058\,\omega_ot)\right]\psi(0), \qquad \psi(t) \approx \cos(0.7058\,\omega_ot)\,\psi(0)
$$
Yaw is almost a pure slow oscillation; roll is a tiny mix of both modes.
![[R7026R L11 s23 response to initial yaw error.png]]
*Released with $\psi(0) = 5°$: yaw oscillates ±5° undamped; roll stays within ±0.04° (Sidi Fig. 5.3.2, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]).*

**Constant roll torque, $T_{dx} = 10^{-5}$ N m (Question 4, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]):** the fast mode dominates roll:
$$
\phi(s) \approx \frac{T_{dx}}{I_x\,s\,(s^2 + (1.9784\omega_o)^2)} \ \Rightarrow\ \phi(t) \approx 1.70\left[1 - \cos(1.9784\,\omega_ot)\right]\ \text{deg}
$$
Offset $T_{dx}/(4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)) = 1.7°$: roll has the full $4\omega_o^2$ stiffness. Held between 0 and 3.4°, but undamped; yaw follows only through the coupling (< 2°).
![[R7026R L11 s24 response to constant roll torque.png]]

**Constant yaw torque, same 10 µN m (Question 5, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]):** $I_z = 4$ is small, the slow mode dominates:
$$
\psi(s) \approx \frac{T_{dz}}{I_z\,s\,(s^2 + (0.7058\omega_o)^2)} \ \Rightarrow\ \psi(t) \approx 267\left[1 - \cos(0.7058\,\omega_ot)\right]\ \text{deg}
$$
**Far outside any linear model.** The yaw stiffness $\omega_o^2(I_y - I_x) = \omega_o^2\cdot2$ is tiny. As $\psi$ grows, the effective $I_x$ and $I_y$ swap and GG stability is lost.
![[R7026R L11 s25 response to constant yaw torque.png]]
*Full simulation under $T_{dz} = 10^{-5}$ N m: yaw runs away, pitch breaks down (Sidi Fig. 5.3.4, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]).*

> [!important] Key lessons from Example 1 ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]])
> The boom moved the satellite into the GG-stable region ($I_y \approx I_x \gg I_z$). Roll disturbances give bounded, moderate motion. Yaw disturbances can give huge angles (small $I_z$, almost no restoring torque about $Z_B$ near nadir). **Pure GG is not enough for yaw**: damping or active control is required. Pitch gets $3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)$, yaw only $\omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)$: about 100× less.

### Passive dampers ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28–29]])
![[R7026R L11 s28 passive damper types.png]]
*(1) point-mass damper, (2) spring-boom damper with a fluid damper at the tip, (3) hysteresis rod in the Earth's field. All three turn libration energy into heat ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28]]).*

1. **Point-mass damper:** mass–spring–dashpot inside the satellite.
2. **External spring–boom damper:** the boom sits on a compliant spring; a fluid damper at the tip. Hard to model (distributed spring).
3. **Magnetic hysteresis rods:** high-permeability rods; motion through the Earth's field causes hysteresis losses. $B \propto 1/R^3$ → only in low orbits.
4. **Boom articulation damper:** damping in the boom hinge; damps pitch and roll, not yaw.
5. **Viscous wheel damper:** a free wheel in viscous fluid; the slip between wheel and housing dissipates energy. Wheel along $Y_B$ → pitch damping; along $X_B$ → roll damping.

**Yaw damping is especially hard** for passive systems.

### Wheel damper: model ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30–33]])
Pitch equation + a damper wheel $I_w$ on $Y_B$:
$$
I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta + I_w\dot\omega_w = T_{dy}, \qquad I_w\dot\omega_w = D(\dot\theta - \omega_w)
$$
The fluid torque $D(\dot\theta - \omega_w)$ speeds the wheel up and, equal and opposite, brakes the body. $D$ = viscous damping coefficient, $\dot\theta - \omega_w$ = slip.

Characteristic polynomial (third order: one more state, the wheel speed):
$$
\Delta(s) = I_ws^3 + D\left(1 + \frac{I_w}{I_y}\right)s^2 + 3I_w\omega_o^2\sigma_ys + 3D\omega_o^2\sigma_y
$$
Only the $D$ terms can move the roots off the imaginary axis.

**Two limits of $D$ (Question 6, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]):**
- $D = 0$: $\Delta = I_ws(s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y)$: wheel spins freely, pitch undamped.
- $D \to \infty$: divide by $D$; one root → $-\infty$, the rest satisfy $(1 + I_w/I_y)s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y = 0$: wheel **locked** to the body, again undamped (slightly lower frequency, more inertia).
- **No damping at either end → the best $D$ is in between**: a root-locus problem.

**Steady state ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]):** $\theta_{ss} = \lim_{s\to0}\frac{sT_{dy}(sI_w + D)}{I_ys\,\Delta(s)} = \frac{T_{dy}}{3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)}$. **The damper changes the transient, not the bias.** Only more $I_x - I_z$ reduces the bias.

### Why the boom matters ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35–38]])
**Before the boom** ($I = (6, 8, 4)$), $T_{dy} = 10^{-5}$ N m: $\theta_{ss} = \frac{10^{-5}}{3(0.001038)^2\cdot2} = 88.6°$.

**Can we trust that? (Question 7, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]])** No: the model is linearized for small angles. The full GG pitch torque is $\frac32\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\sin2\theta$, at most $\frac32(0.001038)^2\cdot2 \approx 3.2\cdot10^{-6}$ N m < $T_{dy}$.
![[R7026R L11 s36 no pitch equilibrium.png]]
*The disturbance line (10) and the GG torque curve (max 3.2, in µN m) never cross: there is no pitch angle where the satellite can rest ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]).*

**Read the linear result as a verdict, not a prediction: this satellite tumbles.**

**After the boom** ($I_x - I_z = 76$): $\theta_{ss} = 2.33°$ ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]]). Boom augmentation is essential. But the motion is still undamped, swinging 0–4.7° once an hour ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]]) → **we need a damper**.

### Wheel damper: root locus ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39–44]])
Divide $\Delta(s)$ by $I_ws(s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y)$ to get the form $1 + D\cdot G(s) = 0$:
$$
1 + \frac{D\left(\frac{1}{I_w} + \frac{1}{I_y}\right)\left(s^2 + \frac{3\omega_o^2\sigma_y}{1 + I_w/I_y}\right)}{s(s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y)} = 0
$$
- Poles: $0$, $s_p = \pm j\omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$. Zeros: $s_z = \pm s_p/\sqrt{1 + I_w/I_y}$, $|s_z| < |s_p|$.
- **$D$ plays the role of the loop gain.** Each branch leaves $s_p$ ($D = 0$) and ends at $s_z$ ($D \to \infty$), bulging into the left half plane in between.

![[R7026R L11 s39 damper root locus sketch.png]]
*Sketch (not to scale): as $D$ grows, each branch leaves the pole $s_p$ and ends at the zero $s_z$; the third root runs to $-\infty$ ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]]).*

![[R7026R L11 s40 damper root locus.png]]
*The real root locus (Sidi Fig. 5.3.5): the loop between $\omega_p$ and $\omega_z$ is small, so the best damping angle $\arcsin\xi$ is small ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40]]).*

**Why so little damping? (Question 8, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=42|s. 41–42]])** $I_w = 1$, $I_y = 82$:
$$
\frac{|s_z|}{|s_p|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 1/82}} = 0.994
$$
Pole and zero only **0.6 % apart**: a branch that short can't swing far into the left half plane. $\zeta_{max} \approx 0.0030$ at $D \approx 0.002$ N m s. More damping needs a larger $I_w/I_y$: a heavier wheel → mass.

**Design trials ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43]])**, gain $K = D(1/I_w + 1/I_y)$:

| $10^3D$ | 2.0 | 1.0 | 0.75 | 1.0 | 2.0 | 3.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $I_w$ [kg m²] | 0.4 | 0.4 | 0.4 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| $10^3K$ | 5.02 | 2.51 | 1.88 | 1.01 | 2.02 | 3.04 |
| $10^3\zeta$ | 0.75 | 1.1 | 1.2 | 2.6 | 3.0 | 2.6 |

Best: $I_w = 1$, $D = 0.002$ → $\zeta = 0.0030$.

**Same thing in state space ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44]]),** $x = [\theta\ \dot\theta\ \omega_w]^T$:
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y & -D/I_y & D/I_y \\ 0 & D/I_w & -D/I_w \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(s1 - A)\,I_w = \Delta(s)
$$
![[R7026R L11 s44 damping ratio vs D.png]]
*Sweeping $D$ and computing `eig(A)`: $\zeta$ peaks at 0.0030 ($I_w = 1$, $D \approx 1.7\cdot10^{-3}$) and 0.0012 ($I_w = 0.4$). No damper reaches even 0.01 ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44]]).*

### How slow is that? ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=45|s. 45–48]])
Envelope $e^{-\zeta\omega_\theta t}$; to 1/10: $\zeta\omega_\theta T_{0.1} = \ln10 = 2.3$. In orbits:
$$
\frac{T_{0.1}}{T_{orb}} = \frac{2.3}{2\pi\zeta\sqrt{3\sigma_y}} = \frac{2.3}{2\pi\cdot0.003\cdot1.667} \approx 73
$$
(Question 9: $\omega_\theta = 1.73\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s, $T_{0.1} = 2.3/(0.003\cdot1.73\cdot10^{-3}) \approx 4.4\cdot10^5$ s ≈ **5 days** for one disturbance to fade.)
![[R7026R L11 s47 pitch with wheel damper.png]]
*Pitch with the best damper ($I_w = 1$, $D = 0.002$) in a full simulation: the oscillation fades only over many orbits (Sidi Fig. 5.3.6, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=47|s. 47]]).*

**Conclusions on passive wheel damping ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=48|s. 48]]):** (1) increasing $I_x - I_z$ is essential for the bias; (2) the damper helps the transient only, and only a little ($\zeta \approx 0.003$, tens of orbits); (3) for fast response add other passive damping or active control.

### Active magnetic damping (Example 2, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=49|s. 49–53]])
Same satellite, constant yaw disturbance $T_{dz} = 10^{-5}$ N m (which passive GG couldn't hold). Two magnetorquers along $X_B$ and $Z_B$, PD laws on the measured angles:
$$
T_{cx} = -(K_{px}\phi + K_{dx}\dot\phi), \qquad T_{cz} = -(K_{pz}\psi + K_{dz}\dot\psi)
$$
GG holds $Y_B$ passively. From $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$ with $M_y = 0$ (L10): $M_x = T_{cz}/B_y$, $M_z = -T_{cx}/B_y$, **only where $B_y \neq 0$**.
![[R7026R L11 s50 two magnetorquers.png]]

![[R7026R L11 s51 magnetic damping response.png]]
*Example 2 under $T_{dz} = 10^{-5}$ N m: yaw (top) is now held at a fraction of a degree, roll stays tiny, but pitch (middle) gets a periodic disturbance (Sidi Fig. 5.3.7, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=51|s. 51]]).*

**Limitations ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=53|s. 53]]):**
- $\mathbf B$ varies along the orbit, so the torques do too.
- An unwanted torque appears about $Y_B$: $T_{mby} = -B_zM_x + B_xM_z$. It's harmonic, so pitch's passive damping can't fully remove it.
- The law divides by $B_y$. In a polar orbit $\mathbf B$ lies in the orbit plane, $B_y \approx 0$, and it fails → needs a lower inclination.
- Still: roll and yaw are greatly improved.

### Active damping with a reaction wheel + LQR ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54–55]])
Pitch plant of Example 1 with a wheel torque (L4):
$$
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y & 0 \end{bmatrix}x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I_y \end{bmatrix}(T_c + T_{dy}), \qquad T_c = -Kx, \quad |T_c| \le 5\ \text{mN m}
$$
Bryson weights $\theta_{max} = 0.5°$, $T_{max} = 5$ mN m ⇒ $\omega_n = 0.084$ rad/s, $\zeta = 0.707$.
![[R7026R L11 s54 LQR pitch wheel results.png]]
*(a) passive GG: a 2° error swings around the 2.33° bias forever; active: flat. (b) active: below 0.01° within minutes, 3.6 arcsec steady error. (c) the wheel momentum grows linearly ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54]]).*

> [!important] What is the price? (Question 10, [[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=55|s. 55]])
> The active loop is >200× better damped, but it needs an attitude sensor, a computer, a wheel and power **for the whole mission**. The constant disturbance doesn't go away: the wheel absorbs it, **0.061 N m s per orbit, without limit**, until it saturates and must be unloaded with magnetorquers or thrusters (L10, L13).
> **Passive: cheap, robust, sloppy. Active: precise, but needs hardware, power and momentum management.** Choosing between them: L14.

Typical accuracy ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=56|s. 56]]): 10–30° with passive damping, 2–10° with magnetic damping; the active wheel above reaches arcseconds.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Pitch under a constant torque** ($\sigma_y = (I_x - I_z)/I_y$)
$$
\theta(t) = C\left[1 - \cos\omega_\theta t\right], \qquad C = \theta_{ss} = \frac{T_{dy}}{3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)}, \qquad \omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}
$$
**Roll offset:** $\phi_{ss} \approx \dfrac{T_{dx}}{4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)}$; **yaw offset:** $\psi_{ss} \approx \dfrac{T_{dz}}{I_z\omega_2^2}$ (slow mode).

**Roll–yaw modes:** $\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2 = \omega_o^2(1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z)$, $\omega_1^2\omega_2^2 = 4\omega_o^4\sigma_x\sigma_z$.

**Max GG pitch torque:** $\frac32\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)$ (at $\theta = 45°$).

**Wheel damper**
$$
\Delta(s) = I_ws^3 + D\left(1 + \tfrac{I_w}{I_y}\right)s^2 + 3I_w\omega_o^2\sigma_ys + 3D\omega_o^2\sigma_y, \qquad \frac{|s_z|}{|s_p|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + I_w/I_y}}
$$
**Decay time:** $\zeta\omega_\theta T_{0.1} = 2.3$.

**Magnetic two-axis:** $M_x = T_{cz}/B_y$, $M_z = -T_{cx}/B_y$; leak $T_{mby} = -B_zM_x + B_xM_z$.

**LQR on one axis (Bryson, double integrator):** $K_1 = T_{max}/\theta_{max}$, $K_2 = \sqrt{2K_1I}$ ⇒ $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_1/I}$, $\zeta = 0.707$. Wheel momentum growth $= T_d\cdot t$.

## Common mistakes
- **The final value theorem on an undamped satellite.** The passive response never settles, so the theorem does not apply (L1). $C = T_{dy}/(3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z))$ is the *mean* of the swing, not a final value. With damping it becomes the final value. Project 2, Problem 4 asks exactly this.
- **Peak = $C$.** From rest at 0 the angle swings between 0 and $2C$.
- **Believing a large linear offset.** The linear model assumes small angles. Compare $T_d$ with the largest torque gravity gradient can give, $\frac32\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)$ (at 45°). If $T_d$ is larger, there is no equilibrium: it tumbles.
- **Expecting a damper to reduce the bias.** Damping only shortens the transient. Only more stiffness ($I_x - I_z$, or a control gain) reduces the offset.
- **"More $D$ = more damping."** At $D \to \infty$ the wheel is locked to the body and the motion is undamped again. The best $D$ is in between (root locus).
- **2.3 or 4.6?** Down to 1/10 uses $\ln 10 = 2.3$. The 4.6 of L1 is for 1 % ($\ln 100$).
- **Two-axis magnetic damping in a polar orbit.** The law divides by $B_y$, which is close to 0 there. Check the inclination first.
- **Forgetting where the disturbance goes.** With an active wheel the constant torque does not disappear: $h_w$ grows as $T_d t$ until the wheel is unloaded.
- **Degrees in Bryson's rule.** $\theta_{max} = 0.5° = 0.008727$ rad.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_11_handout.pdf#page=45|s. 21, 45, 54]]) The Example 1 satellite ($I = (80, 82, 4)$ kg m², $\omega_o = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s) feels $T_{dy} = 10^{-5}$ N m. (a) Passive: bias, swing and period. (b) Best wheel damper ($\zeta = 0.003$): time to reduce the oscillation tenfold. (c) Active wheel with LQR and Bryson weights $\theta_{max} = 0.5°$, $T_{max} = 5$ mN m: gains, $\omega_n$, steady error, and wheel momentum after one orbit.

**Solution:**
1. *Passive:* $3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z) = 3\cdot1.0774\cdot10^{-6}\cdot76 = 2.457\cdot10^{-4}$ N m/rad.
   - $C = 10^{-5}/2.457\cdot10^{-4} = 0.0407$ rad $= 2.33°$; it swings between 0 and 4.66°.
   - $\sigma_y = 76/82 = 0.927$, $\omega_\theta = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}\sqrt{2.78} = 1.73\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s → period $2\pi/\omega_\theta = 3630$ s ≈ 60 min.
2. *Wheel damper:* $T_{0.1} = \dfrac{2.3}{0.003\cdot1.73\cdot10^{-3}} = 4.43\cdot10^5$ s ≈ 5.1 days $= 4.43\cdot10^5/6053 \approx 73$ orbits.
3. *LQR:*
   - $K_1 = T_{max}/\theta_{max} = 0.005/0.008727 = 0.573$ N m/rad
   - $K_2 = \sqrt{2K_1I_y} = \sqrt{2\cdot0.573\cdot82} = 9.69$ N m s/rad
   - $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_1/I_y} = \sqrt{0.573/82} = 0.0836$ rad/s, $\zeta = K_2/(2\sqrt{K_1I_y}) = 9.69/(2\cdot6.85) = 0.707$ ✓
   - Steady error: total stiffness $K_1 + 2.457\cdot10^{-4} \approx 0.5732$; $\theta_{ss} = 10^{-5}/0.5732 = 1.745\cdot10^{-5}$ rad $= 3.6$ arcsec ✓
   - Wheel momentum: the wheel cancels $T_{dy}$ continuously, so $h_w$ grows by $T_{dy}\cdot T_{orb} = 10^{-5}\cdot6053 = 0.061$ N m s per orbit ✓.
4. *Compare:* $\zeta$ 0.003 → 0.707 (>200×); bias 2.33° → 3.6″. The price is the hardware and momentum dumping.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: A satellite with $I_x = I_z$. What does a constant pitch torque do?

> [!example]- Answer
> No GG restoring torque ($\sigma_y = 0$, $s^2 = 0$): $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \dot\theta_0t + \frac{T_{dy}}{2I_y}t^2$, it drifts without bound.

- Q: Why is yaw the weakest axis of a gravity-gradient satellite?

> [!example]- Answer
> Gravity gradient gives no yaw torque ($G_z = 0$). The only yaw stiffness is the orbital coupling $\omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)$, and in region I $I_y \approx I_x$, so it's tiny (~100× less than pitch's $3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)$). Small $I_z$ makes it worse.

- Q: The linear model gives $\theta_{ss} = 88.6°$. What do you conclude?

> [!example]- Answer
> The linear model is invalid there. Check the full torque $\frac32\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\sin2\theta$: its max ($3.2\cdot10^{-6}$ N m) is below the disturbance ($10^{-5}$), so no equilibrium exists and the satellite tumbles.

- Q: Does a wheel damper reduce the steady-state pitch error? Why is its damping so small?

> [!example]- Answer
> No, $\theta_{ss} = T_{dy}/(3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z))$ is unchanged; it only affects the transient. The root locus runs from $s_p$ to $s_z = s_p/\sqrt{1 + I_w/I_y}$, which for $I_w/I_y = 1/82$ is only 0.6 % away, so the branch can't reach far into the left half plane ($\zeta \le 0.003$).

- Q: Two-axis magnetic damping: which axes are damped, what fails in a polar orbit, and what side effect appears?

> [!example]- Answer
> Roll and yaw (magnetorquers along $X_B$, $Z_B$); pitch is held by GG. The law divides by $B_y$; in a polar orbit $\mathbf B$ lies in the orbit plane so $B_y \approx 0$ and the dipoles blow up. Side effect: a periodic torque leaks into pitch, $T_{mby} = -B_zM_x + B_xM_z$.

- Q: An active pitch wheel holds 3.6 arcsec. Why might you still fly passive GG?

> [!example]- Answer
> The active loop needs a sensor, computer, wheel and power for the whole mission, and the wheel absorbs every constant disturbance (0.061 N m s per orbit here) so it must be unloaded by magnetorquers or thrusters. Passive GG is cheap and robust if 10–30° (or 2–10° with magnetic damping) is good enough.
