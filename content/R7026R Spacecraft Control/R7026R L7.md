**Question:** Given the noise models of L6, which observer gain $L$ gives the *best possible* estimate (Kalman filter), how does it run on a flight computer (discrete, time-varying, extended), and how do we combine it with LQ state feedback into one optimal controller (LQG)?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L4]], [[R7026R L5]], [[R7026R L6]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L8]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
The Kalman filter is the observer whose gain $L$ minimizes the estimation-error covariance $P$ for given noise intensities $R_1$ (process) and $R_2$ (measurement); $P$ solves a Riccati equation and $L = PC^TR_2^{-1}$, which automatically trades a fast estimate (large $L$) against noise amplification (small $L$). On a flight computer it runs as predict (with the model) → update (with the measurement), with time-varying $P$ and $L$ that handle outages and changing sensors; continuous noise is converted to per-sample covariances via $Q_d \approx NR_1N^Th$ (exactly by Van Loan) and $R_d = R_2/h$, and for nonlinear models the extended Kalman filter linearizes around the estimate (not guaranteed optimal). LQG = LQ state feedback $K$ + Kalman filter $L$, designed separately (separation principle); integral action comes from modeling a low-frequency disturbance, references from a reference model, and the closed-loop rms errors come straight from a discrete Lyapunov equation.

## Roadmap
<!-- Read this first. ⭐ = core: you must be able to do it by hand (exam, projects). No star = understand the idea. (extra) = example or background, skim it. -->
**Why this lecture?** L5 left a question open: how fast should the observer be? A fast one amplifies sensor noise, a slow one lets disturbances through. With the noise models of L6, the Kalman filter answers it *optimally*. Add the LQ state feedback of L4 and you get LQG: the best controller you can build from noisy measurements. This is Project 1.

**The storyline**
1. ⭐ *The observer with noise*: the error is driven by $Nv_1 - Lv_2$, so $L$ trades process noise against sensor noise.
2. *What "best" means*: the smallest error covariance $P$, in every direction at once.
3. ⭐ *The continuous Kalman filter*: $P$ from a Riccati equation and $L = PC^TR_2^{-1}$, "uncertainty ÷ sensor noise". It is LQR on the dual system, and its innovations are white.
4. ⭐ *The discrete Kalman filter*: the same in discrete time, written as a predictor.
5. ⭐ *From continuous to discrete noise*: $Q_d \approx NR_1N^Th$ (exact with Van Loan) and $R_d = R_2/h$. Learn the table that tells intensities from covariances.
6. ⭐ *The time-varying filter*: initialize → predict → update. The scalar case shows where $L = P^-/(P^- + R)$ comes from.
7. ⭐ *Gyro + star tracker*: estimate the gyro bias and get an angle better than either sensor alone.
8. *Extended Kalman filter*: linearize at the estimate. It works well but can diverge. *(extra)* Orbit determination from GPS.
9. ⭐ *LQG*: $K$ from the LQ Riccati equation, $L$ from the Kalman one, designed separately.
10. *Integral action and references*: claim a disturbance at $\omega = 0$ and the design grows an integrator. A reference enters through $N_r$.
11. *Discrete LQG on the flight computer*: read → update → act → predict.
12. ⭐ *Closed-loop covariance*: `dlyap` gives the rms of every signal without simulating. Scalar: $P = W/(1 - a^2)$.
13. *Tuning*: which weight to turn for which problem.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Kalman filter:** The optimal observer: the $L$ that makes the estimation-error covariance $P$ as small as possible.
- **Estimation-error covariance $P = E[\tilde x\tilde x^T]$:** How uncertain the estimate is. Diagonal = variances of each state's error.
- **Process noise $v_1$ (intensity $R_1$):** Unknown disturbances that move the state.
- **Measurement noise $v_2$ (intensity $R_2$):** Sensor noise on $y$.
- **Riccati equation (filter):** $AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T - PC^TR_2^{-1}CP = 0$. Gives $P$, then $L = PC^TR_2^{-1}$.
- **Innovations $\nu = y - C\hat x$:** The "surprise" in each measurement. White if the filter is optimal: a testable health check in flight.
- **Predict / update:** Predict with the model (uncertainty grows), update with the measurement (uncertainty shrinks).
- **$Q_d$, $R_d$:** Per-sample covariances of the sampled noise. $Q_d \approx NR_1N^Th$, $R_d = R_2/h$.
- **Extended Kalman filter (EKF):** For nonlinear models: propagate with $f$, linearize only for $P$ and $L$. Can diverge.
- **LQG:** LQ (optimal state feedback) + Gaussian (Kalman filter). Optimal output feedback.
- **Integral action via disturbance model:** Claim a disturbance at $\omega = 0$ ($1/(s + \delta)$) and the optimal design grows an integrator.
- **Discrete Lyapunov equation:** $P = A_{cl}PA_{cl}^T + GWG^T$. Gives the rms of every signal of the loop without simulation.

> [!info] Notation in this lecture ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3]])
> $K$ = state-feedback gain ($u = -K\hat x$), $L$ = observer/Kalman gain. Many books write $K$ for the Kalman gain! MATLAB: `lqr` returns $K$, `kalman` returns $L$. Noise intensities $R_1, R_2, R_{12}$; LQ weights $Q_1, Q_2$ and LQ Riccati solution $S$. (L4's $Q, R, P$ → here $Q_1, Q_2, S$, because $P$ is now the error covariance.)

### From L4–L6 to this lecture ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3–4]])
We have: LQR weights (L4), observers with gain by pole placement (L5), white noise, spectra and shaping filters (L6). Historical note ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4]]): Norbert Wiener solved filtering with integral equations and spectral factorization (1949); Rudolf Kalman with state-space formulas and Riccati equations (1960).

### The observer with noise ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5–6]])
Plant with process disturbance $v_1$ and measurement disturbance $v_2$:
$$
\dot x = Ax + Bu + Nv_1, \qquad y = Cx + Du + v_2
$$
Same observer as L5, $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x - Du)$. Now the error is
$$
\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x + Nv_1 - Lv_2
$$
![[R7026R L7 s6 observer with noise.png]]

**Why not make $L$ as large as possible? ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]])** Large $L$ → fast error decay and small effect of $v_1$, but **$v_2$ enters multiplied by $L$**. $L$ trades the two disturbances against each other, and that trade is exactly what the Kalman filter settles for us.

Recap ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]): $L$ can make the error converge if $(A, C)$ is **detectable**, and arbitrarily fast if **observable** (L3). Practitioners often just assume this and then the observer doesn't work. Test it!

### What "best" means ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8–9]])
With a constant $L$ and $R_{12} = 0$, the error spectrum is (L6: $\Phi_y = G\Phi_uG^*$)
$$
\Phi_{\tilde x}(\omega) = H(i\omega)\big(NR_1N^T + LR_2L^T\big)H^*(i\omega), \qquad H(i\omega) = (i\omega I - A + LC)^{-1}
$$
and the covariance is the area under it:
$$
P = E\big[\tilde x\tilde x^T\big] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\Phi_{\tilde x}(\omega)\,d\omega
$$
![[R7026R L7 s8 error spectrum area.png]]
The Kalman filter chooses $L$ to make $P$ as small as possible.

**$P$ is a matrix: what does "minimal" mean? ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]])** $P^\circ$ is minimal if $\alpha^TP^\circ\alpha \le \alpha^TP\alpha$ for **every** direction $\alpha$ and every other $L$ (i.e. $P - P^\circ$ is p.s.d.). $\alpha^TP\alpha$ = the error variance along $\alpha$.
![[R7026R L7 s9 error ellipses.png]]
*Angle and gyro-bias error ellipses: the Kalman filter's ellipse lies inside all others ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]). No combination of states is estimated worse than with any other $L$.*

### The Kalman filter, continuous time ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10–12]])
> [!important] Steady-state Kalman filter ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]])
> Process $\dot x = Ax + Bu + Nv_1$, $y = Cx + Du + v_2$, with $v = [v_1; v_2]$ white noise of intensity $\begin{bmatrix} R_1 & R_{12} \\ R_{12}^T & R_2 \end{bmatrix}$.
> Requirements: $(A, C)$ detectable (+ a stabilizability condition on the noise).
> The optimal estimate is the observer
> $$\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x - Du), \qquad L = (PC^T + NR_{12})R_2^{-1}$$
> where $P$ solves the **algebraic Riccati equation**
> $$AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T - (PC^T + NR_{12})R_2^{-1}(PC^T + NR_{12})^T = 0$$

With $R_{12} = 0$: $L = PC^TR_2^{-1}$. Read it as **"uncertainty in the estimate ÷ noise in the sensor"**: large $P$ (unsure) or small $R_2$ (good sensor) → large $L$ (trust the measurement).

> [!tip] Compare with LQR (L4)
> LQR: $A^TS + SA + Q - SBR^{-1}B^TS = 0$, $K = R^{-1}B^TS$.
> Kalman: $AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T - PC^TR_2^{-1}CP = 0$, $L = PC^TR_2^{-1}$.
> Same equation with $A \to A^T$, $B \to C^T$, $Q \to NR_1N^T$, $R \to R_2$: **duality** again (L3). The Kalman filter is LQR on the dual system.

**Properties ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):**
1. Optimal among **all linear filters** (not just observers).
2. If the noise is **Gaussian**: optimal among **all filters**.
3. The **innovations** $\nu = y - C\hat x - Du$ are **white noise**.
4. MATLAB: `icare` (or `lqe`, `kalman`).

Why is (3) useful? Because it's **testable in flight**: if the innovations aren't white, there's still structure in $y$ the filter isn't using → the model or the noise intensities are wrong.

**Non-stationary filter ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]):** the Riccati equation can also be dynamic, $\dot P = AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T - \dots$. Start with a **large $P(0)$** (we don't know $x(0)$), and $P$ converges to the steady-state solution.

### The discrete-time Kalman filter ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]])
$x(k+1) = Ax + Bu + Nv_1$, $y = Cx + Du + v_2$, with covariances (per sample) $R_1, R_2, R_{12}$:
$$
\hat x(k+1|k) = A\hat x(k|k-1) + Bu(k) + L\big(y(k) - C\hat x(k|k-1) - Du(k)\big)
$$
$$
L = (APC^T + NR_{12})(CPC^T + R_2)^{-1}
$$
$$
P = APA^T + NR_1N^T - (APC^T + NR_{12})(CPC^T + R_2)^{-1}(APC^T + NR_{12})^T
$$
$P$ = the covariance of the optimal prediction error $x(k) - \hat x(k|k-1)$. MATLAB: `idare`, `dlqe`, `kalmd`.

Notation: $\hat x(k|k-1)$ = estimate of $x(k)$ using measurements up to $k-1$ (a **prediction**); $\hat x(k|k)$ = after also using $y(k)$ (an **update**).

### From continuous noise to discrete noise ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15–18]])
**Process noise: $Q_d$ ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]).** Sampling $\dot x = Ax + Bu + Nv_1$ (intensity $R_1$) every $h$ gives $x(k+1) = A_dx(k) + B_du(k) + w(k)$ with
$$
w(k) = \int_0^h e^{A\tau}Nv_1\big((k+1)h - \tau\big)\,d\tau, \qquad Q_d = E\,ww^T = \int_0^h e^{A\tau}NR_1N^Te^{A^T\tau}d\tau \approx NR_1N^Th
$$
$w(k)$ = the noise collected in one sample interval; different intervals are independent → a white sequence.
![[R7026R L7 s15 process noise in one sample.png]]
*One axis, 40 realizations in one sample: the rate spreads like $\sqrt t$, the angle like $t^{3/2}$ ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]).*

**$Q_d$ grows like $h$, not $h^2$:** the noise is white, its pushes at different instants are independent, and their *variances* add (like a random walk).

**Van Loan's method ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]):** one matrix exponential gives both $A_d$ and $Q_d$ exactly:
$$
F = \exp\left(\begin{bmatrix} -A & NR_1N^T \\ 0 & A^T \end{bmatrix}h\right) = \begin{bmatrix} F_{11} & F_{12} \\ 0 & F_{22} \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; A_d = F_{22}^T, \quad Q_d = F_{22}^TF_{12}
$$
```matlab
n = size(A,1);
F  = expm([-A, N*R1*N'; zeros(n), A']*h);
Ad = F(n+1:end, n+1:end)';
Qd = Ad*F(1:n, n+1:end);
% or all at once: kalmd(sys, R1, R2, h)
```
For one axis: $Q_d = R_1\begin{bmatrix} h^3/3 & h^2/2 \\ h^2/2 & h \end{bmatrix}/I^2$. ($NR_1N^Th$ keeps only the corner $R_1h/I^2$.) One line of MATLAB: no reason to approximate.

**Measurement noise: $R_d = R_2/h$ ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]).** A star tracker averages its continuous noise over the sample:
$$
\bar v_2(k) = \frac1h\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}v_2(t)\,dt, \qquad R_d = E\,\bar v_2^2 = \frac{R_2}{h}
$$
Example: $R_2 = 20$ arcsec² s, $h = 0.2$ s → $R_d = 100$ arcsec², i.e. $\sigma = 10''$ per reading.

Sampling 4× faster doubles the noise on each reading. Does the filter get worse? **No:** $R_dh = R_2$ is unchanged: noisier readings, but more of them.

**Notation table ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]):**

| | process noise | measurement noise | cross |
|---|---|---|---|
| continuous (L6): **intensity** [u]² s | $R_1$ of $v_1(t)$ | $R_2$ of $v_2(t)$ | $R_{12}$ |
| sampled every $h$: **covariance** [u]² | $Q_d$ of $w(k)$ | $R_d = R_2/h$ | — |
| discrete theorems ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]) | $NR_1N^T$ | $R_2$ | $R_{12}$ |
| time-varying algorithm | $N_kQ_kN_k^T$ | $R_k$ | $S_k$ |

Same letters, different meaning: in the discrete theorems $R_1, R_2$ are **covariances per sample**, not intensities. A gyro datasheet's $0.01°/\sqrt{s}$ is $\sqrt R$.

### The time-varying (non-stationary) Kalman filter ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19–21]])
Everything may vary with time ($A_k, C_k, Q_k, R_k$), so the gain $L_k$ is recomputed every step. On a real spacecraft the gyro drives the prediction many times a second, the star tracker updates once a second, and not at all while the Sun is in its view → $C_k$ changes with time.

![[R7026R L7 s19 P sawtooth.png]]
*Angle uncertainty of the gyro + star tracker filter, started at 0.1° ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]): it grows at every gyro prediction, drops at every star-tracker update, and settles in a few seconds.*

> [!important] Kalman filter algorithm ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]), case $S_k = 0$
> 1. **Initialize:** $\hat x(0|0) = x_0$, $P(0|0) = P_0$.
> 2. **Predict** (with the model):
> $$\hat x(k+1|k) = A_k\hat x(k|k) + B_ku(k), \qquad P(k+1|k) = A_kP(k|k)A_k^T + N_kQ_kN_k^T$$
> 3. **Update** (with the measurement):
> $$L_{k+1} = P(k+1|k)C^T\big(CP(k+1|k)C^T + R_{k+1}\big)^{-1}$$
> $$\tilde y = y(k+1) - C\hat x(k+1|k) - Du, \qquad \hat x(k+1|k+1) = \hat x(k+1|k) + L_{k+1}\tilde y$$
> $$P(k+1|k+1) = P(k+1|k) - L_{k+1}CP(k+1|k)$$
> 4. $k = k + 1$, go to 2.

> [!tip] Plain words
> **Predict:** "Where should it be now, given where I thought it was and what I commanded?" The uncertainty grows ($+Q$) because the disturbances pushed it a bit.
> **Update:** "The sensor says something else. Move my estimate part of the way toward it." How far = $L$, decided by who's more trustworthy: my prediction ($P$) or the sensor ($R$). The uncertainty shrinks.

**Why this gain? One scalar update ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]).** Prediction $\hat x^-$ with error variance $P^-$, reading $y = x + v$ with $E\,v^2 = R$. Update $\hat x^+ = \hat x^- + L(y - \hat x^-)$. The new error is $(1 - L)(x - \hat x^-) - Lv$, so
$$
P^+(L) = (1 - L)^2P^- + L^2R
$$
Minimize: $\frac{dP^+}{dL} = -2(1 - L)P^- + 2LR = 0$ ⇒
$$
L = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}, \qquad P^+ = (1 - L)P^- = \frac{P^-R}{P^- + R}
$$
![[R7026R L7 s22 optimal scalar gain.png]]
*$P^- = 2$, $R = 1$: $L = 0$ keeps the prediction ($P^+ = 2$), $L = 1$ takes the reading ($P^+ = 1$), $L = 2/3$ gives $P^+ = 2/3$, **better than either** ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]).*

- $P^-$ large (poor prediction): $L \to 1$, trust the measurement.
- $R$ large (poor sensor): $L \to 0$, trust the prediction.

Note: $P^+ = \frac{P^-R}{P^- + R}$ is always **smaller than both** $P^-$ and $R$ (like resistors in parallel). Combining two uncertain sources always helps.

**Important notes ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]]):**
- In practice $R_k$, $Q_k$, $S_k$ are rarely known. Common assumption: $S_k = 0$.
- $x_0$ and $P_0$ strongly affect the initial behaviour.
- $P$ tells the quality of the estimate, **but only as the model predicts it**: $P$ is computed from $A, C, Q, R$ alone; the measurements never enter. That's why checking the innovations matters.

### Spacecraft example: gyro + star tracker ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27–30]])
A spacecraft must know its attitude angle $\theta$ to a few arcseconds, all the time. Two sensors:
- **Rate gyro:** reads the body rate every second with 0.01°/s noise, plus a bias $b$ that wanders slowly. Integrated alone → the angle **drifts without limit**.
- **Star tracker:** measures $\theta$ itself every second with 0.005° (18″) noise and **no drift**, but every reading is noisy, and the Sun can blind it.

(Hubble carries exactly this pair.) Find a filter better than either sensor alone, which also estimates $b$.

**Model (L6, sampled $h = 1$ s), $x = [\theta, b]^T$, gyro reading $\omega_m$ as input:**
$$
\theta(k+1) = \theta(k) + h(\omega_m - b) - hv_g, \qquad b(k+1) = b(k) + v_b, \qquad y = \theta + v_{st}
$$
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} h \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad N = \begin{bmatrix} -h & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = [1\ 0]
$$
Noise per sample: $\sigma_g = 0.01°/s$, $\sigma_b = 10^{-4}$ °/s per step, $\sigma_{st} = 0.005°$.

**Steady state (`idare`):**
- Predictor gain $L = [0.838, -0.0082]^T$: the angle follows the star tracker closely, the bias is corrected a little each step.
- Predicted errors: angle 0.011°, bias 0.0010°/s. **After each update the angle error is 0.0046°, better than the star tracker alone (0.005°).**
- Eigenvalues of $A - LC$: 0.17 and 0.990 ⇒ the bias estimate has a time constant of ~100 samples.

![[R7026R L7 s29 gyro bias estimate.png]]
*True bias 0.05°/s, start from $\hat b = 0$ ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]). Two samples are enough for a first guess (the bias is observable through the star tracker, L3); $P$ shrinks as information accumulates.*

**Continuous intensities ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30]]):** $R_g = \sigma_g^2h = 10^{-4}$ (°/s)² s (angle random walk $\sqrt{R_g} = 0.01°/\sqrt{\text{s}} = 0.6°/\sqrt{\text{hour}}$, the unit on gyro datasheets), $R_b = \sigma_b^2/h = 10^{-8}$ (rate random walk), $R_2 = \sigma_{st}^2h$. The gyro alone: angle error $\sqrt{R_gt + R_bt^3/3}$ = 0.12° after 100 s, 1.9° after 1000 s.

### Extended Kalman filter ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31–32]])
The Kalman filter assumes a linear model. For a nonlinear one,
$$
x(k+1) = f_k(x(k), u(k)) + Nv_1, \qquad y = g_k(x(k), u(k)) + v_2
$$
**Idea:** propagate the estimate with the nonlinear $f$ and $g$ themselves, and **linearize only to compute $P$ and $L$**:
- Predict: $\hat x(k+1|k) = f_k(\hat x(k|k), u(k))$.
- Innovation: $\tilde y = y(k+1) - g_{k+1}(\hat x(k+1|k), u)$.
- Covariance and gain: with the Jacobians (L2) at the **estimate**: $A_k = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\big|_{\hat x(k|k)}$, $C_{k+1} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\big|_{\hat x(k+1|k)}$.

**Not necessarily optimal ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32]]):** every Jacobian is evaluated at $\hat x$, not at the true $x$ (which we don't have).
![[R7026R L7 s32 ekf linearization.png]]
*The slope the EKF uses (at $\hat x$) vs the slope the true state needs (at $x$) ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32]]).*

A poor estimate → poor linearization → poorer estimate still. **The EKF can diverge, and $P$ won't warn you**: it's computed from the Jacobians, so it reports the accuracy of a model that has already gone wrong.

### Space example: orbit determination from GPS ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33–34]])
LEO satellite, $x = [r^T, v^T]^T \in \mathbb{R}^6$:
$$
\dot r = v, \qquad \dot v = -\mu\frac{r}{\|r\|^3} + a_d \quad (a_d: \text{drag}, J_2, \dots \text{ as process noise})
$$
GPS gives position fixes every 10 s with ~10 m noise: $y = r + v_2$, $C = [I\ 0]$. Nonlinear dynamics, linear measurement → EKF: predict by integrating the orbit, linearize with $A_k \approx I + h\begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ G(\hat r) & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $G(r) = \frac{\mu}{\|r\|^5}(3rr^T - \|r\|^2I)$ (the gravity gradient).

What we get:
- **The velocity is never measured, yet it's estimated** (like the lunar lander in L5, here at 7.6 km/s).
- Many fixes are averaged through the dynamics: far more accurate than differencing two positions.
- $P$ = the orbit accuracy operations needs (collision warnings, pass predictions).
- During a GPS outage the prediction continues and **$P$ grows**, telling us how much to trust it.
- Linearized around a circular orbit in the rotating frame, the error dynamics are the Clohessy–Wiltshire equations (L2).

![[R7026R L7 s34 gps outage.png]]

Drag is badly known in LEO: it shows up in the **innovations** (they stop being white) → increase the process noise $R_1$ on the velocity.

**Related variants ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35]]):** hybrid (continuous model, discrete measurements), sensor fusion (more sensors = more rows in $C$; the filter fuses them automatically), Kalman–Bucy (continuous time-varying), Unscented Kalman Filter (nonlinear).

### LQG: optimal control with noise ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37–39]])
![[R7026R L7 s37 lqg loop.png]]
*Attitude control: $z$ = the pointing error to keep small, $u$ = wheel torques, $y$ = gyro and star-tracker readings ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]]).*

Task: find $L$ and $K$ that minimize $\|z\|_{Q_1}^2 + \|u\|_{Q_2}^2$ for $\dot x = Ax + Bu + Nv_1$, $z = Mx$, $y = Cx + v_2$.

$L$ shapes the estimate and $K$ acts on it. **Must we design them together? No** (separation principle):

> [!important] LQG ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]])
> $$\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x - Du), \qquad u = -K\hat x$$
> $$L = (PC^T + NR_{12})R_2^{-1}, \qquad K = Q_2^{-1}B^TS$$
> $$AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T - (PC^T + NR_{12})R_2^{-1}(\dots)^T = 0 \quad \text{(filter: only } A, C, N, R)$$
> $$A^TS + SA + M^TQ_1M - SBQ_2^{-1}B^TS = 0 \quad \text{(control: only } A, B, M, Q)$$

**Properties ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]]):** optimal among all linear controllers; with Gaussian noise among all controllers. MATLAB: `icare`.

### Integral action ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40]], 48)
Integrating controller ⇔ sensitivity function $S(i\omega) \to 0$ at low frequencies (this $S$ is the sensitivity, not the Riccati solution). LQG makes $S(i\omega)$ small **where the process-disturbance spectrum is large**.

So to buy integral action, tell the design about a disturbance whose spectrum is **infinite at $\omega = 0$**: $\Phi_v = 1/\omega^2$, $G_v = 1/s$ (L6). E.g. sunlight on an off-center solar array → almost constant torque → without integral action, a steady pointing error.

Problem: $1/s$ is marginally stable and $u$ can't control it → the augmented system isn't stabilizable. **Solution:** use $G_v = \frac{1}{s + \delta}$ with a small $\delta > 0$ (discrete: $\frac{1}{z - 1 + \delta}$).

→ **We don't add an integrator by hand; we claim a disturbance at low frequency, and the optimal design grows one for us.**

### Reference signals ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=41|s. 41–44]], 49–50)
LQ makes $\|z\|$ small. To track $r$: define the controlled variable $\bar z = z - r = -e$ and treat $r$ as a **measured disturbance** generated by a model $\dot x_r = A_rx_r + B_rv_r$, $r = C_rx_r$ (augment the state).

**Piecewise constant reference** (point at target A, then B, then C) = integrated white noise ($\dot x_r = v_r$). With $u^*(r)$ = the constant input that holds $z = r$ in steady state, minimize $\|z - r\|_{Q_1}^2 + \|u - u^*(r)\|_{Q_2}^2$. Result:
$$
u = -K\hat x + N_rr, \qquad N_r = \big(M(BK - A)^{-1}B\big)^{-1} \quad \text{(discrete: } N_r = (M(I - A + BK)^{-1}B)^{-1})
$$
$K$ and $\hat x$ unchanged; $N_r$ is L4's $N$.

### Discrete LQG on the flight computer ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=45|s. 45–47]])
Discrete LQ: $K = (B^TSB + Q_2)^{-1}B^TSA$, $S = A^TSA + M^TQ_1M - A^TSB(B^TSB + Q_2)^{-1}B^TSA$. MATLAB: `idare`, `dlqr`.

If $u(k)$ may use $y(k)$ directly, the law becomes $u(k) = -K\hat x(k|k)$: **update the estimate with $y(k)$ first, then feed it back** ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=47|s. 47]]):

*One sample of the flight software: read $y(k)$ → update → act → predict. Steps 1–3 must take a small fraction of $h$; then the torque is held. This is what Project 1 implements.*

### Closed-loop covariance: rms without simulation ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=51|s. 51–53]])
A stable discrete loop driven by white noise $w$ (covariance $W$), $x(k+1) = A_{cl}x(k) + Gw(k)$. Since $x(k)$ only depends on earlier $w$:
$$
P(k+1) = A_{cl}P(k)A_{cl}^T + GWG^T \quad\xrightarrow{\text{stationary}}\quad P = A_{cl}PA_{cl}^T + GWG^T
$$
the **discrete Lyapunov equation**, `P = dlyap(Acl, G*W*G')`. The rms of $z = Mx$ is $\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(MPM^T)}$. (Continuous: $AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T = 0$, `lyap`, the same equation L2 used for stability.)

Scalar: $x(k+1) = ax(k) + w$ ⇒ $P = \frac{W}{1 - a^2}$. $a = 0.9$: $5.26W$; $a = 0.5$: $1.33W$. The noise pumps variance in; the loop drains the fraction $1 - a^2$ each sample. A slow loop ($a$ near 1) stores more.

**The discrete LQG loop ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=52|s. 52]]):** stack $x$ and the prediction error $\tilde x$: $A_{cl}$ is block-triangular with eigenvalues of $A_d - B_dK$ and $A_d - LC$ (separation again). `Pcl = dlyap(Acl, G*W*G')` gives the rms of every signal.

**Worked example ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=53|s. 53]]):** one axis, $I = 100$ kg m², white disturbance torque $\sqrt{R_1} = 0.2$ mN m $\sqrt s$, star tracker $R_2 = 20$ arcsec² s, $h = 0.2$ s, Bryson with $\theta_{max} = 10''$, $T_{max} = 10$ mN m.
- $Q_d$ by Van Loan; $R_d = R_2/h = 100$ arcsec² (10″ per reading).
- `dlqr`: $K = [168, 184]$; filter $L_\infty = [0.082, 0.018]^T$; eigenvalue magnitudes 0.82 (control), 0.96 (filter).
- `dlyap`: **rms $\theta = 3.43''$**, rms $T_c = 1.16$ mN m (an 11 h simulation gives 3.41″ and 1.16 mN m).

![[R7026R L7 s53 lqg closed loop simulation.png]]
*The true attitude stays **three times tighter** than one star-tracker reading: the filter averages many readings through the model ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=53|s. 53]]).*

### Optimal does not mean good: tuning ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54–56]])
LQ control is not magic: design is still iterative, but now over $Q_1, Q_2, R_1, R_2$ instead of PID gains or poles.
![[R7026R L7 s54 tuning loop.png]]

Why weights are better: their effect is easier to predict:

| Problem | Remedy: increase… |
|---|---|
| A controlled variable varies too much (simulation) | its diagonal element of $Q_1$ |
| A control signal is too active | its diagonal element of $Q_2$ |
| Too sensitive to process disturbances (some frequencies) | the process-disturbance spectrum there (model and/or $R_1$) |
| Too sensitive to measurement noise (some frequencies) | the measurement-noise spectrum there (model and/or $R_2$) |
| Loop (nearly) unstable with oscillation (model error) | the measurement-noise spectrum at that frequency ($R_2$) → better robustness |

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Kalman filter, continuous** ($R_{12} = 0$)
$$
\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x), \quad L = PC^TR_2^{-1}, \quad AP + PA^T + NR_1N^T - PC^TR_2^{-1}CP = 0
$$
where $P$ = error covariance, $R_1$ = process-noise intensity, $R_2$ = measurement-noise intensity.

**Kalman algorithm, discrete**
$$
\text{predict: } \hat x^- = A\hat x + Bu, \quad P^- = APA^T + NQN^T
$$
$$
\text{update: } L = P^-C^T(CP^-C^T + R)^{-1}, \quad \hat x^+ = \hat x^- + L(y - C\hat x^-), \quad P^+ = (I - LC)P^-
$$
**Scalar intuition:** $L = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R}$, $P^+ = \frac{P^-R}{P^- + R}$.

**Sampled noise**
$$
Q_d = \int_0^h e^{A\tau}NR_1N^Te^{A^T\tau}d\tau \approx NR_1N^Th, \qquad R_d = \frac{R_2}{h}
$$
**LQG**
$$
u = -K\hat x, \quad K = Q_2^{-1}B^TS, \quad A^TS + SA + M^TQ_1M - SBQ_2^{-1}B^TS = 0
$$
**Closed-loop covariance**
$$
P = A_{cl}PA_{cl}^T + GWG^T \ (\texttt{dlyap}), \qquad \text{scalar: } P = \frac{W}{1 - a^2}
$$
**MATLAB:** `kalman`, `lqe`, `dlqe`, `kalmd`, `icare`, `idare`, `lqr`, `dlqr`, `dlyap`.

## Common mistakes
- **$K$ or $L$?** In this course $K$ is the state-feedback gain and $L$ the Kalman gain. Many books (and websites) call the Kalman gain $K$.
- **Intensity or covariance?** Continuous $R_1, R_2$ are intensities (unit² s). Per-sample $Q_d$, $R_d$ are covariances (unit²). In the discrete theorems the letters $R_1, R_2$ mean per-sample covariances. Check the units before you insert a number.
- **Multiply or divide by $h$?** Process noise: $Q_d \approx NR_1N^T\,h$ (multiply). Measurement noise: $R_d = R_2/h$ (divide). Sampling faster gives noisier readings but not a worse filter.
- **Predictor gain or update gain?** The steady-state theorem gives the *predictor* gain. The algorithm's $L_{k+1}$ is the *update* gain, $L_\infty$. They are related by $L_{\text{pred}} = A_dL_\infty$ (Project 1 asks for both).
- **Using the wrong $P$.** Predict from $P(k|k)$, compute the gain from $P(k+1|k)$, and update with $P(k+1|k+1) = (I - LC)\,P(k+1|k)$.
- **An update that makes things worse.** After an update, $P^+$ must be smaller than both $P^-$ and $R$ (scalar case). If it is not, there is an arithmetic error.
- **Trusting $P$ blindly.** $P$ is computed from the model alone and never sees a measurement. If the model is wrong, $P$ is wrong too. Check that the innovations are white.
- **Integral action with an exact $1/s$.** The augmented system is then not stabilizable and the LQ design fails. Use $1/(s + \delta)$ with a small $\delta > 0$.
- **Gyro datasheet units.** The angle random walk in °/√hour is $\sqrt{R_g}$, an intensity. To get a variance per sample you still multiply by $h$ (in the right time unit).

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23–25]]) One spacecraft axis holds its angle between samples, but a random disturbance torque nudges it each step; a Sun sensor measures it noisily:
$$
x(k+1) = x(k) + v_1(k), \qquad y(k) = x(k) + v_2(k), \qquad Q = R = 1\ \text{deg}^2, \quad P(0|0) = 1\ \text{deg}^2
$$
Run the Kalman recursion for three steps and find where the gain settles. Does the filter lean on the prediction or on the measurement?

**Solution:**
1. *The algorithm here* ($A = C = N = 1$):
$$
P(k+1|k) = P(k|k) + Q, \qquad L_{k+1} = \frac{P(k+1|k)}{P(k+1|k) + R}, \qquad P(k+1|k+1) = (1 - L_{k+1})P(k+1|k)
$$
2. *Turn the handle:*

| $k$ | $P(k+1\lvert k)$ | $L_{k+1}$ | $P(k+1\lvert k+1)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $1 + 1 = 2$ | $\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$ | $\frac13\cdot2 = \frac23$ |
| 1 | $\frac23 + 1 = \frac53$ | $\frac{5/3}{8/3} = \frac58 = 0.625$ | $\frac38\cdot\frac53 = \frac58$ |
| 2 | $\frac58 + 1 = \frac{13}{8}$ | $\frac{13/8}{21/8} = \frac{13}{21} \approx 0.619$ | $\frac{8}{21}\cdot\frac{13}{8} = \frac{13}{21}$ |

   The gains are ratios of Fibonacci numbers: $\frac23, \frac58, \frac{13}{21}, \dots$

![[R7026R L7 s24 gain convergence.png]]
*The gain settles after about three steps ([[Lecture_7_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]).*
3. *Steady state:* $P(k+1|k+1) = P(k|k) = p$:
$$
p = \frac{(p + 1)\cdot1}{(p + 1) + 1} = \frac{p + 1}{p + 2} \;\Rightarrow\; p^2 + p - 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; p = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2} = 0.618\ \text{deg}^2
$$
$$
L_\infty = \frac{p + 1}{p + 2} = \frac{1.618}{2.618} = 0.618
$$
   (The golden ratio! And $P(k+1|k) = p + Q = 1.618$ solves the Riccati equation $P = P + 1 - \frac{P^2}{P + 1}$, i.e. $P^2 - P - 1 = 0$.)
4. *Interpretation:* the disturbance and sensor are equally noisy ($Q = R$), yet $L_\infty = 0.618 > 0.5$: the filter leans **a little toward the measurement**. Because the prediction carries the new disturbance $Q$ *plus* the leftover uncertainty $p$, while the measurement only carries $R$.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Why not just choose a very large observer gain $L$?

> [!example]- Answer
> The error dynamics are $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x + Nv_1 - Lv_2$: large $L$ makes the error decay fast and suppresses $v_1$, but multiplies the measurement noise $v_2$. The Kalman filter picks the $L$ that best balances the two.

- Q: Scalar update: prediction variance $P^- = 4$, sensor variance $R = 1$. Find $L$ and $P^+$.

> [!example]- Answer
> $L = 4/(4 + 1) = 0.8$, $P^+ = 4\cdot1/(4 + 1) = 0.8$. Smaller than both 4 and 1: combining always helps.

- Q: A star tracker has $R_2 = 50$ arcsec² s and is sampled at $h = 0.5$ s. What is the noise per reading? What if you sample at 0.125 s?

> [!example]- Answer
> $R_d = 50/0.5 = 100$ arcsec², $\sigma = 10''$. At 0.125 s: $R_d = 400$, $\sigma = 20''$ per reading, but 4× more readings, so the filter does equally well ($R_dh$ unchanged).

- Q: Why can the EKF diverge without $P$ showing it?

> [!example]- Answer
> The Jacobians are evaluated at the estimate, not the true state. A bad estimate gives a bad linearization, and $P$ is computed from those same Jacobians, so it reports the accuracy of the wrong model. Check the innovations instead.

- Q: How do you get integral action in an LQG design?

> [!example]- Answer
> Augment the model with a low-frequency disturbance, $G_v = 1/(s + \delta)$ with small $\delta$ (not exactly $1/s$, which isn't stabilizable). The optimal controller then makes the sensitivity small at low frequency, i.e. it contains (almost) an integrator.

- Q: A scalar discrete loop $x(k+1) = 0.8x(k) + w$, $W = 1$. Stationary variance?

> [!example]- Answer
> $P = W/(1 - a^2) = 1/(1 - 0.64) = 2.78$.
