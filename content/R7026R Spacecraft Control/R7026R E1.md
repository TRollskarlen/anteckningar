**Question:** How far can weak magnetorquers go (three-axis control with one wheel, control over an orbit, wheel unloading), and how do you get the fastest possible slew out of a torque-limited actuator without chatter?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L10]], [[R7026R L13]], [[R7026R L2]], [[R7026R L3]], [[R7026R L4]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R E2]], [[R7026R L14]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Magnetorquers are cheap and fuel-free, but weak ($\propto 1/R^3$, useless at GEO) and never give torque along $\mathbf B$: two rods on $X_B$, $Z_B$ plus a pitch wheel give full three-axis control (scale all commands together when one saturates), rods alone are uncontrollable at every instant but can be controllable over an orbit (time-varying Gramian), and unloading with $\mathbf M = k(\Delta\mathbf h\times\mathbf B)/B^2$ is Lyapunov-stable with the gain chosen on the orbit-averaged model ($\Delta h_{ss} \approx T_d/(k\lambda_y)$). The fastest rest-to-rest slew with $|T| \le T_{max}$ is bang-bang with one switch, $t_{min} = 2\sqrt{\theta_f/u}$; linear laws (PD, LQR) can't use the full torque and take ~3× longer. In practice a PD takes over near the target, and an advanced switching curve (for delays and torque uncertainty) removes chatter, which matters most with on/off thrusters.

**Why this lecture?** An *optional* lecture: the study guide says whether E1 and E2 are given is decided during the course, so check before you spend exam time on it. It is about getting the most out of actuators that are weak or limited. Magnetorquers are cheap and use no fuel, but they are weak and only give torque in two directions at a time. A time-optimal slew uses *all* the torque *all* the time, which no linear law does.

**The storyline**
1. *Why magnetic torques, how strong?* The field falls as $1/R^3$: useful in LEO, useless at GEO.
2. *The field in the orbit frame*: $B_x$ and $B_z$ swing once per orbit, $B_y \propto \cos i$ is nearly constant.
3. ⭐ *Two rods and one wheel*: $M_z = -T_{cx}/B_y$, $M_x = T_{cz}/B_y$, and the wheel takes pitch plus the rods' leak. Scale saturated commands, do not clip them.
4. *Magnetorquers alone*: never controllable at an instant, but they can be over an orbit (the Gramian).
5. ⭐ *Magnetic unloading*: the law, its Lyapunov proof, and $k$ chosen from $\lambda_y(i)$.
6. ⭐ *Time-optimal maneuvers*: bang-bang with one switch, $t_{min} = 2\sqrt{\theta_f/u}$. PD and LQR are about 3× slower.
7. ⭐ *The switching curve*: $e = -\dot e|\dot e|/(2u)$ in the phase plane, and a PD near the target.
8. *Sampling, delays and wrong torque*: they cause chatter, which costs propellant with thrusters.
9. *Removing the chatter*: switch earlier by the delay, and blend linearly between the curves.
10. ⭐ *What a slew asks of the wheel*: $T = 4I\theta_f/t_f^2$, $h = 2I\theta_f/t_f$ (the same as L14).

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Magnetorquer (torqrod):** a coil around a ferromagnetic core; no moving parts, no fuel, a few watts, ~1/10 the cost of a wheel. Weak, no torque along $\mathbf B$, depends on orbit position.
- **Dipole field:** $|B| \propto 1/R^3$, twice as strong over the poles as the equator. 25.6 µT at 400 km, 0.106 µT at GEO.
- **Field in the orbit frame:** $B_x$, $B_z$ swing once per orbit; $B_y$ nearly constant $\propto\cos i$. Equatorial: only along $Y_B$.
- **Two rods + one wheel:** rods on $X_B$, $Z_B$ for roll and yaw (divide by $B_y$), wheel on $Y_B$ for pitch and the rods' parasitic pitch torque.
- **Scaling vs clipping:** scale all commands by one factor so the torque keeps its direction (stays on the Euler axis).
- **Time-varying controllability:** rank test → Gramian $W = \int\Phi BB^T\Phi^Tdt$ over an interval.
- **Unloading law:** $\mathbf M = \frac{k}{B^2}\Delta\mathbf h\times\mathbf B$ → $\mathbf T = -k\Delta\mathbf h_\perp$; Lyapunov $V = \frac12|\Delta\mathbf h|^2$.
- **Bang-bang / time-optimal:** full torque, then full braking, one switch; switching curve $e = -\dot e|\dot e|/(2u)$.
- **Chatter:** rapid torque reversals near the switching curve from delays or wrong torque; costs fuel and valve wear with thrusters.
- **Advanced switching curve:** switch earlier by the delay; blend linearly between the curves.

You have $\mathbf T = \mathbf M\times\mathbf B$, the two-axis damping scheme and B-dot (L10), reaction wheels, Euler-axis control and dumping $-K_wh_w$ (L13), controllability, Lyapunov, PD and LQR (L2–L4). ### Why magnetic torques? How strong? ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–6]])
Question 1: worth replacing a wheel in **LEO when slow attitude changes are OK**, and always as the second actuator that unloads the wheels.

| altitude | $\lvert B\rvert$ at equator | torque, 100 A m² |
|---|---|---|
| 400 km | 25.6 µT | 1.28 mN m |
| 800 km | 21.5 µT | 1.08 mN m |
| GEO | 0.106 µT | 5.3 µN m |

(Torque $= MB\sin\alpha$ with an average $\alpha = 30°$.) GEO is 6.6 Earth radii out: $6.6^3 \approx 290$× weaker.
![[R7026R E1 s5 dipole field LEO vs GEO.png]]
*A low orbit (dashed) lies deep in the dipole field ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]).*

Question 2: GEO disturbances ~15 µN m: holding needs $15\cdot10^{-6}/(0.106\cdot10^{-6}\cdot0.5) \approx 280$ A m² (heavy but possible); slewing at 1 mN m would need ~19 000 A m², impossible. **Magnetic control belongs in low orbits.**

**The field in the orbit frame ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]], backup [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]):** dipole along the Earth's axis, $m = 7.96\cdot10^{15}$ T m³, argument of latitude $u = \omega_ot$:
$$
B_x = \frac{m}{R^3}\cos u\sin i, \qquad B_y = -\frac{m}{R^3}\cos i, \qquad B_z = \frac{2m}{R^3}\sin u\sin i
$$
![[R7026R E1 s6 field in the orbit frame.png]]
*At 800 km: $B_x$ and $B_z$ swing once per orbit ($B_z$ with twice the amplitude); $B_y$ is constant, $\propto\cos i$. Equatorial: $\mathbf B$ only along $Y_B$. Polar: $\mathbf B$ in the orbit plane ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]).*

The real dipole is tilted ~10° and the Earth turns under the orbit → flight software carries a field model, and a magnetometer measures $\mathbf B$ in body axes.

### Two magnetorquers and one wheel ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7–9]])
Rods on $X_B$, $Z_B$, wheel on $Y_B$. With $M_y = 0$: $T_x = -M_zB_y$, $T_z = M_xB_y$, so
$$
M_z = -\frac{T_{cx}}{B_y}, \qquad M_x = \frac{T_{cz}}{B_y}, \qquad T_w = -\dot h_{wy} = T_{cy} - (M_zB_x - M_xB_z)
$$
The rods also push on $Y_B$ ($M_zB_x - M_xB_z$); the wheel supplies the rest of the pitch torque.
![[R7026R E1 s7 two magnetorquers and a wheel.png]]

Question 3: why the wheel on $Y_B$? The law divides by $B_y$, nearly constant in an inclined orbit. With the wheel on $Z_B$ we'd divide by $B_z$, which crosses zero twice per orbit. In a near-polar orbit $B_y \approx 0$: this scheme doesn't work.

**Saturation ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]):** clipping each command separately turns the torque, so the maneuver leaves its Euler axis. Instead **scale all by one factor**:
$$
s = \min\left(1,\ \frac{M_{max}}{|M_x|},\ \frac{M_{max}}{|M_z|},\ \frac{T_{w,max}}{|T_w|}\right)
$$
![[R7026R E1 s8 scale instead of clip.png]]
*Scaling keeps the direction of the commanded torque; clipping turns it ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]).*

**Example ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]):** $I = (30, 40, 20)$, 800 km, $i = 40°$, rods 150 A m², 0.2 N m wheel, PD $\omega_n = (0.1, 0.3, 0.1)$, $\zeta = 1$, command (3°, −4°, 1°). First commands 15.7 mN m (roll), 251 mN m (pitch); available $150\cdot16.5$ µT = 2.5 mN m from the rods, 200 mN m from the wheel → $s = 0.16$ for 18 s.
![[R7026R E1 s9 magnetic control simulation.png]]
*Within 5 % after 57 s (roll), 17 s (pitch), 58 s (yaw), no overshoot; the unsaturated linear design needs 47 s and 16 s ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]).*

Magnetic torques do the job a little slower than designed, at a tenth of the cost of two more wheels.

### Magnetorquers alone ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10–11]])
Without a wheel, the best dipole is normal to $\mathbf B$:
$$
\mathbf M = \frac{\mathbf B\times\mathbf T_c}{B^2} \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf M\times\mathbf B = \mathbf T_c - (\hat b\cdot\mathbf T_c)\hat b
$$
The part of the command along the field is lost. **At any instant: not controllable. Over an orbit: it may be.**

Question 4: with time-varying $B(t)$ the rank test becomes a **Gramian** over an interval:
$$
W = \int_0^T\Phi(T, \tau)B(\tau)B(\tau)^T\Phi(T, \tau)^Td\tau \quad\text{must have full rank}
$$
**Example ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]):** GG satellite $I = (50, 52, 3)$ at 700 km, three rods, no wheel:

| inclination | eigenvalues of $\int[B\times][B\times]^Tdt/\int B^2dt$ | rank of 6-state Gramian |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | 1, 1, 0 | 4 |
| 40° | 0.87, 0.64, 0.49 | 6 |
| 98° (SSO) | 0.99, 0.80, 0.21 | 6 |

- Equatorial: $\mathbf B$ always along $Y_B$ → **pitch can never be controlled magnetically**.
- Inclined: controllable, but the smallest eigenvalue shows the weakest direction: here **yaw** ($\mathbf B$ is mostly radial, along $Z_B$).
- Controllable on paper, weak in practice: rods alone give coarse pointing. Check stability over an orbit in simulation, not at a frozen instant.

### Magnetic unloading of the wheels ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–19]])
Why ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]): constant $T_d$ flows into the wheels until they saturate; only an external torque removes it. In LEO rods do it continuously and fuel-free while the wheels hold attitude. Three-axis maneuvering spacecraft keep total momentum near zero (stored momentum makes them stiff and hard to slew).

**The law ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]], = L13):** want $\mathbf T = -k\Delta\mathbf h$, $\Delta\mathbf h = \mathbf h_w - \mathbf h_N$. Choose $\mathbf M \perp \mathbf B$:
$$
\mathbf M = \frac{k}{B^2}(\Delta\mathbf h\times\mathbf B) \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf T = -k\Delta\mathbf h_\perp
$$
Derivation (backup [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]): cross $\mathbf M\times\mathbf B = -k\Delta\mathbf h$ with $\mathbf B$: $B^2\mathbf M - (\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf M)\mathbf B = -k\mathbf B\times\Delta\mathbf h$; with $\mathbf M \perp \mathbf B$ the middle term vanishes.

Question 5 ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]): at an instant, any $\Delta\mathbf h \parallel \mathbf B$ can't be removed. Over an inclined orbit $\mathbf B$ turns → every direction on average. Equatorial: pitch-wheel momentum **never**.

**Why it works: Lyapunov ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]):** the wheels absorb the torques, $\dot{\mathbf h}_w = \mathbf T_d + \mathbf M\times\mathbf B - \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h_w$. With $V = \frac12|\Delta\mathbf h|^2$, $\mathbf h_N = 0$, no disturbance:
$$
\dot V = \Delta\mathbf h\cdot\dot{\mathbf h}_w = -k\,\Delta\mathbf h\cdot\Delta\mathbf h_\perp - \underbrace{\Delta\mathbf h\cdot(\boldsymbol\omega\times\Delta\mathbf h)}_{=0} = -k|\Delta\mathbf h_\perp|^2 \le 0
$$
($\Delta\mathbf h\cdot\Delta\mathbf h_\perp = |\Delta\mathbf h_\perp|^2$ since the parallel part is orthogonal to $\Delta\mathbf h_\perp$.) Excess momentum never grows; in an inclined orbit $\mathbf B$ keeps turning, so $V \to 0$.

**Choosing $k$ ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]):** average over an orbit: $\Delta\dot{\mathbf h} \approx -k\langle1 - \hat b\hat b^T\rangle\Delta\mathbf h + \mathbf T_d$: first order, pole at $-k\lambda$, $\Delta h_{ss} \approx T_d/(k\lambda)$. For $Y_B$ momentum (backup [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35]]):
$$
\lambda_y = 1 - \frac{\cos^2i}{\sqrt{1 + 3\sin^2i}}
$$
| $i$ | 0° | 20° | 40° | 60° | 98° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\lambda_y$ | 0 | 0.24 | 0.61 | 0.86 | 0.99 |

Depends on inclination, not altitude. Larger $k$: less stored momentum, but bigger dipoles and torques. Pick $k$ from the allowed momentum, check $|M| \approx k|\Delta h_\perp|/B$, simulate. (Pole placement on the averaged model, confirmed on the time-varying one.)

**Example at 400 km ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=18|s. 18]]):** same as [[R7026R L13]]: $k = 10^{-3}$ → predicted 1.65 N m s, simulated 1.8 average.

**Other orbits (Question 6, [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]):** SSO 500 km, aero pitch torque $10^{-7}$ N m, wheels ≤ 5 mN m s: $k \approx 10^{-7}/(0.99\cdot0.005) = 2\cdot10^{-5}$ s⁻¹; dipole $\approx 2\cdot10^{-5}\cdot0.005/2.5\cdot10^{-5} = 0.004$ A m²: a small air coil. GEO can't (field ~230× weaker than at 500 km) → thrusters. **Polar orbits: easiest to unload pitch; equatorial: hardest.**

### Time-optimal maneuvers ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21–22]])
Slewing time is lost observing time. One axis: $I\ddot\theta = T$, $|T| \le T_{max}$, $u = T_{max}/I$ (for a large slew: the turn about the Euler axis with $I$ about that axis). Fastest rest-to-rest turn by $\theta_f$: full torque, then full braking, **one switch halfway**:
$$
t_{min} = 2\sqrt{\theta_f/u}, \qquad \dot\theta_{peak} = \sqrt{\theta_fu}
$$
(Each half: $\frac{\theta_f}{2} = \frac12u\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^2$.)
![[R7026R E1 s21 bang-bang slew.png]]
*Bang-bang: accelerate with $+T_{max}$, switch, brake with $-T_{max}$ ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]).*

**Why not PD or LQR? (Question 7, [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]])** $I = 600$ kg m², $T_{max} = 0.4$ N m, 2° slew:
![[R7026R E1 s22 time-optimal vs PD vs LQR.png]]
*Time-optimal: $t_{min} = 14.5$ s (1 % band after 13.4 s). PD that never saturates ($K\theta_f = T_{max}$ → $\omega_n = 0.138$, $\zeta = 1$): 48 s. LQR with Bryson weights: 48 s with 0.09° overshoot ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]).*

A linear law uses the full torque only for an instant; minimizing time means using all of it all the time. LQR minimizes a quadratic cost, not time.

### Bang-bang in the phase plane ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23–24]])
With constant torque the path in the $(e, \dot e)$ plane is a parabola ($e = \theta_{com} - \theta$). Only one parabola reaches the origin with full braking: the **switching curve**
$$
e = -\frac{\dot e|\dot e|}{2u}, \qquad T = T_{max}\operatorname{sign}\left(e + \frac{\dot e|\dot e|}{2u}\right)
$$
(Braking from rate $\dot e$ at deceleration $u$ covers $\dot e^2/(2u)$.)
![[R7026R E1 s23 switching curve phase plane.png]]
*(a) From A: accelerate to B on the switching curve, brake along it to the origin C. (b) In practice a linear law takes over near the origin (Sidi Fig. 7.6.1, [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]).*

**The practical algorithm ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]):** at the origin the sign function **chatters** (any delay or error flips the torque). So near the target a PD takes over, $T = Ke + K_d\dot e$ ($K = \omega_n^2I$, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_nI$), used when $|\dot e| \le T_{max}/K_d$ and the PD torque doesn't exceed $T_{max}$. Far away: bang-bang.
![[R7026R E1 s24 quasi time-optimal algorithm.png]]
*The quasi-time-optimal algorithm (Sidi Fig. 7.6.2, [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]).*

### Sampling, delays and wrong torque ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25–27]])
**Sampling ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]):** same imager, linear range $\omega_n = 0.5$, $\zeta = 1$:
![[R7026R E1 s25 effect of sampling time.png]]
*$T_{sam} = 0.01$ s: ideal, 13.4 s. 0.2 s: braking starts late, 0.09° overshoot, 20.5 s. 2.5 s: misses the linear range, still swinging 0.46° after a minute ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]).*

**Wrong torque or delay ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]]):** the curve assumes exact $u$ and no delay.
![[R7026R E1 s26 chatter from wrong torque or delay.png]]
*1.5 $T_{max}$ and 0.2 s delay: overshoots the curve, chatters along it (8 reversals instead of 2). 0.5 $T_{max}$: braking too weak, 0.67° overshoot, 45 s ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]]).*

Question 8: with a wheel, chatter only costs some power. With **on/off thrusters**, every reversal is another pair of pulses: propellant and valve wear.

**Thrusters ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27]]):** a pair of jets at arm $r$: $T = 2Fr$; propellant
$$
m_p = \frac{\int|T|\,dt}{rI_{sp}g_0}
$$
![[R7026R E1 s27 thruster pair torque.png]]
The 2° slew with 0.8 N m jets and 0.5 s sampling: plain law 8 reversals, 4.5 g; with the cure 2 reversals, 3.7 g. **For thrusters, chatter must be removed, not tolerated.**

### Removing the chatter ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28–29]])
- **Known loop delay $\Delta t$:** issue the braking command earlier (point 2) so the delayed path meets the switching curve (point 3). Advanced curve:
$$
e_2 = e_1 - \operatorname{sign}(\dot e)\left(2\Delta t|\dot e| + u\Delta t^2\right)
$$
- Between the two curves the torque varies **linearly** from $+T_{max}$ to $-T_{max}$: no hard switching.
- **Uncertain torque:** design with $u = (T_{max}/I)(1 - p)$; $p = -1$ allows twice the nominal torque.

![[R7026R E1 s28 advanced switching curve.png]]
*Switching early by the delay keeps the path on the nominal curve (Sidi Fig. 7.6.7, [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28]]).*

Derivation (backup [[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]): let $x = e > 0$ be the error and $v = \dot x < 0$ its rate (the error is closing), with acceleration $-u$ while the torque still accelerates the spacecraft. The braking command is issued at point 2, but the torque reverses only $\Delta t$ later, at point 3: $v_3 = v_2 - u\Delta t$, $x_3 = x_2 + v_2\Delta t - \frac12u\Delta t^2$. Require $x_3 = v_3^2/(2u)$ (on the nominal curve) and eliminate: $x_2 = \frac{v_2^2}{2u} - 2v_2\Delta t + u\Delta t^2$. Since $v_2 < 0$, the term $-2v_2\Delta t = +2|v_2|\Delta t$: the switch moves *earlier*, to a larger remaining error.

**Result ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]):** wheel delivers $2T_{max}$, sampling 0.5 s: plain law 8 reversals, 13.0 s; compensated ($\Delta t = 0.5$, $p = -1$) 2 reversals, 9.5 s. Removing chatter also made it **faster**.

### What a time-optimal slew asks of the wheel ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30]])
$$
T_{max} = \frac{4I\theta_f}{t_f^2}, \qquad h_{max} = \frac{2I\theta_f}{t_f}
$$
Sky survey: $I = 100$, 0.2 rad in 10 s → 0.8 N m and 4 N m s. Long full-torque periods heat the motor: real profiles accelerate, **coast**, then brake. Add the momentum stored by disturbances between unloadings. Halving the time: 4× torque, 2× momentum (L14 case studies).

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Field:** $B \approx \frac{m}{R^3}$ ($m = 7.96\cdot10^{15}$ T m³), up to 2× over the poles; orbit frame $B_y = -\frac{m}{R^3}\cos i$.

**Two rods + wheel:** $M_z = -T_{cx}/B_y$, $M_x = T_{cz}/B_y$, $T_w = T_{cy} - (M_zB_x - M_xB_z)$; scale $s = \min(1, M_{max}/|M_x|, M_{max}/|M_z|, T_{w,max}/|T_w|)$.

**Rods alone:** $\mathbf M = \frac{\mathbf B\times\mathbf T_c}{B^2}$, $\mathbf M\times\mathbf B = \mathbf T_c - (\hat b\cdot\mathbf T_c)\hat b$.

**Unloading:** $\mathbf M = \frac{k}{B^2}\Delta\mathbf h\times\mathbf B$, $\mathbf T = -k\Delta\mathbf h_\perp$, $\Delta h_{ss} \approx \frac{T_d}{k\lambda_y}$, $\lambda_y = 1 - \frac{\cos^2i}{\sqrt{1 + 3\sin^2i}}$.

**Time-optimal:** $u = T_{max}/I$, $t_{min} = 2\sqrt{\theta_f/u}$, $\dot\theta_{peak} = \sqrt{\theta_fu}$; switching curve $e = -\frac{\dot e|\dot e|}{2u}$; $T = T_{max}\operatorname{sign}\left(e + \frac{\dot e|\dot e|}{2u}\right)$.

**Thruster propellant:** $m_p = \frac{\int|T|dt}{rI_{sp}g_0}$.

## Common mistakes
- **Altitude instead of radius.** $B \approx m/R^3$ with $R$ measured from the Earth's center ($R_E$ + altitude), not the altitude itself.
- **Taking the full $MB$.** The torque is $MB\sin\alpha$. Assuming $\alpha = 90°$ all the time overestimates what a rod gives (the slides use an average $\alpha = 30°$).
- **Dividing by a small $B_y$.** The two-rod scheme fails in near-polar orbits, where $B_y \approx 0$.
- **Clipping each command.** That turns the torque away from the Euler axis. Scale all commands by the same factor $s$.
- **$\lambda_y$ from the altitude.** It depends only on the inclination. At $i = 0$ it is 0: pitch momentum cannot be unloaded magnetically.
- **The time-optimal formula with degrees or the wrong $u$.** $u = T_{max}/I$ (rad/s²) and $\theta_f$ in rad: $t_{min} = 2\sqrt{\theta_f/u}$.
- **Expecting PD or LQR to be time-optimal.** A linear law uses the full torque only at one instant. LQR minimizes a quadratic cost, not time.
- **Thruster arms.** A pair of jets at arm $r$ gives $T = 2Fr$, and the propellant uses the same arm: $m_p = \int|T|dt/(rI_{sp}g_0)$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_E1_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 21–22, 27]]) A small imager, $I = 600$ kg m² about the slew axis, $T_{max} = 0.4$ N m, must turn 2° rest-to-rest. (a) Find $t_{min}$, the peak rate and where the switch happens. (b) What PD stiffness never saturates, and its $\omega_n$? (c) The same slew with a thruster pair giving 0.8 N m on a 1 m arm ($I_{sp} = 220$ s): ideal bang-bang propellant?

**Solution:**
1. *Time-optimal:* $u = 0.4/600 = 6.67\cdot10^{-4}$ rad/s², $\theta_f = 2° = 0.0349$ rad.
$$
t_{min} = 2\sqrt{\frac{0.0349}{6.67\cdot10^{-4}}} = 2\sqrt{52.4} = 14.5\ \text{s}, \qquad \dot\theta_{peak} = \sqrt{0.0349\cdot6.67\cdot10^{-4}} = 4.82\cdot10^{-3}\ \text{rad/s} = 0.28°/\text{s}
$$
   Switch at $t = 7.2$ s, halfway ($\theta = 1°$). Check on the switching curve: $e = 1° = 0.01745$ rad, $\dot e^2/(2u) = (4.82\cdot10^{-3})^2/(1.33\cdot10^{-3}) = 0.01745$ ✓.
2. *PD that never saturates:* largest error $\theta_f$ at the start: $K\theta_f = T_{max}$ ⇒ $K = 0.4/0.0349 = 11.5$ N m/rad, $\omega_n = \sqrt{K/I} = \sqrt{0.0191} = 0.138$ rad/s ✓. With $\zeta = 1$ it takes ~48 s, about 3× the optimum.
3. *Thrusters at 0.8 N m:* $u = 1.33\cdot10^{-3}$, $t = 2\sqrt{0.0349/1.33\cdot10^{-3}} = 10.2$ s. $\int|T|dt = 0.8\cdot10.2 = 8.2$ N m s.
$$
m_p = \frac{8.2}{1\cdot220\cdot9.81} = 3.8\cdot10^{-3}\ \text{kg} \approx 3.8\ \text{g}
$$
   (The slide's compensated law used 3.7 g; the chattering plain law 4.5 g.)

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Why is the pitch wheel put on $Y_B$ in the two-rods-one-wheel scheme, and where does the scheme fail?

> [!example]- Answer
> The rod law divides by $B_y$, which is nearly constant ($\propto\cos i$) in an inclined orbit; $B_z$ crosses zero twice per orbit. It fails in near-polar orbits where $B_y \approx 0$.

- Q: A magnetorquer command saturates. Why scale instead of clip?

> [!example]- Answer
> Clipping one component changes the direction of the torque, so the maneuver leaves its Euler axis. Scaling all commands by one factor keeps the direction and only reduces the size.

- Q: Are magnetorquers alone controllable? Answer for an instant and for an orbit, equatorial and inclined.

> [!example]- Answer
> At an instant: no, never along $\mathbf B$. Over an orbit (time-varying Gramian): equatorial, no ($\mathbf B$ always along $Y_B$, pitch uncontrollable, rank 4); inclined, yes (rank 6), though the weakest direction (yaw at 40°) is slow.

- Q: Show that the unloading law never increases the excess momentum.

> [!example]- Answer
> $V = \frac12|\Delta\mathbf h|^2$, $\dot V = \Delta\mathbf h\cdot(-k\Delta\mathbf h_\perp - \boldsymbol\omega\times\Delta\mathbf h) = -k|\Delta\mathbf h_\perp|^2 \le 0$.

- Q: A sky survey must turn 0.1 rad in 5 s with $I = 200$ kg m². Wheel torque and momentum?

> [!example]- Answer
> $T = 4I\theta/t^2 = 4\cdot200\cdot0.1/25 = 3.2$ N m; $h = 2I\theta/t = 2\cdot200\cdot0.1/5 = 8$ N m s. (That torque calls for CMGs.)

- Q: Why does chatter matter more with thrusters than with wheels, and how is it removed?

> [!example]- Answer
> Each reversal is another pair of pulses: propellant and valve wear. Remove it with an advanced switching curve (switch earlier by the loop delay, $2\Delta t|\dot e| + u\Delta t^2$), a linear blend between the curves, and a design $u$ reduced for torque uncertainty.
