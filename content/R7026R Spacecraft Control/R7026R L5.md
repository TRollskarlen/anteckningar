**Question:** The state feedback of L4 needs the whole state, but sensors only give part of it. How do we estimate the rest (observer), what happens when we feed back the estimate (compensator, separation principle), and how does it all run on a flight computer that samples (discrete design, dead-beat)?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L3]], [[R7026R L4]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L6]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
An observer runs the model on board and corrects it with the measurement, $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x)$, so the estimation error obeys $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x$; if $(A, C)$ is observable, $L$ can put these poles anywhere by duality with state feedback ($L = $ `acker(A', C', p)'`), typically 2–6× faster than the controller, slower if the sensor is noisy. Feeding back the estimate, $u = -K\hat x$, gives a compensator whose closed-loop poles are just the controller poles plus the observer poles (separation principle), so $K$ and $L$ can be designed separately. For the flight computer, the plant is discretized exactly with a zero-order hold, $A_d = e^{Ah}$, $B_d = \int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma$, poles map as $z = e^{sh}$, and the same tools (acker, place, dlqr) work in discrete time; dead-beat (all poles at $z = 0$) is the fastest possible but costs huge torques and unfiltered noise.

## Roadmap
<!-- Read this first. ⭐ = core: you must be able to do it by hand (exam, projects). No star = understand the idea. (extra) = example or background, skim it. -->
**Why this lecture?** L4 assumed that every state is measured. In reality a star tracker gives the angle but not the rate. An observer rebuilds the missing states from the model and the measurements. Feeding back the *estimate* still works (separation principle). Finally, everything has to run on a flight computer that samples, so we move the design to discrete time.

**The storyline**
1. *Open-loop estimator*: only simulating the model fails, because nothing corrects the errors.
2. ⭐ *The observer*: model + correction $L(y - C\hat x)$. The error obeys $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x$, and $u$ drops out.
3. ⭐ *Designing $L$*: match coefficients (in observable form $L$ fills the first column), or use duality: $L$ = `acker(A', C', p)'`.
4. ⭐ *Body rate without a gyro*: for one axis $L_1 = 2\zeta_e\omega_e$, $L_2 = \omega_e^2$. Why the observer beats a filtered derivative: it knows the torque.
5. *(extra) Lunar lander*: the same double integrator, the same observer.
6. *Only detectable*: the modes that $L$ cannot move must die out by themselves.
7. ⭐ *Choosing the observer poles*: 2–6× faster than the controller, slower with a noisy sensor. This trade-off is what the Kalman filter (L7) solves optimally.
8. ⭐ *Compensator and separation principle*: with $u = -K\hat x$ the poles are those of $A - BK$ plus those of $A - LC$, so $K$ and $L$ are designed separately.
9. *The compensator as a transfer function*: a lead network that falls out by itself.
10. ⭐ *Exact discretization (ZOH)*: $A_d = e^{Ah}$, $B_d = \int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma$. For one axis, $B_d = [h^2/(2I),\ h/I]^T$.
11. ⭐ *From $s$ to $z$ and the sample rate*: map the poles with $z = e^{sh}$, place them on $(A_d, B_d)$, and sample 20–40× faster than the bandwidth.
12. ⭐ *Dead-beat*: all poles at $z = 0$, so the state is zero after $n$ samples. The torque grows as $1/h^2$.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Observer (estimator):** A copy of the model running on board, corrected by the measurement error $y - C\hat x$. Gives the states we don't measure (e.g. rate without a gyro).
- **Observer gain $L$:** How strongly the measurement corrects the model. Big $L$ = trust the sensor, small $L$ = trust the model.
- **Estimation error $\tilde x = x - \hat x$:** Obeys $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x$. Its poles decide how fast the estimate converges.
- **Duality for design:** Designing $L$ for $(A, C)$ = designing a state feedback for $(A^T, C^T)$, then transposing.
- **Detectability:** Enough for an observer: what it can't see must die out by itself.
- **Compensator:** Observer + state feedback $u = -K\hat x$. As a transfer function it's an $n$-th order filter (for one axis: a PD with built-in low-pass, a lead network).
- **Separation principle:** Closed-loop poles = controller poles ($A - BK$) ∪ observer poles ($A - LC$). Design $K$ and $L$ separately.
- **Zero-order hold (ZOH):** The actuator holds $u(k)$ constant over each sample. Makes the discretization exact.
- **Discretization:** $A_d = e^{Ah}$, $B_d = \int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma$. Exact, not an approximation.
- **$z = e^{sh}$:** Continuous poles map to discrete poles; stable ⇔ inside the unit circle.
- **Sample rate rule:** Sample 20–40× faster than the closed-loop bandwidth.
- **Dead-beat:** All discrete poles at $z = 0$ → state is zero after $n$ samples. Fastest possible, very expensive.

## Notes

### From L1–L4 to this lecture ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3–4]])
L4's state feedback $u = -Kx$ needs the **whole** state. But on one spacecraft axis, $x = [\theta, \omega]^T$, the star tracker only gives $\theta$. Without a gyro (or after a gyro failure), $\omega$ must be **estimated**.
![[R7026R L5 s4 axis without gyro.png]]
So we replace $x$ by an estimate $\hat x$ from an **estimator**: $u = -K\hat x$.

By the end: the complete loop of a flight computer: **measure → estimate → compute $u$ → hold it until the next sample.**

### First idea: open-loop estimator ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]])
If I know $x(0)$, $u(t)$, $A$ and $B$, I can just **simulate** the model: $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu$, $\hat x(0) = x(0)$. Then $\hat x(t) = x(t)$ exactly.

Is it robust? **No.** With any uncertainty (wrong $x(0)$, unknown disturbance, wrong model) the error $\tilde x = x - \hat x$ may drift away, and nothing ever corrects it.

### The observer: use feedback ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6–7]])
**Idea:** compare the measured output $y$ with the predicted output $C\hat x$, and correct the model where they disagree:
$$
\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L\,(y - C\hat x), \qquad \hat x(0) \text{ arbitrary (e.g. 0)}
$$
![[R7026R L5 s6 observer feedback.png]]
*Left: the observer = model + correction $L(y - \hat y)$. Right: one axis, $I = 10$ kg m², an unknown $10^{-5}$ N m torque (solar-pressure size) and unknown initial rate $10^{-4}$ rad/s. The model alone is 14° off after 10 min; the observer with $L = [0.56, 0.16]^T$ stays within 0.004° ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]).*

**Error dynamics ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]):** subtract the observer from the plant ($\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx$):
$$
\dot{\tilde x} = \dot x - \dot{\hat x} = A(x - \hat x) - LC(x - \hat x) = (A - LC)\,\tilde x
$$
The input $u$ cancels out! **The stability and speed of the error are set by the eigenvalues of $A - LC$.**

> [!tip] Plain words
> The observer is a simulator with a "nudge". Every moment it checks: "the sensor says $y$, my model says $C\hat x$, the difference must be my error". It nudges $\hat x$ in proportion to that difference. $L$ decides how hard it nudges. Compare with state feedback: there $K$ moved the poles of $A - BK$; here $L$ moves the poles of $A - LC$.

### How to design $L$ ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8–12]])
**Which canonical form? The observable one ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8–10]]).** There $C = [1\ 0 \cdots 0]$, so $LC$ only has a first column $[L_1, \dots, L_n]^T$, and
$$
A - LC = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 - L_1 & 1 & 0 & \cdots \\ -a_2 - L_2 & 0 & 1 & \cdots \\ \vdots & & & \ddots \\ -a_n - L_n & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
\det(sI - A + LC) = s^n + (a_1 + L_1)s^{n-1} + \dots + (a_n + L_n)
$$
Exactly like $K$ in the top row of the control canonical form (L4), but now in the first column.

**Do we need the canonical form? No ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]]).** Yet another duality:
> designing a controller for a controllable system ≡ designing an observer for an observable system

$A - LC$ has the same eigenvalues as its transpose $A^T - C^TL^T$. That's a state-feedback problem for the dual pair $(A^T, C^T)$ with "gain" $L^T$. So:
```matlab
% controller design
K = acker(A, B, poles);
% observer design
L = (acker(A', C', poles))';
```

### Spacecraft: body rate without a gyro ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–14]])
One axis, $x = [\theta, \omega]^T$, star tracker $y = \theta$. $(A, C)$ is observable (L3). Observer:
$$
\dot{\hat\theta} = \hat\omega + L_1(\theta - \hat\theta), \qquad \dot{\hat\omega} = T/I + L_2(\theta - \hat\theta)
$$
$$
A - LC = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 & 1 \\ -L_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad s^2 + L_1s + L_2
$$
Poles 4× faster than the PD loop of L4 ($\omega_n = 0.1$): $\omega_e = 0.4$ rad/s, $\zeta_e = 0.7$ ⇒ $L_1 = 2\zeta_e\omega_e = 0.56$, $L_2 = \omega_e^2 = 0.16$. (Subscript $e$ = estimator; $\omega_o$ is kept for the orbital rate.)

**What does the observer do with the angle? ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]])**
$$
\hat\omega(s) = \underbrace{\frac{L_2s}{s^2 + L_1s + L_2}\,\theta(s)}_{\text{filtered derivative}} + \underbrace{\frac{s + L_1}{s^2 + L_1s + L_2}\,\frac{T(s)}{I}}_{\text{model}}
$$
- The classic fix without a gyro: differentiate the measured angle and low-pass filter the noise. That's the **first term alone**.
- The observer adds the **second term**: the applied torque is known, so the estimate **doesn't lag** during a maneuver.

![[R7026R L5 s14 observer vs filtered derivative.png]]
*A 52° slew, star-tracker noise 20 arcsec at 10 Hz, same $L$: the filtered derivative lags by up to 0.23°/s, the observer by 0.004°/s ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]).*

### Space example: descent rate of a lunar lander ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]])
![[R7026R L5 s15 lunar lander.png]]
A lander descends vertically: altitude $h$, velocity $v$, thrust acceleration $a_T$ (commanded by guidance, so known), lunar gravity $g_M = 1.62$ m/s². Only $h$ is measured (altimeter):
$$
\dot h = v, \quad \dot v = a_T - g_M, \quad y = h \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \ B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \ C = [1\ 0], \ u = a_T - g_M
$$
**The same double integrator as one spacecraft axis** → the same observer works. E.g. $\omega_e = 0.5$ rad/s, $\zeta_e = 0.7$ ⇒ $L = [0.7, 0.25]^T$.

Why not just differentiate $h$? Altimeter noise and terrain passing under the lander would be amplified by $s$. The observer **blends** the altimeter with the thrust we commanded.

### When the system is only detectable ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]])
In the observability decomposition (L3), $x_2$ never reaches $y$. Design $L_1$ for the observable part $(A_{11}, C_1)$ and use $L = [L_1^T\ 0]^T$:
$$
\dot{\tilde x} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} - L_1C_1 & 0 \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}\tilde x
$$
Error poles: those of $A_{11} - L_1C_1$ (ours to place) and those of $A_{22}$ (no $L$ moves them). The estimate converges ⇔ $A_{22}$ is stable ⇔ **detectable**. The error in $x_2$ dies at $A_{22}$'s own rate; the slower those modes, the poorer the estimate.

Spacecraft: a damped bending mode of a solar array with its node at the star tracker ($c_i = 0$, L3) is unobservable but decays: the observer simply ignores it.

→ Observers (and the Kalman filter, L7) need **detectability**, not full observability.

### Multiple outputs and MIMO ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17–18]])
Same equations for MIMO: $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x)$, error poles from $\det(sI - A + LC) = 0$. But `acker` only works for single input/output. For MIMO use `place` (a pole can be repeated at most rank $B$ times):
```matlab
K = place(A, B, poles);
L = (place(A', C', poles))';
```
E.g. all three axes: $n = 6$ states, $m = 3$ wheel torques, $p = 3$ star-tracker angles.

### Choosing the observer poles ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19–20]])
Rules of thumb:
- The observer should be **2–6 times faster** than the controller.
- The **noisier the sensors, the slower** the observer should be.
- The **slower the observer, the less resilient** the controller is to disturbances.

Algorithms: dominant second-order poles, or LQR on $(A^T, C^T)$ = the steady-state Kalman filter (L7).

![[R7026R L5 s19 fast vs slow observer.png]]
*The same axis hit by a torque the observer doesn't know about (e.g. a thruster leak) ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]). The fast observer follows at once but carries the star-tracker noise; the slow one is smooth but about 15 s late.*

**Connection to estimation theory ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]):** with process noise $w$ and measurement noise $\nu$ ($\dot x = Ax + Bu + w$, $y = Cx + \nu$):
$$
\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x + w - L\nu
$$
- $L$ **big** → the effect of $w$ is small, but $\nu$ is **amplified**.
- $L$ **small** → $\nu$ barely matters, but $w$ has a bigger influence.

The statistically optimal trade-off = **Kalman filter** (L7).

### Compensator: observer + feedback ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21–25]])
![[R7026R L5 s21 compensator loop.png]]
*Top: the compensator. Bottom: the same loop on board: the star tracker is $C$, wheel + spacecraft are the plant, the observer and $-K$ are a few lines of flight software ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]).*

Interconnection: $u = -K\hat x$, so
- Plant: $\dot x = Ax - BK\hat x = (A - BK)x + BK\tilde x$ (using $\hat x = x - \tilde x$)
- Error: $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x$ (as before)

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot x \\ \dot{\tilde x} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A - BK & BK \\ 0 & A - LC \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ \tilde x \end{bmatrix}
$$
Block **upper-triangular** ⇒ the eigenvalues are those of the diagonal blocks:

> [!important] Separation principle ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]])
> $$\det[sI - (A - BK)]\cdot\det[sI - (A - LC)] = 0$$
> The closed loop keeps the **controller poles** and the **observer poles**, unchanged. → Design $K$ (L4) and $L$ (this lecture) **separately**.

![[R7026R L5 s25 separation principle poles.png]]
*Axis without a gyro: PD gains $K = [0.1, 1.4]$ and observer $L = [0.56, 0.16]^T$. The 4×4 loop's eigenvalues (circles) are exactly the controller (×blue) and observer (×orange) poles ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]).*

> [!tip] Plain words
> The plant is "driven by the estimation error" ($BK\tilde x$), but the error itself evolves on its own, never influenced by $x$. Once the error has died out, the loop behaves exactly like the full-state feedback of L4.

### What is the compensator as a transfer function? ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26–27]])
Is $C(s) = U(s)/Y(s)$ a PID? **No**, in general an $n$-th order filter:
$$
\dot{\hat x} = (A - BK - LC)\hat x + Ly, \quad u = -K\hat x \quad\Rightarrow\quad C(s) = -K(sI - A + BK + LC)^{-1}L
$$
For the axis without a gyro: $C(s) = -0.28\,\frac{s + 0.057}{s^2 + 0.70s + 0.25}$, a PD with a built-in low-pass = a **lead network**. When choosing $K$ and $L$ we never cared about $C(s)$; it just comes out.

### Going digital: what the flight computer does ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29–31]])
![[R7026R L5 s29 flight computer loop.png]]
- The star tracker delivers one attitude every $h$ seconds: $\theta(k) = \theta(kh)$.
- The computer computes $u(k)$ (observer and $-K$).
- The wheel holds $u(k)$ until the next sample (**zero-order hold**); the spacecraft moves in continuous time in between.

Question: which discrete model links $u(k)$ to $\theta(k+1)$?

**Two workflows ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30–31]]):**
- *Discrete equivalents:* design $C_c(s)$ in continuous time, then approximate it with $C_d(z)$ (`c2d`: ZOH, Tustin $s \mapsto \frac{2}{h}\frac{z-1}{z+1}$, matched poles/zeros). Always an approximation, good only if sampled fast; meaningful mostly for PIDs.
- *Discrete design:* in state-space control $K$ and $L$ are plain matrices with no $s$ to approximate ⇒ **discretize the plant exactly, then design $K$ and $L$ directly in discrete time.** ← this course.

### Exact discretization with ZOH ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32–35]])
Start from the L2 solution, from $t_0 = kh$ to $t = kh + h$:
$$
x(k+1) = e^{Ah}x(k) + \int_{kh}^{kh+h} e^{A(kh+h-\tau)}Bu(\tau)\,d\tau
$$
With ZOH, $u(\tau) = u(k)$ is constant on $[kh, kh+h)$, so it comes out of the integral. Substituting $\sigma = kh + h - \tau$:
$$
x(k+1) = \underbrace{e^{Ah}}_{A_d}x(k) + \underbrace{\left(\int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma\right)}_{B_d}u(k)
$$
![[R7026R L5 s33 zero order hold.png]]
*Wanted torque (dashed) vs delivered: constant over each sample ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=33|s. 33]]).*

> [!important] ZOH discretization
> $$A_d = e^{Ah}, \qquad B_d = \int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma, \qquad C_d = C, \qquad D_d = D$$
> **Exact**, not an approximation (given the ZOH). MATLAB: `c2d`.

**Example: one axis, sampled ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36]]).** $A^2 = 0$ ⇒ $e^{A\sigma} = I + A\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \sigma \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$:
$$
A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B_d = \int_0^h \begin{bmatrix} \sigma/I \\ 1/I \end{bmatrix}d\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} h^2/(2I) \\ h/I \end{bmatrix}
$$
With torque $T$ constant over a sample, the axis turns by $\frac{Th^2}{2I}$ and speeds up by $\frac{Th}{I}$ (exactly the constant-acceleration formulas). Forward Euler ($A_d \approx I + Ah$, $B_d \approx Bh = [0, h/I]^T$) **misses the $h^2/(2I)$**.

### From $s$ to $z$ ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]])
If $A$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $A_d = e^{Ah}$ has eigenvalue $e^{\lambda h}$. The same map as L1: $z = e^{sh}$, left half plane → inside the unit circle.

| $h$ | $z = e^{sh}$ for $s = -0.07 \pm 0.0714j$ | $\lvert z\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 s | $0.930 \pm 0.067j$ | 0.932 |
| 10 s | $0.375 \pm 0.325j$ | 0.497 |

![[R7026R L5 s37 s to z poles.png]]
*Fast sampling crowds the poles near $z = 1$; dead-beat puts them at the origin ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]]).*

**Discrete design recipe:** (1) choose the poles in $s$ from the specs (L1, L4), (2) map them with $z = e^{sh}$, (3) place them with `acker`/`place` on $(A_d, B_d)$, or use `dlqr`.

### Choosing the sample rate ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=38|s. 38]])
Rule of thumb: sample **20–40 times faster** than the closed-loop bandwidth, $\omega_s = 2\pi/h \ge 20\,\omega_{BW}$.
- **Too slow:** the hold acts like a delay of about $h/2$, eating phase margin; rough response between samples.
- **Too fast:** more computation, all poles crowd near $z = 1$ (numerically delicate), and any finite-difference rate estimate amplifies sensor noise by $1/h$.
- Before sampling, **filter out what lies above $\pi/h$**, or it aliases into the band we control (L6).

Example: PD attitude loop, $\omega_n = 0.1$ ⇒ $\omega_{BW} \approx \omega_n$ ⇒ $\omega_s \ge 2$–4 rad/s ⇒ $h \le 3.1$–1.6 s. In practice the star tracker (several attitudes per second) sets the pace.

**What doesn't change in discrete time ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]]):** controllability and observability (except for pathological $h$), canonical forms, reference handling, second-order/LQR strategies, `acker` and `place`.

### Dead-beat control ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40–43]])
**Only in discrete time.** If $(A, B)$ is controllable, put **all** eigenvalues of $A - BK$ at $z = 0$:
$$
\alpha(z) = z^n, \qquad K = [0 \cdots 0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}A^n \quad \text{(Ackermann)}
$$
All eigenvalues zero ⇒ $(A - BK)^n = 0$ (nilpotent, Cayley–Hamilton) ⇒
$$
x(n) = (A - BK)^nx(0) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x(0)
$$
**The state is zero after at most $n$ steps!** No continuous controller can do this ($z = 0$ ↔ $s = -\infty$).

**One axis, $n = 2$ ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=41|s. 41]]):**
$$
K = [0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}_d^{-1}A_d^2 = \left[\frac{I}{h^2},\ \frac{3I}{2h}\right], \qquad A_d - B_dK = \begin{bmatrix} \frac12 & \frac h4 \\ -\frac1h & -\frac12 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (A_d - B_dK)^2 = 0
$$
Start at rest, $\theta(0) = \theta_0$:

| $k$ | $\theta(k)$ | $\dot\theta(k)$ | torque $u(k)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $\theta_0$ | 0 | $-I\theta_0/h^2$ |
| 1 | $\theta_0/2$ | $-\theta_0/h$ | $+I\theta_0/h^2$ |
| 2 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

Push for one sample, brake for one sample, done: a perfect **bang–bang** maneuver.

![[R7026R L5 s42 dead-beat simulation.png]]
*$\theta_0 = 10°$, zero after two samples in both cases: ten times faster needs one hundred times the torque ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=42|s. 42]]).*

**The price ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43]]):** $I = 10$ kg m², $\theta_0 = 10° = 0.175$ rad:

| $h$ | first torque $\lvert u(0)\rvert = I\theta_0/h^2$ | done after |
|---|---|---|
| 1 s | 1.75 N m | 2 s |
| 10 s | 0.0175 N m | 20 s |

Small-satellite wheels give tens of mN m: $h = 1$ s asks for ~100× more. Torque grows as $1/h^2$. $K \propto 1/h^2$ also multiplies star-tracker noise, and if $I$ is slightly wrong the state isn't zero after two samples. → **Dead-beat is the fastest a discrete controller can be: a useful limit, rarely a good attitude controller.**

**Dead-beat observer ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44]]):** all eigenvalues of $A - LC$ at 0 → estimation error zero after $n$ samples. One axis with a star tracker: $L = [2, 1/h]^T$, which after two samples gives
$$
\hat{\dot\theta}(k+1) = \frac{\theta(k) - \theta(k-1)}{h}
$$
a **pure finite difference**: no filtering at all, every star-tracker error goes straight into the rate, divided by $h$.

Problems: dead-beat controller → uses the actuators a lot; dead-beat observer → amplifies noise a lot.

**Observer**
$$
\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x), \qquad \dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x, \qquad \tilde x = x - \hat x
$$
where $\hat x$ = estimate, $L$ = observer gain, $\tilde x$ = estimation error.

**Design by duality:** $L = $ `acker(A', C', p)'` (SISO), `place(A', C', p)'` (MIMO).

**One axis / lander observer** ($C = [1\ 0]$, double integrator)
$$
\det(sI - A + LC) = s^2 + L_1s + L_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad L_1 = 2\zeta_e\omega_e,\ L_2 = \omega_e^2
$$
**Compensator and separation**
$$
u = -K\hat x, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} \dot x \\ \dot{\tilde x} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A - BK & BK \\ 0 & A - LC \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ \tilde x \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C(s) = -K(sI - A + BK + LC)^{-1}L
$$
**ZOH discretization**
$$
A_d = e^{Ah}, \qquad B_d = \int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma, \qquad z = e^{sh}
$$
**One axis sampled:** $A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $B_d = \begin{bmatrix} h^2/(2I) \\ h/I \end{bmatrix}$.

**Sample rate:** $\omega_s = 2\pi/h \ge 20\,\omega_{BW}$.

**Dead-beat:** $\alpha(z) = z^n$, $K = [0 \cdots 0\ 1]\,\mathcal{C}^{-1}A^n$; one axis $K = [I/h^2,\ 3I/(2h)]$.

## Common mistakes
- **$LC$, not $CL$.** The error matrix is $A - LC$ ($n \times n$). $CL$ has a different size and is meaningless here.
- **Forgetting the final transpose.** By duality you design a "gain" $L^T$ for $(A^T, C^T)$, so $L$ = `acker(A', C', p)'`. Without the last `'` you get a row instead of a column.
- **Feeding the observer the wrong input.** The observer must use the input that *actually* reached the plant. If the actuator saturates, use the saturated value (Project 1 says so explicitly), otherwise $u$ no longer cancels in the error equation.
- **Observer slower than the controller.** Then the loop is limited by how slowly the estimate converges, not by $K$. Usual choice: 2–6× faster.
- **Forward Euler instead of the exact discretization.** $B_d \approx Bh$ misses the $h^2/(2I)$ term. With a ZOH, $A_d = e^{Ah}$ and $B_d = \int_0^h e^{A\sigma}B\,d\sigma$ are exact.
- **Using $z = e^{sh}$ on everything.** It maps *poles* exactly. Zeros and whole transfer functions do not map that simply (that is what `c2d` is for).
- **Two different $h$.** In this lecture $h$ is the sample time. In the lander example the altitude is also $h$, and in Part 3 $h$ is angular momentum. Write $h_s$ when they can be confused.
- **Dead-beat in continuous time.** It does not exist there: $z = 0$ corresponds to $s = -\infty$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_5_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]], 36) A lunar lander measures only its altitude $h$. (a) Design an observer for $[h, v]^T$ with $\omega_e = 0.5$ rad/s, $\zeta_e = 0.7$. (b) The flight computer samples every $h_s = 0.1$ s with a zero-order hold. Write the exact discrete model.

**Solution:**
1. *Model:* $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $C = [1\ 0]$. Observable: $\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, rank 2 ✓.
2. *Error dynamics:* $LC = \begin{bmatrix} L_1 & 0 \\ L_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$,
$$
A - LC = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 & 1 \\ -L_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(sI - A + LC) = \det\begin{bmatrix} s + L_1 & -1 \\ L_2 & s \end{bmatrix} = s^2 + L_1s + L_2
$$
3. *Match the dream polynomial* $s^2 + 2\zeta_e\omega_es + \omega_e^2 = s^2 + 0.7s + 0.25$:
$$
L_1 = 0.7, \qquad L_2 = 0.25 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad L = [0.7,\ 0.25]^T
$$
4. *Observer equations:*
$$
\dot{\hat h} = \hat v + 0.7\,(h - \hat h), \qquad \dot{\hat v} = (a_T - g_M) + 0.25\,(h - \hat h)
$$
   It uses the commanded thrust (known) plus a correction from the altimeter.
5. *Discretize* ($A^2 = 0$, so $e^{A\sigma} = I + A\sigma$), with $h_s = 0.1$:
$$
A_d = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B_d = \begin{bmatrix} h_s^2/2 \\ h_s \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.005 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}
$$
   (Here the "inertia" is 1 because $u$ is already an acceleration.)
6. *Check the sample rate:* observer bandwidth ≈ 0.5 rad/s; $\omega_s = 2\pi/0.1 = 63$ rad/s ≫ $20 \cdot 0.5 = 10$ ✓.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: Why does the input $u$ not appear in the estimation-error equation?

> [!example]- Answer
> Both the plant and the observer get the same $Bu$, so it cancels when subtracting: $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x$. This is why the observer must know the applied input.

- Q: The controller poles are at $-0.07 \pm 0.07j$. Roughly where should the observer poles be? What if the star tracker is very noisy?

> [!example]- Answer
> 2–6× faster, e.g. $\omega_e \approx 0.2$–0.6 rad/s. If the sensor is noisy, choose the slow end (closer to 2×), since a fast observer (large $L$) amplifies measurement noise.

- Q: State the separation principle. Why does it hold?

> [!example]- Answer
> The closed-loop poles with $u = -K\hat x$ are the poles of $A - BK$ plus the poles of $A - LC$. In coordinates $(x, \tilde x)$ the system matrix is block upper-triangular, $\begin{bmatrix} A - BK & BK \\ 0 & A - LC \end{bmatrix}$, and the eigenvalues of a block-triangular matrix are those of its diagonal blocks.

- Q: Map the continuous pole $s = -2$ with $h = 0.5$ s. Is it stable?

> [!example]- Answer
> $z = e^{-2 \cdot 0.5} = e^{-1} \approx 0.37$. Inside the unit circle → stable.

- Q: Why is dead-beat rarely a good attitude controller?

> [!example]- Answer
> The torque scales as $1/h^2$ (e.g. 1.75 N m for a 10° turn with $h = 1$ s, ~100× what small wheels give), the gains multiply sensor noise, and with model errors (wrong $I$) it doesn't reach zero anyway.
