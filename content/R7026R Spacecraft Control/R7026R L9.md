**Question:** How does a spacecraft's attitude respond to torques (Euler's equations with wheels), what torque does gravity itself put on it, and when does gravity gradient alone hold it pointing at the Earth?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L8]], [[R7026R L2]], [[R7026R L3]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L10]], [[R7026R L11]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
In principal axes (eigenvectors of $[I]$) Euler's equation $\mathbf T = \dot{\mathbf h} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ with $\mathbf h = [I]\boldsymbol\omega + \mathbf h_w$ gives the full nonlinear attitude dynamics, and linearized around nadir pointing (L8 kinematics + the linearized gravity-gradient torque $G_x = 3\omega_o^2(I_z - I_y)\phi$, $G_y = 3\omega_o^2(I_z - I_x)\theta$, $G_z = 0$) it splits into a decoupled pitch equation and a coupled roll–yaw pair. Pitch is a pendulum, $I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta = T_y$, stable only if $I_x > I_z$ (the axis of least inertia points at the Earth); roll–yaw is stable only inside regions A or B of the $\sigma_x$–$\sigma_z$ plane. Even when stable, nothing is damped: the passive satellite oscillates forever, which L11 fixes.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Inertia matrix $[I]$:** $\mathbf h = [I]\boldsymbol\omega$. Symmetric, constant in body axes; moments on the diagonal, products of inertia off it.
- **Principal axes:** Eigenvectors of $[I]$; the principal moments are the eigenvalues. In these axes $[I]$ is diagonal and all equations shorten. Major axis = largest moment, minor = smallest.
- **Euler's moment equation:** $\mathbf T = \dot{\mathbf h}_I = \dot{\mathbf h}_B + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$. The derivative in inertial space = derivative in body axes + the rotation of the axes.
- **Momentum exchange devices:** Reaction wheels, momentum wheels, CMGs. Their momentum $\mathbf h_w$ adds to the body's; speeding a wheel up turns the body the other way (internal torque $-\dot{\mathbf h}_w$).
- **$\mathbf T_c$, $\mathbf T_d$:** control torques (thrusters, magnetorquers) and disturbance torques (drag, solar pressure, magnetic).
- **Gravity-gradient torque:** The near end of the body is pulled harder than the far end → a torque that turns the axis of least inertia toward the Earth. $\propto 1/R_0^3$, so strongest in LEO.
- **Gravity gradient changes role:** a disturbance in L6, here a **spring inside the plant** (the $3\omega_o^2$ and $4\omega_o^2$ stiffness terms).
- **Momentum bias:** A wheel spinning all the time along $Y_B$ (orbit normal) gives gyroscopic stiffness in roll and yaw without control action.
- **Inertia ratios:** $\sigma_x = \frac{I_y - I_z}{I_x}$, $\sigma_y = \frac{I_x - I_z}{I_y}$, $\sigma_z = \frac{I_y - I_x}{I_z}$; all $|\sigma| < 1$. $\sigma_y$ decides pitch, $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$ decide roll–yaw.
- **Libration:** The undamped oscillation of a gravity-gradient satellite around the local vertical.

### From L8 and R7025R to this lecture ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3]])
L8 gave the **kinematics** (how attitude changes with the rates). This lecture adds the **dynamics** (how the rates change with torques), so that kinematics + dynamics = **the attitude plant** that every later lecture controls. New today: principal axes as an eigenvalue problem, Euler's equation with wheels, the gravity-gradient torque derived and linearized, the linearized roll/pitch/yaw equations, when gravity gradient alone holds attitude, and the same results in state space.

### Angular momentum and kinetic energy (review, [[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–5]])
Each particle $m_i$ at $\mathbf r_i$ from the CM moves with $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i$, so
$$
\mathbf h = \sum_i \mathbf r_i\times m_i(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i) = [I]\boldsymbol\omega, \qquad [I] = \begin{bmatrix} I_{xx} & -I_{xy} & -I_{xz} \\ -I_{yx} & I_{yy} & -I_{yz} \\ -I_{zx} & -I_{zy} & I_{zz} \end{bmatrix}
$$
- Moments: $I_{xx} = \sum m_i(y_i^2 + z_i^2)$ (distance squared from the $x$ axis). Products: $I_{xy} = \sum m_i x_iy_i$.
- Kinetic energy: $T_{rot} = \frac12\boldsymbol\omega^T[I]\boldsymbol\omega = \frac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf h$.
- Moment of inertia about any axis $\hat\xi$: $I_\xi = \hat\xi^T[I]\hat\xi$.

> [!tip] Question 1 ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]): can $T_{rot}$ change while $\mathbf h$ stays constant?
> Yes. Internal friction (fuel slosh, flexing antennas) removes energy but cannot change $\mathbf h$ (no external torque). L12 shows this decides which spin axis is stable.

### Principal axes ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6–12]])
In axes aligned with the mass distribution $[I']$ is diagonal. Finding them = an **eigenvalue problem**:
$$
\det([I] - \lambda 1) = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3 \text{ (principal moments)}, \qquad [I]\mathbf e_i = \lambda_i\mathbf e_i \ \Rightarrow\ \text{principal axes}
$$
Put the eigenvectors as **columns** of $[A]$: $[I'] = [A]^T[I][A] = \operatorname{diag}(I_1, I_2, I_3)$, and $\boldsymbol\omega' = [A]^T\boldsymbol\omega$. (In L8's convention the DCM from the old axes to the principal axes is $[A]^T$.)

**Example 1 ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8–11]]):**
$$
[I] = \begin{bmatrix} 20 & -10 & 0 \\ -10 & 30 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 40 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{kg m}^2 \ \Rightarrow\ \lambda = 13.82,\ 36.18,\ 40
$$
$\mathbf e_1 = (0.851, 0.526, 0) = (\cos31.7°, \sin31.7°, 0)$: the principal axes are the body axes turned **31.7° about $Z_B$**. $I_z = 40$ is untouched because $Z_B$ had no products of inertia.

![[R7026R L9 s8 principal axes rotated.png]]
*The mass is spread along $\mathbf e_1$, 31.7° from $X_B$; that's why $I_{xy} \neq 0$ in the build axes ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]).*

MATLAB: `[V, D] = eig(I)`. The sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary (Question 2, [[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12]]): pick the signs so that $\det[A] = +1$ (right-handed, a proper rotation, L8). **From here on the body axes are principal axes.**

### Euler's moment equations ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–15]])
Newton for rotation holds in **inertial** space, $\mathbf M = \frac{d\mathbf h}{dt}\big|_I$. But $\mathbf h$ is written in rotating body axes, so use the transport theorem $\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\big|_I = \frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\big|_B + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf A$:
$$
\mathbf M = \dot{\mathbf h}_B + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h
$$
In principal axes, $\mathbf h = (I_x\omega_x, I_y\omega_y, I_z\omega_z)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_x &= I_x\dot\omega_x + \omega_y\omega_z(I_z - I_y) \\
M_y &= I_y\dot\omega_y + \omega_x\omega_z(I_x - I_z) \\
M_z &= I_z\dot\omega_z + \omega_x\omega_y(I_y - I_x)
\end{aligned}
$$
- **Nonlinear** (products of rates), no general closed-form solution.
- Solvable in special cases: symmetric body, torque-free, small angles (**this lecture**), steady spin about a principal axis (**L12**).
- Memory trick: cyclic $x \to y \to z \to x$; each line has the "next two" rates times "the one after minus the next".

> [!tip] Question 3 ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]): no moment, is $\boldsymbol\omega$ constant?
> Not in general. $\mathbf h$ is constant **in inertial space**, but the $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ term keeps moving $\boldsymbol\omega$ around in the body frame (nutation, L12). Only spin exactly about a principal axis keeps $\boldsymbol\omega$ constant.

### Adding momentum wheels ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16–20]])
Real spacecraft carry **momentum exchange devices**: reaction wheels, momentum wheels, CMGs (L13). A motor spins a flywheel, $h_w = I_w\Omega$.

![[R7026R L9 s16 reaction wheel.png]]
*A reaction wheel: speeding the flywheel up pushes the spacecraft the other way, an internal torque ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]]).*

Write $\mathbf T$ for $\mathbf M$, split the external torque, and add the wheel momentum:
$$
\mathbf T = \mathbf T_c + \mathbf T_d = \dot{\mathbf h} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h, \qquad \mathbf h = \underbrace{[I]\boldsymbol\omega}_{\mathbf h_B} + \underbrace{(h_{wx}, h_{wy}, h_{wz})}_{\mathbf h_w}
$$
- $\mathbf T_c$: control torques from **outside** the body (thrusters, magnetorquers). Wheel torques are internal and appear through $\dot{\mathbf h}_w$.
- $\mathbf T_d$: disturbances: gravity gradient, drag, solar pressure, magnetic.

Component form ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19]]), e.g. the $x$ line:
$$
T_x = \dot h_x + \dot h_{wx} + (\omega_yh_z - \omega_zh_y) + (\omega_yh_{wz} - \omega_zh_{wy})
$$
This complete nonlinear model is what 6-DOF simulators integrate ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]): $\boldsymbol\omega$ in body axes, wheel momenta along their mounting axes, $\mathbf T_d$ from the orbit and environment.

**To design controllers we linearize** ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]): principal axes (no products of inertia) + small angles and rates → Laplace and state-space models → pole placement, LQR, observers (L1–L5).

### The gravity-gradient torque ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23–29]])
**Why it exists:** gravity falls off as $1/r^2$. In a body tilted from the vertical, the part nearer the Earth is pulled harder than the CM, the far part less. Relative to the CM that's a pair of forces → a torque that turns the **axis of least inertia toward the Earth**.

![[R7026R L9 s23 dumbbell gravity gradient.png]]
*A dumbbell tilted by $\theta$: relative to the CM the lower mass is pulled down, the upper one up, so the long axis is turned back to the vertical ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]).*

**Geometry ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24–25]]):** orbit frame at the CM with $\mathbf k_R$ toward the Earth's center; the body is turned from it by $\phi, \theta, \psi$. A mass element $dm$ sits at $\boldsymbol\rho$ from the CM.
![[R7026R L9 s24 gravity gradient geometry.png]]
*$\mathbf R$: Earth center to CM, $\boldsymbol\rho$: CM to $dm$, $\mathbf r = \mathbf R + \boldsymbol\rho$ (Sidi Fig. 4.8.1, [[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=24|s. 24]]).*

$\mathbf R = -R_0\mathbf k_R$ in body axes via the 3-2-1 DCM: $R_x = R_0\sin\theta$, $R_y = -R_0\sin\phi\cos\theta$, $R_z = -R_0\cos\phi\cos\theta$.

**Derivation sketch ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26–27]]):**
1. Force on $dm$: $d\mathbf F = -\frac{\mu\,dm}{|\mathbf r|^3}\mathbf r$. Moment about the CM: $d\mathbf G = \boldsymbol\rho\times d\mathbf F$.
2. Since $\rho \ll R_0$: $\frac{1}{r^3} \approx \frac{1}{R_0^3}\left(1 - \frac{3(\mathbf R\cdot\boldsymbol\rho)}{R_0^2}\right)$. The "1" part integrates to zero ($\int\boldsymbol\rho\,dm = 0$ at the CM); the correction is the gradient.
3. Integrate: $\mathbf G = \frac{3\mu}{R_0^5}\int(\mathbf R\cdot\boldsymbol\rho)(\boldsymbol\rho\times\mathbf R)\,dm$, then use the inertia definitions:
$$
G_x = \frac{3\mu}{2R_0^3}(I_z - I_y)\sin2\phi\cos^2\theta, \quad G_y = \frac{3\mu}{2R_0^3}(I_z - I_x)\sin2\theta\cos\phi, \quad G_z = \frac{3\mu}{2R_0^3}(I_x - I_y)\sin2\theta\sin\phi
$$
**No $\psi$ anywhere**: turning about the local vertical doesn't change how the mass is spread along it.

**Compact vector form ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=28|s. 28]]):** with $\mathbf n$ = the nadir unit vector in body axes (third column of $[A]$):
$$
\mathbf G = \frac{3\mu}{R_0^3}\,\mathbf n\times[I]\mathbf n
$$
Easy to code in a simulator: no Euler angles needed. If $\mathbf n$ is along a principal axis, $[I]\mathbf n \parallel \mathbf n$ and $\mathbf G = 0$: **every principal axis pointing at the Earth is an equilibrium** (stable or not is the question below).

**Linearized ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]):** small $\phi, \theta$, circular orbit, $\omega_o^2 = \mu/R_0^3$, $\sin2\alpha \approx 2\alpha$:
$$
G_x = 3\omega_o^2(I_z - I_y)\phi, \qquad G_y = 3\omega_o^2(I_z - I_x)\theta, \qquad G_z = 0
$$
> [!tip] Question 4: why can gravity gradient not restore a yaw error?
> Yaw turns about the local vertical; to first order that doesn't change the mass distribution along the vertical, so no torque. **Any yaw stiffness must come from the orbital motion** (the $\omega_o$ coupling of L8), and it shows up in the yaw equation below.

### Building the linearized equations ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30–34]])
Ingredients:
1. L8 kinematics: $\boldsymbol\omega \approx (\dot\phi - \omega_o\psi,\ \dot\theta - \omega_o,\ \dot\psi + \omega_o\phi)$.
2. The linearized $\mathbf G$ (now **inside** the plant: it depends on the attitude, so it's a spring, not an outside disturbance as in L6; $\mathbf T_d$ from now on = only drag, solar, magnetic).
3. The wheel momenta, including a possible **momentum bias** $h_{wyo}$.

Keep only first-order terms in angles, rates and wheel momentum changes.

**Momentum bias ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]]):** a wheel that spins all the time along $Y_B$ (normal to the orbit plane): $h_{wy} = I_{wy}\omega_{wy} + h_{wyo}$. A wheel at constant speed gives **no torque**, but its momentum **resists being turned** (gyroscopic stiffness) → roll and yaw stiffness with no control action. Geostationary comsats fly this way (L13, E2).
![[R7026R L9 s31 momentum bias wheel.png]]
*Bias wheel along $Y_B$, perpendicular to the orbit plane. With $h_{wyo} = -h$, $h > 0$, its momentum points along the orbit normal ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]]).*

**Where $4\omega_o^2$ comes from, roll axis ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]):** roll line of Euler's equation, $T_{cx} + T_{dx} + G_x = I_x\dot\omega_x + (I_z - I_y)\omega_y\omega_z$:
1. $I_x\dot\omega_x = I_x\ddot\phi - I_x\omega_o\dot\psi$.
2. $\omega_y \approx -\omega_o$, $\omega_z = \dot\psi + \omega_o\phi$ ⇒ $(I_z - I_y)\omega_y\omega_z \approx \omega_o(I_y - I_z)\dot\psi + \mathbf{1}\cdot\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi$.
3. Move $G_x$ to the right: $-G_x = \mathbf 3\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi$.

Sum: $T_{cx} + T_{dx} = I_x\ddot\phi + \mathbf 4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi + \omega_o(I_y - I_z - I_x)\dot\psi$.

![[R7026R L9 s34 roll stiffness 3 plus 1.png]]
*Looking along the flight direction: gravity gradient pulls $Z_B$ back to nadir (the 3); the orbit rate along $-Y_R$ has a component $\omega_o\phi$ on $Z_B$ (the 1) ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=34|s. 34]]).*

> [!important] 4 = 3 + 1
> Three parts gravity gradient, one part orbital motion through $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$. In yaw ($G_z = 0$) the orbital part is the **whole** stiffness, $\omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)\psi$. Pitch has no orbital part, so it keeps $3\omega_o^2$.

### The full linearized equations ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35–37]])
**Roll ($X_B$, about the velocity: the footprint moves across the ground track):**
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{dx} + T_{cx} = {}& I_x\ddot\phi + 4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi + \omega_o(I_y - I_z - I_x)\dot\psi \\
&+ \dot h_{wx} - \omega_oh_{wz} - \dot\psi h_{wyo} - \phi\omega_oh_{wyo} \\
&- I_{xy}\ddot\theta - I_{xz}\ddot\psi - I_{xz}\omega_o^2\psi + 2I_{yz}\omega_o\dot\theta
\end{aligned}
$$
**Pitch ($Y_B$, in the orbit plane: the footprint moves along the track):**
$$
T_{dy} + T_{cy} = I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta + \dot h_{wy} - I_{xy}(\ddot\phi - 2\omega_o\dot\psi - \omega_o^2\phi) + I_{yz}(-\ddot\psi - 2\omega_o\dot\phi + \omega_o^2\psi)
$$
**Yaw ($Z_B$, about the local vertical: the nadir point stays, the image rotates):**
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{dz} + T_{cz} = {}& I_z\ddot\psi + \omega_o(I_z + I_x - I_y)\dot\phi + \omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)\psi \\
&+ \dot h_{wz} + \omega_oh_{wx} + \dot\phi h_{wyo} - \psi\omega_oh_{wyo} \\
&- I_{yz}\ddot\theta - I_{xz}\ddot\phi - 2\omega_oI_{xy}\dot\theta - \omega_o^2I_{xz}\phi
\end{aligned}
$$
Three kinds of terms in each: **inertia/gravity gradient** (line 1), **wheels** (line 2), **products of inertia** (line 3). The wheel terms are carried for later: L11 damps a GG satellite with a wheel, L13 uses momentum bias.

**Principal axes, no wheels ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 38–39]]):**
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
T_x &= I_x\ddot\phi + 4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi + \omega_o(I_y - I_z - I_x)\dot\psi \\
T_y &= I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta \\
T_z &= I_z\ddot\psi + \omega_o(I_z + I_x - I_y)\dot\phi + \omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)\psi
\end{aligned}}
$$
**Pitch stands alone** (no $\phi$, $\psi$); **roll and yaw are coupled** through $\omega_o$. Exactly the coupling L8 predicted from the kinematics.

### Purely passive gravity-gradient stabilization ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=40|s. 40–41]])
No control ($T_c = 0$, $h_w = 0$): motion only from the initial angles/rates and the disturbances. A typical design: a **boom with a tip mass** along $Z_B$ makes $I_z$ small.

Divide by the inertias and define
$$
\sigma_x = \frac{I_y - I_z}{I_x}, \qquad \sigma_y = \frac{I_x - I_z}{I_y}, \qquad \sigma_z = \frac{I_y - I_x}{I_z}
$$

### Pitch stability ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=42|s. 42–44]])
$I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta = 0$ ⇒ $s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y = 0$ (L2's gravity-gradient pendulum).
- $\sigma_y > 0$ ($I_x > I_z$): $s = \pm j\omega_\theta$, $\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$ → **simply stable**, undamped libration.
- $\sigma_y < 0$: one real positive root → **tumbles**.

![[R7026R L9 s43 long satellite hang.png]]
*Question 7: a long thin satellite (least inertia about the long axis) must hang with that axis at the Earth ($Z_B$). Pointed along the velocity it tumbles in pitch ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43]]).*

![[R7026R L9 s44 pitch libration simulation.png]]
*$I = (80, 82, 4)$ kg m² at 800 km: a 60 min libration that never decays. A constant $T_{dy} = 10^{-5}$ N m swings it between 0 and 4.7° ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44]]).*

Why 0 to 4.7°: a constant torque shifts the equilibrium to $\theta_{ss} = T_{dy}/(3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)) = 2.33°$; starting from 0, an undamped oscillator swings symmetrically about the new equilibrium, so it peaks at $2\theta_{ss} = 4.66°$.

### Roll–yaw stability ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=45|s. 45–51]])
$$
\ddot\phi + 4\omega_o^2\sigma_x\phi - \omega_o(1 - \sigma_x)\dot\psi = \frac{T_{dx}}{I_x}, \qquad \ddot\psi + \omega_o^2\sigma_z\psi + \omega_o(1 - \sigma_z)\dot\phi = \frac{T_{dz}}{I_z}
$$
Characteristic equation (Laplace, $T_d = 0$):
$$
s^4 + \omega_o^2(1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z)s^2 + 4\omega_o^4\sigma_x\sigma_z = 0
$$
Only even powers ⇒ if $s$ is a root, so is $-s$. A root in the left half plane means a mirror root in the right half plane. **So the best possible is simply stable**: all four roots on the imaginary axis ⇔ both solutions for $s^2$ real and negative:
1. $1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z > 4\sqrt{\sigma_x\sigma_z}$ (real roots for $s^2$: discriminant > 0),
2. $\sigma_x\sigma_z > 0$ (product of the $s^2$ roots positive: same sign),
3. $1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z > 0$ (sum negative: both negative).

Plus **pitch**: $I_x > I_z$, which combined with the triangle inequality $I_y < I_x + I_z$ gives $\sigma_x > \sigma_z$ ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=49|s. 49]]). And always $|\sigma_i| < 1$ ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=46|s. 46]]): e.g. $\sigma_x = \int(z^2 - y^2)dm / \int(z^2 + y^2)dm$.

![[R7026R L9 s51 gravity gradient stability regions.png]]
*Stability regions in the $\sigma_x$–$\sigma_z$ plane (Sidi Fig. 5.3.1). Quadrants II and IV fail $\sigma_x\sigma_z > 0$; only points below $\sigma_x = \sigma_z$ have stable pitch. **Region A** ($I_y > I_x > I_z$, quadrant I) is the one used in practice; **region B** (in quadrant III, $I_x > I_z > I_y$) is mathematically stable but rarely used ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=51|s. 51]]).*

> [!tip] Reading region A in words
> $I_y > I_x > I_z$: **largest inertia about the orbit normal** (pitch axis), **smallest about the local vertical** (the boom along $Z_B$). Like a pendulum swinging in the orbit plane, with the long axis hanging down.

### State-space view ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=52|s. 52–55]])
**Pitch**, $x = [\theta\ \dot\theta]^T$:
$$
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -3\omega_o^2\sigma_y & 0 \end{bmatrix}x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I_y \end{bmatrix}T_y, \qquad \det(s1 - A) = s^2 + 3\omega_o^2\sigma_y
$$
Eigenvalues = the poles found by hand. Lecture 11 adds the damping that makes them move into the left half plane.

**Roll–yaw**, $x = [\phi\ \dot\phi\ \psi\ \dot\psi]^T$:
$$
\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -4\omega_o^2\sigma_x & 0 & 0 & \omega_o(1 - \sigma_x) \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -\omega_o(1 - \sigma_z) & -\omega_o^2\sigma_z & 0 \end{bmatrix}x + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1/I_x & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/I_z \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} T_x \\ T_z \end{bmatrix}
$$
Its characteristic polynomial is the quartic above. Checking eigenvalues on a grid of $(\sigma_x, \sigma_z)$ reproduces the stability regions with no algebra:
![[R7026R L9 s54 stability map eigenvalues.png]]
*Gray = all eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. Quadrant I plus a thin sliver in quadrant III (region B). The cross is the $I = (80, 82, 4)$ example ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54]]).*

**One torque for two axes? ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=55|s. 55]])** With only a roll torque, $B_x = [0\ 1/I_x\ 0\ 0]^T$: rank$[B_x\ AB_x\ A^2B_x\ A^3B_x] = 4$ → **controllable** (L3). Dually, measuring only roll, $C = [1\ 0\ 0\ 0]$, the system is **observable**: yaw can be estimated from roll. The orbital coupling carries roll actions into yaw and yaw motion into roll. It's slow (time scale one orbit), but it's the principle behind **momentum-bias control without a yaw sensor** (L13, E2).

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Principal axes:** $\det([I] - \lambda1) = 0$, $[I]\mathbf e_i = \lambda_i\mathbf e_i$, $[I'] = [A]^T[I][A]$ ($[A]$ = eigenvectors as columns).

**Euler's equation with wheels**
$$
\mathbf T_c + \mathbf T_d = \dot{\mathbf h} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h, \qquad \mathbf h = [I]\boldsymbol\omega + \mathbf h_w
$$
**Principal axes, no wheels:** $M_x = I_x\dot\omega_x + \omega_y\omega_z(I_z - I_y)$ and cyclic.

**Gravity gradient** ($\mathbf n$ = nadir in body axes, $\omega_o^2 = \mu/R_0^3$)
$$
\mathbf G = 3\omega_o^2\,\mathbf n\times[I]\mathbf n; \qquad G_x = 3\omega_o^2(I_z - I_y)\phi,\quad G_y = 3\omega_o^2(I_z - I_x)\theta,\quad G_z = 0
$$
**Linearized, principal axes, no wheels**
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_x &= I_x\ddot\phi + 4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)\phi + \omega_o(I_y - I_z - I_x)\dot\psi \\
T_y &= I_y\ddot\theta + 3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)\theta \\
T_z &= I_z\ddot\psi + \omega_o(I_z + I_x - I_y)\dot\phi + \omega_o^2(I_y - I_x)\psi
\end{aligned}
$$
**Inertia ratios:** $\sigma_x = \frac{I_y - I_z}{I_x}$, $\sigma_y = \frac{I_x - I_z}{I_y}$, $\sigma_z = \frac{I_y - I_x}{I_z}$.

**Pitch:** stable iff $I_x > I_z$; $\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\sigma_y}$; constant torque: $\theta_{ss} = \frac{T_{dy}}{3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)}$.

**Roll–yaw:** $s^4 + \omega_o^2(1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z)s^2 + 4\omega_o^4\sigma_x\sigma_z = 0$; stable iff $1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z > 4\sqrt{\sigma_x\sigma_z}$, $\sigma_x\sigma_z > 0$. All three axes: also $\sigma_x > \sigma_z$.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=52|s. 44, 52–53]]) A gravity-gradient satellite with principal inertias $I = (80, 82, 4)$ kg m² in a circular orbit at 800 km. (a) Is it stable in pitch and in roll–yaw? (b) Find the pitch libration period and the two roll–yaw frequencies. (c) A constant pitch disturbance $T_{dy} = 10^{-5}$ N m acts. What is the pitch offset and the peak angle if it starts at $\theta = 0$?

**Solution:**
1. *Orbit rate:* $R_0 = 7178$ km, $\omega_o = \sqrt{3.986\cdot10^{14}/(7.178\cdot10^6)^3} = 1.038\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s, orbit period $2\pi/\omega_o = 100.9$ min.
2. *Inertia ratios:*
$$
\sigma_x = \frac{82 - 4}{80} = 0.975, \qquad \sigma_y = \frac{80 - 4}{82} = 0.927, \qquad \sigma_z = \frac{82 - 80}{4} = 0.5
$$
3. *Pitch:* $I_x = 80 > I_z = 4$ ✓ stable. $\omega_\theta = \omega_o\sqrt{3\cdot0.927} = 1.667\,\omega_o$ → period $100.9/1.667 = 60.5$ min.
4. *Roll–yaw conditions:*
   - $\sigma_x\sigma_z = 0.4875 > 0$ ✓
   - $1 + 3\sigma_x + \sigma_x\sigma_z = 1 + 2.925 + 0.4875 = 4.4125$, and $4\sqrt{0.4875} = 2.793$: $4.41 > 2.79$ ✓
   - $\sigma_x = 0.975 > \sigma_z = 0.5$ ✓ (consistent with pitch). Point lies in **region A**.
5. *Roll–yaw frequencies:* $\left(\frac{s^2}{\omega_o^2}\right)^2 + 4.4125\frac{s^2}{\omega_o^2} + 1.95 = 0$:
$$
\frac{s^2}{\omega_o^2} = \frac{-4.4125 \pm \sqrt{19.47 - 7.80}}{2} = \frac{-4.4125 \pm 3.416}{2} = -0.498 \text{ or } -3.914
$$
   $s = \pm0.706j\,\omega_o$ (period 143 min) and $s = \pm1.978j\,\omega_o$ (period 51 min). Matches [[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=53|s. 53]] ✓. Two undamped roll–yaw modes.
6. *Constant pitch torque:* $\theta_{ss} = \dfrac{10^{-5}}{3(1.038\cdot10^{-3})^2(80 - 4)} = \dfrac{10^{-5}}{2.457\cdot10^{-4}} = 0.0407$ rad $= 2.33°$. No damping: from $\theta(0) = 0$ it oscillates between 0 and $2\theta_{ss} = 4.7°$ ✓ (the orange curve of [[Lecture_9_handout.pdf#page=44|s. 44]]).

- Q: Find the principal moments of $[I] = \begin{bmatrix} 20 & -10 \\ -10 & 30 \end{bmatrix}$ (the $xy$ block of Example 1) and the angle of $\mathbf e_1$ from $X_B$.

> [!example]- Answer
> $\lambda = 25 \pm \sqrt{5^2 + 10^2} = 25 \pm 11.18$ → $13.82$ and $36.18$ kg m². For $\lambda_1$: $(20 - 13.82)e_x - 10e_y = 0$ ⇒ $e_y = 0.618e_x$ ⇒ angle $= \arctan0.618 = 31.7°$.

- Q: Write Euler's equation with a wheel and say where the wheel torque enters.

> [!example]- Answer
> $\mathbf T_c + \mathbf T_d = \dot{\mathbf h} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ with $\mathbf h = [I]\boldsymbol\omega + \mathbf h_w$. The wheel torque is internal: it appears as $\dot{\mathbf h}_w$ on the right side. Speeding a wheel up ($\dot h_w > 0$) acts on the body like a torque $-\dot h_w$.

- Q: Why is the roll stiffness $4\omega_o^2(I_y - I_z)$ but the pitch stiffness only $3\omega_o^2(I_x - I_z)$?

> [!example]- Answer
> Both get $3\omega_o^2$ from gravity gradient. Roll gets one more $\omega_o^2$ from the orbital motion via $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf h$ (the orbit rate $-\omega_o$ about $Y$ has a component $\omega_o\phi$ on $Z_B$ when rolled). Pitch is about the orbit-rate axis itself, so it gets no such term.

- Q: A satellite has $I = (100, 60, 120)$ kg m². Can it be gravity-gradient stable in this orientation?

> [!example]- Answer
> Pitch needs $I_x > I_z$: $100 > 120$ fails → it tumbles in pitch. (Also $\sigma_x = (60 - 120)/100 = -0.6$, $\sigma_z = (60 - 100)/120 = -0.33$, $\sigma_x < \sigma_z$.) The axis of least inertia ($Y$, 60) should point at the Earth instead.

- Q: Why can't a passive gravity-gradient satellite be asymptotically stable?

> [!example]- Answer
> The characteristic polynomials have only even powers of $s$ (no damping terms), so roots come in pairs $\pm s$. Any root in the left half plane has a twin in the right half plane. The best possible is all roots on the imaginary axis: undamped libration. Damping must be added (L11).

- Q: Only a roll thruster is available. Can yaw still be controlled? Can a roll sensor alone tell you the yaw?

> [!example]- Answer
> Yes to both for the roll–yaw model: the controllability matrix with $B_x$ has rank 4 and the observability matrix with $C = [1\ 0\ 0\ 0]$ has rank 4. The orbital coupling $\omega_o(1 - \sigma)$ terms link the axes, slowly (time scale of one orbit).
