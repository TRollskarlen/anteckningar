**Question:** Given a mission's requirements, which attitude control system fits, how do you size it with first-cut numbers, and how do you report and defend the choice?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L9]], [[R7026R L10]], [[R7026R L11]], [[R7026R L12]], [[R7026R L13]] · **Leads to:** the exam, the projects

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
Start from the requirements (what points where, how accurately, how fast, which orbit, how long, with what mass/power/cost), then pick from the ladder: gravity gradient (10–30°, cheapest) → GG + magnetic damping (2–10°, LEO) → spin / dual spin → momentum bias (~0.05° roll, GEO comsats) → three-axis reaction wheels or CMGs (arcminutes–arcseconds, agile, most expensive). Every block of the loop (sensors, estimator, control law, actuators, momentum dumping) must be chosen, often with a second system for detumbling, ΔV or safe mode, and agility (not accuracy) usually sizes the wheels: rest-to-rest bang-bang needs $T = 4I\theta/t^2$, $h = 2I\theta/t$. A feasibility report traces every requirement to a number or a simulation with noise and actuator limits, lists the risks, and ends with one table, one choice and its price.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Course aim:** assess and report on the feasibility of different attitude control systems in different situations. L8–L13 built the tools one system at a time; a mission asks the reverse question.
- **First questions:** what must point where, how accurately, how fast it must turn, in which orbit, for how long, with how much mass, power and money.
- **The ladder of systems:** GG → GG + magnetic → spin → dual spin → momentum bias → reaction wheels/CMGs: accuracy, cost and complexity all rise.
- **Mission phases:** detumbling, ΔV burns, safe mode: one mission often needs two systems.
- **Agility sizes the wheels:** halving the slew time quadruples the torque.
- **Feasibility report:** requirements → candidates → models and numbers → simulation evidence → risks → comparison and recommendation.

*The six systems of L8–L13: gravity gradient, spin, dual spin, magnetic, momentum bias, reaction wheels ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=3|s. 3]]).*

Question 1: before choosing anything, ask **what must point where, how accurately, how fast it must turn, in which orbit, for how long, and with how much mass, power and money**.

### The systems side by side ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4]])
| system | points | typical accuracy | cost, power | main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| gravity gradient + passive damping | Earth (nadir) | 10–30° | lowest | weak, barely damped, no yaw stiffness (L9, L11) |
| gravity gradient + magnetic damping | Earth | 2–10° | low | LEO only (L11) |
| spin | one axis, inertial | nutation-limited | low | only one axis; major-axis rule (L12) |
| dual spin | antenna to Earth, rotor inertial | better than spin | medium | damper placement (L12) |
| momentum bias | Earth, wheel on orbit normal | roll ~0.05° with active control | medium | yaw only via coupling (L13) |
| three-axis reaction wheels (or CMGs) | anywhere, agile | arcminutes to arcseconds | highest | momentum dumping, jitter, CMG singularities (L10, L13) |

Orders of magnitude for a first choice; real designs vary widely.

### A checklist for a first choice ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]])
1. **Pointing target:** inertial (stars, Sun) or Earth? One axis or three?
2. **Accuracy and stability:** degrees, arcminutes or arcseconds? Jitter?
3. **Agility:** how large and how fast are the slews?
4. **Orbit:** LEO (magnetic field, gravity gradient, drag) or GEO / deep space?
5. **Budgets:** mass, power, volume, cost; lifetime and reliability (moving parts).
6. **Phases:** detumbling, ΔV burns, safe mode: one mission often needs two systems.

![[R7026R L14 s5 every block must be chosen.png]]
*The whole loop with the lectures that cover each block: control law (PD, LQR, B-dot, quaternion feedback: L11–L13), actuators (wheels, magnetorquers, thrusters: L10, L13), spacecraft (Euler + kinematics: L8, L9), sensors and estimator (TRIAD, Kalman filter: L10) ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=5|s. 5]]).*

**The answer is rarely one actuator: every block of this loop has to be chosen.**

### Case 1: CubeSat taking wide-field Earth images ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]])
**Requirements:** LEO, camera roughly at the Earth (~5°), tiny mass and power budget, low cost.

**Choice (Question 2):**
- Magnetometer and coarse Sun sensors; three magnetorquers.
- B-dot detumbling after deployment (L10), then magnetic pointing (E1); one small wheel as a momentum bias improves accuracy (L13).
- Alternative: a gravity-gradient boom with magnetic damping, if 5–10° is enough and a deployable fits (L9, L11).

![[R7026R L14 s6 cubesat ADCS.png]]
*3U CubeSat: magnetometer on a short boom, coarse Sun sensors, three magnetorquers (x, y, z), camera to nadir; or a gravity-gradient boom ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]).*

Cheap, robust, no fuel, but slow, and only where the Earth's field is strong.

### Case 2: Agile Earth-observation satellite ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7–8]])
**Requirements:** LEO, point at any target within seconds to a minute, arcminute accuracy, stable during each image.

**Choice (Question 3):**
- Four reaction wheels, skewed (or CMGs for the most agile, L13).
- Star trackers + gyros in a Kalman filter (L7, L10); quaternion-error control for large slews (L13).
- Magnetorquers to dump wheel momentum (L10, L13, E1).

![[R7026R L14 s7 agile imager ADCS.png]]

The most capable and most expensive system: every element of L8–L13 appears in it.

**Sizing the wheels ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]):** rest-to-rest bang-bang (accelerate half the time, brake half; the time-optimal slew of E1). Half the angle in half the time with constant $\ddot\theta = T/I$: $\frac\theta2 = \frac12\frac TI\left(\frac t2\right)^2$, and the peak rate $\dot\theta_{max} = \frac TI\frac t2$, so
$$
T = \frac{4I\theta}{t^2}, \qquad h = I\dot\theta_{max} = \frac{2I\theta}{t}
$$
![[R7026R L14 s8 slew time costs torque and momentum.png]]
*Wheel torque ($\propto 1/t^2$) and momentum ($\propto 1/t$) for a 30° roll of $I_x = 1000$ kg m², vs slew time ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]).*

- 60 s: 0.58 N m and 17 N m s; 120 s: 0.15 N m and 8.7 N m s. **Halving the slew time quadruples the torque.**
- L13 flew this slew: four 0.2 N m wheels (0.35 N m in roll) need 79 s; four CMGs did it in 33 s, limited by momentum.
- Disturbance $2\cdot10^{-5}$ N m → 0.11 N m s per 95-min orbit; magnetic unloading needs ~1.3 A m² in a 30 µT field ($T_d/B$, doubled because on average only about half of $\mathbf M\times\mathbf B$ is usable).

**Agility, not accuracy, usually sizes the wheels.**

### Case 3: Geostationary communication satellite ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]])
**Requirements:** antennas on the Earth to ~0.05° in roll, 15 years, no star tracker, no gyros in routine operation.

**Choice (Question 4):**
- **Momentum bias:** one wheel along the orbit normal; Earth sensor for roll and pitch (L13).
- Yaw through the roll–yaw coupling, no yaw sensor.
- Thrusters (or solar torques) dump momentum: at GEO the field is only ~0.1 µT and lies along the pitch axis, so magnetorquers can't unload the wheel (E2).

![[R7026R L14 s9 GEO comsat momentum bias.png]]
*Bias wheel $\mathbf h$ along the pitch axis = orbit normal, Earth sensor (roll, pitch), thrusters for dumping (example: NASA's TDRS, [[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9]]).*

Simple, long-lived, few sensors: the classic choice for GEO comsats.

### Case 4: Spinning probe or upper stage ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=10|s. 10]])
**Requirements:** keep one axis (thrust or antenna) fixed in inertial space through long cruises and ΔV burns; simple and robust.

**Choice (Question 5):**
- **Spin stabilization about the major axis**, with a nutation damper (L12).
- Spin rate from the allowed nutation during burns ($\theta_{av} \propto 1/\omega_z^2$), traded against spin-up and despin fuel.
- Deep-space probes have flown this way (Juno).

![[R7026R L14 s10 spinning probe.png]]

A minor-axis spinner with dampers on board repeats Explorer 1.

### Case 5: Space telescope ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=11|s. 11]])
**Requirements:** hold a star field to a fraction of an arcsecond for long exposures; slew between targets.

**What limits the pointing now? (Question 6)**
- Reaction wheels and gyros, with fine-guidance sensors on the instrument (Hubble).
- **The wheels' own imbalance becomes the disturbance:** a sinusoid at the wheel speed (L6) → isolators and speed planning (the error budget: E2).
- **Solar arrays flex:** their vibration modes limit the control bandwidth (beyond this course).

**The control loop is no longer the hard part; the noise and disturbance budget is.**

### Reporting a feasibility assessment ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13–14]])
For each candidate system:
1. Which requirement **drives** the choice (accuracy, agility, lifetime, orbit)?
2. A first-cut **model and numbers**: inertia, disturbances, actuator and sensor sizes (L9–L11, L13).
3. A **simulation** showing the requirement is met, with noise and actuator limits (MATLAB/Simulink).
4. **What could go wrong:** saturation, singularities, sensor outages, unobservable states, energy dissipation.
5. **Cost, mass and power** against the alternatives.

**Report outline ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]):**

| Outline | What a reader checks |
|---|---|
| 1. Requirements with numbers; mark the one that drives the design | every requirement traced to a number and to a plot or calculation |
| 2. Two or three candidate systems, each with a sketch of sensors and actuators | assumptions, units and data sources stated |
| 3. Models and first-cut numbers | each figure says what it shows and what it proves |
| 4. Simulation evidence with noise and actuator limits | limits of the model admitted (linearization, rigid body, idealized sensors) |
| 5. Risks: saturation, singularities, outages, failures | a recommendation that follows from the comparison |
| 6. Comparison and recommendation: one table, one choice, its price | |

The project reports follow the same logic.

**Spacecraft (L8–L13):**
- DCM, Euler angles and quaternions: convert, combine two rotations ([[R7026R L8]])

- spin: nutation frequency, stability with dissipation, despin; the dual-spin condition ([[R7026R L12]])

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Rest-to-rest bang-bang slew** (angle $\theta$, time $t$, inertia $I$)
$$
T = \frac{4I\theta}{t^2}, \qquad h_{max} = \frac{2I\theta}{t}
$$
**Momentum from a constant disturbance:** $\Delta h = T_dt$ (per orbit: $T_d\cdot T_{orb}$).

**Magnetic unloading dipole (rule of thumb):** $M \approx 2T_d/B$.

**First-choice accuracies:** GG + passive 10–30°, GG + magnetic 2–10°, momentum bias ~0.05° roll (active), wheels arcmin–arcsec.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_14_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]) An agile imager ($I_x = 1000$ kg m²) must roll 30° rest-to-rest. (a) What wheel torque and momentum does a 60 s slew need, and a 120 s slew? (b) A constant disturbance of $2\cdot10^{-5}$ N m acts in a 95 min orbit: how much momentum per orbit, and what magnetorquer dipole unloads it in a 30 µT field? (c) Four 0.2 N m wheels give 0.35 N m in roll: what is the fastest bang-bang slew?

**Solution:**
1. *60 s:* $\theta = 30° = 0.5236$ rad.
$$
T = \frac{4\cdot1000\cdot0.5236}{60^2} = \frac{2094}{3600} = 0.58\ \text{N m}, \qquad h = \frac{2\cdot1000\cdot0.5236}{60} = 17.5\ \text{N m s}
$$
2. *120 s:* $T = 2094/14400 = 0.145$ N m (¼), $h = 1047/120 = 8.7$ N m s (½).
3. *Disturbance:* $\Delta h = 2\cdot10^{-5}\cdot95\cdot60 = 0.114$ N m s per orbit. Dipole: $T_d/B = 2\cdot10^{-5}/3\cdot10^{-5} = 0.67$ A m², doubled because only about half of $\mathbf M\times\mathbf B$ helps on average → ~1.3 A m².
4. *Fastest slew with 0.35 N m:* $t = \sqrt{4I\theta/T} = \sqrt{2094/0.35} = \sqrt{5984} = 77$ s ≈ the 78 s bang-bang time quoted in L13 (79 s with the real rate-limited law). Momentum at mid-slew: $2\cdot1000\cdot0.5236/77 = 13.6$ N m s; check that the wheels can store it.

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: A 3U CubeSat must point its camera to ~5° in LEO, on a tiny budget. What would you fly?

> [!example]- Answer
> Magnetometer + coarse Sun sensors + three magnetorquers: B-dot detumbling, then magnetic pointing, optionally a small bias wheel. Or a GG boom with magnetic damping if 5–10° is enough and a deployable fits. Cheap, no fuel, works in LEO where the field is strong.

- Q: Why do GEO comsats use thrusters, not magnetorquers, to dump the bias wheel?

> [!example]- Answer
> At GEO the field is only ~0.1 µT and lies along the pitch axis (the wheel axis), and $\mathbf M\times\mathbf B$ is never along $\mathbf B$, so the pitch-wheel momentum can't be removed magnetically.

- Q: Halving the slew time of a rest-to-rest maneuver changes torque and momentum by what factors?

> [!example]- Answer
> Torque ×4 ($T \propto 1/t^2$), momentum ×2 ($h \propto 1/t$). Agility usually sizes the wheels.

- Q: A probe will spin for a long cruise with propellant sloshing in its tanks. What must you check first?

> [!example]- Answer
> That it spins about its **major** axis. With energy dissipation only major-axis spin is stable; a minor-axis spinner would end up tumbling (Explorer 1). Then size the spin rate for the ΔV-burn nutation ($\theta_{av} \propto 1/\omega_z^2$) against spin-up/despin fuel.

- Q: For a space telescope with arcsecond-class pointing, what becomes the limiting factor?

> [!example]- Answer
> Not the control law, but the disturbance and noise budget: wheel imbalance (a sinusoid at the wheel speed), sensor noise, and flexible solar-array modes that limit the bandwidth. Isolators, speed planning and fine-guidance sensors handle it.

- Q: List the six parts of a feasibility report.

> [!example]- Answer
> (1) Requirements with numbers, mark the driving one; (2) 2–3 candidate systems with sketches; (3) models and first-cut numbers; (4) simulation evidence with noise and actuator limits; (5) risks; (6) comparison table, one recommendation, its price.
