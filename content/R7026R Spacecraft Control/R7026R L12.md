**Question:** Why does a spinning body hold its axis, when is spin about an axis stable (rigid vs with energy dissipation), how is spin used during a ΔV burn and removed afterwards, and how are nutation dampers and dual-spin satellites designed?
**Builds on:** [[R7026R L9]], [[R7026R L4]], [[R7026R L2]], [[R7026R L5]] · **Leads to:** [[R7026R L13]], [[R7026R L14]]

## Summary
<!-- AFTER: answer the Question in my own words, max 3 sentences -->
With no torque, a spinning axisymmetric body's transverse rate circles the spin axis at $\lambda = \omega_z(I_z - I_x)/I_x$ (nutation), and a rigid body spins stably about its major or minor axis but not the intermediate one; once energy dissipates while $\mathbf h$ stays fixed, **only the major axis is stable** (Explorer 1). During a ΔV burn spin averages out thrust-misalignment torques, with $\theta_{av} = \frac{4T_d}{\pi\omega_z^2I_z|I_z/I_x - 1|} \propto 1/\omega_z^2$; afterwards thrusters despin ($m = h_z/(gI_{sp}\Delta z)$) and denutate with bang-bang logic, and the fuel depends on the momentum and the arm, not on the thrust. Passive wheel dampers work only for major-axis spin, an active motor-driven wheel works for either axis, and a dual-spin satellite (fast rotor + slow despun platform) needs its damper where $\dot T_P/\lambda_P + \dot T_R/\lambda_R < 0$, i.e. on the platform when $I_R < I_T$.

## Roadmap
<!-- Read this first. ⭐ = core: you must be able to do it by hand (exam, projects). No star = understand the idea. (extra) = example or background, skim it. -->
**Why this lecture?** Spinning is the oldest and cheapest way to hold an axis: the stored momentum makes it stiff. But which axis is stable depends on energy dissipation (the Explorer 1 lesson). The spin must be removed after the ΔV burn, which costs fuel. And the wobble (nutation) must be damped: passively only for major-axis spin, actively for any axis (Project 1). Dual spin combines a spinning rotor with a platform that points.

**The storyline**
1. ⭐ *Torque-free axisymmetric spinner*: the transverse rate turns about the spin axis at $\lambda = \omega_z(I_z - I_x)/I_x$ (body nutation rate).
2. ⭐ *Stability of spin about each axis (rigid body)*: major and minor axes are stable, the intermediate axis is not.
3. *Nutation, body and space cones*: the nutation angle $\tan\theta = \frac{I_x\omega_{xy}}{I_z\omega_z}$.
4. ⭐ *Energy dissipation decides*: with $\mathbf h$ fixed, only the major axis is stable ($\dot T$ formula, Explorer 1).
5. *Spin during the ΔV stage*: thrust misalignment and the ΔV loss.
6. ⭐ *Nutation from a body-fixed torque*: $\theta_{av} \propto 1/\omega_z^2$, which is how you choose the spin rate.
7. ⭐ *Despin and denutation with thrusters*: time and fuel. The fuel does not depend on the thrust, and bang-bang gives only $(4/\pi)F\Delta x$ on average.
8. *Nutation damping in the mission orbit*: the passive wheel damper's root locus shows that it only works for major-axis spin.
9. ⭐ *Active wheel nutation damping*: DC motor model, transfer function, $e_M = -K\omega_x$. One gyro gives stability, the full state gives performance (Project 1).
10. ⭐ *Dual spin*: the damper must sit where $\dot T_P/\lambda_P + \dot T_R/\lambda_R < 0$, i.e. on the platform when $I_R < I_T$.

## Key concepts
<!-- One line each: what it is + why it matters -->
- **Spin stabilization:** a spinning body keeps its spin axis fixed in inertial space unless a torque acts perpendicular to it (gyroscopic stiffness).
- **Nutation:** the spin axis $Z_B$ coning around the fixed $\mathbf h$ at a constant angle $\theta$; seen from the body, $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ turns at $\lambda$.
- **Body cone / space cone:** the body cone (fixed in the body, around $Z_B$) rolls on the space cone (fixed in space, around $\mathbf h$).
- **Major / minor / intermediate axis rule:** rigid spin stable about the largest and smallest inertia axes, unstable about the intermediate one (tennis racket / Dzhanibekov effect).
- **Energy-sink rule:** with $\mathbf h$ fixed, $T = h^2/(2I)$ is smallest for the major axis, so dissipation drives any spin there. Only major-axis spin is stable with dissipation.
- **Thrust misalignment torque:** $T_d = Fd_{cm}\sin\beta$; excites nutation and costs $\Delta V_{loss} = \Delta V(1 - \cos\theta_{av})$.
- **Despin / denutation:** removing $h_z$ and $h_{xy}$ with thrusters; fuel $\propto$ momentum / (arm · $I_{sp}$).
- **Bang-bang denutation:** $T_{cx} = -\operatorname{sign}(\omega_x)F\Delta x$, same for $y$; average effective torque only $(4/\pi)F\Delta x$.
- **Wheel nutation damper:** a wheel on $Y_B$ in viscous fluid (passive) or driven by a DC motor (active).
- **Dual spin:** fast rotor (stiffness) + slow platform (points the antenna). Damper placement from $\dot T_P/\lambda_P + \dot T_R/\lambda_R < 0$.
- **Sidi notation:** $I_S$ spin-axis inertia, $I_T$ transverse, $\omega_S$ spin rate, $\alpha_S = -\lambda$; dual spin: $I_R$ rotor, $\lambda_P$, $\lambda_R$ nutation rates seen from platform and rotor.

### Torque-free axisymmetric spinner ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=4|s. 4–8]])
Axisymmetric ($I_x = I_y$), spinning about $Z_B$ at $\omega_z = n$, small transverse rate $\omega_{xy}$, no torque. Euler's equations:
$$
I_x\dot\omega_x + \omega_yn(I_z - I_y) = 0, \qquad I_y\dot\omega_y + \omega_xn(I_x - I_z) = 0, \qquad I_z\dot\omega_z = 0
$$
Third: $\omega_z = n$ constant. Define $\lambda = n\frac{I_z - I_x}{I_x}$:
$$
\dot\omega_x + \lambda\omega_y = 0, \qquad \dot\omega_y - \lambda\omega_x = 0
$$
Eliminate $\omega_y$: $\ddot\omega_x + \lambda^2\omega_x = 0$ ⇒
$$
\omega_x(t) = \omega_x(0)\cos\lambda t + \frac{\dot\omega_x(0)}{\lambda}\sin\lambda t, \qquad \omega_y(t) = \omega_x(0)\sin\lambda t - \frac{\dot\omega_x(0)}{\lambda}\cos\lambda t
$$
(Backup [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=69|s. 69]]: $\dot\omega_x(0) = -\lambda\omega_y(0)$.) Seen from the body, $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ **keeps its length and turns about $Z_B$ at rate $\lambda$**.
![[R7026R L12 s6 transverse rate circles the spin axis.png]]
*The transverse plane seen along the spin axis: $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ turns by $\lambda t$ ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=6|s. 6]]).*

**Same thing with $e^{At}$ (L2, L5; [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=7|s. 7]]):**
$$
\dot x = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\lambda \\ \lambda & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_A x \ \Rightarrow\ e^{At} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\lambda t & -\sin\lambda t \\ \sin\lambda t & \cos\lambda t \end{bmatrix}
$$
Eigenvalues $\pm i\lambda$: marginally stable. $e^{At}$ is a **rotation** by $\lambda t$. Question 1: $|\omega_{xy}|$ never changes because $A^T = -A$ ⇒ $(e^{At})^Te^{At} = e^{-At}e^{At} = I$: a rotation keeps lengths. (Complex form, backup: $\omega_{xy}(t) = [\omega_x(0) + j\omega_y(0)]e^{j\lambda t}$.)

**Example (Question 2, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=8|s. 8]]):** $n = 60$ rpm $= 2\pi$ rad/s, $I_z = 1000$, $I_x = I_y = 800$ kg m²: $\lambda = 2\pi\cdot\frac{200}{800} = \frac\pi2 \approx 1.57$ rad/s → $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ goes round once every $2\pi/\lambda = 4$ s = four spin periods.

### Stability of spin about each principal axis ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=9|s. 9–11]])
Now a box with three different inertias, spinning about $Z_B$: $\omega_z = n + \epsilon$, small $\epsilon, \omega_x, \omega_y$:
$$
I_x\dot\omega_x + \omega_yn(I_z - I_y) = 0, \quad I_y\dot\omega_y + \omega_xn(I_x - I_z) = 0, \quad I_z\dot\epsilon + \omega_x\omega_y(I_y - I_x) = 0
$$
Differentiate the first, use the second:
$$
\ddot\omega_x + n^2\frac{I_z - I_y}{I_y}\frac{I_z - I_x}{I_x}\omega_x = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ s^2 = -\beta^2, \quad \beta = n\left[\left(1 - \frac{I_z}{I_x}\right)\left(1 - \frac{I_z}{I_y}\right)\right]^{1/2}
$$
![[R7026R L12 s10 box with three principal axes.png]]

> [!important] Question 3: which axes can be stable?
> $\beta$ real (oscillation) needs both brackets with the same sign: $I_z > I_x, I_y$ (**major**) or $I_z < I_x, I_y$ (**minor**). Intermediate axis ($I_x > I_z > I_y$ or reverse): $s^2 > 0$, a positive root, it flips over. Try it with a book held shut; Dzhanibekov saw it with a wing nut in orbit (1985). **This assumes a rigid body with no dissipation.**

### Nutation, body and space cones ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=12|s. 12–13]])
Symmetric body, no torque: $\omega_z$ and $|\omega_{xy}|$ constant ⇒ $|\boldsymbol\omega|$ constant. $\mathbf h$ is constant in inertial space. Split: $\boldsymbol\omega = \boldsymbol\omega_{xy} + \boldsymbol\omega_z$, $\mathbf h = I_x\boldsymbol\omega_{xy} + I_z\boldsymbol\omega_z$. The transverse parts are parallel, so **$\boldsymbol\omega$, $\mathbf h$ and $Z_B$ lie in one plane**, which turns about the fixed $\mathbf h$.
![[R7026R L12 s13 body and space cones.png]]
*Left: $I_x < I_z$, a flat disc spinning about its major axis. Right: $I_x > I_z$, a pencil spinning about its minor axis (Sidi Figs. 4.6.1–4.6.2, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=13|s. 13]]).*

The body cone rolls on the space cone; seen from outside, $Z_B$ wobbles round $\mathbf h$ at the constant **nutation angle** $\theta$: $\tan\theta = \frac{I_x\omega_{xy}}{I_z\omega_z}$ (backup [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=73|s. 73]]).

### Energy dissipation decides ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14–17]])
For spin about a principal axis $\xi$: $T = \frac12I_\xi\omega^2 = \frac{h^2}{2I_\xi}$. With $\mathbf h$ fixed, every motion has $T$ between
$$
T_{max} = \frac{h^2}{2I_{min}} \quad\text{and}\quad T_{min} = \frac{h^2}{2I_{max}}
$$
Question 4: spinning about the minor axis = the **most** energy for that $h$. Dissipation lowers $T$ → the motion drifts toward the major axis ($T_{min}$).
![[R7026R L12 s14 minor axis spinner with fuel slosh.png]]
*Minor-axis spinner ($I_z = 40$, $I_x = 100$, $I_y = 140$, $\omega_z = 10$ rad/s) with sloshing fuel: the transverse rates grow every cycle (Sidi Fig. 6.3.2, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=14|s. 14]]).*

**Derivation ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=15|s. 15]]):** split $\mathbf h$ along and across $Z_B$: $2T = \frac{h^2\sin^2\theta}{I_x} + \frac{h^2\cos^2\theta}{I_z}$ ⇒ $2TI_x = h^2\left[1 - \cos^2\theta\left(1 - \frac{I_x}{I_z}\right)\right]$. Differentiate with $h$ constant:
$$
\dot T = \frac{h^2}{I_z}\cos\theta\sin\theta\left(\frac{I_z}{I_x} - 1\right)\dot\theta
$$
$\dot T < 0$ (dissipation): $I_z > I_x$ ⇒ $\dot\theta < 0$ (nutation decays, **stable**); $I_z < I_x$ ⇒ $\dot\theta > 0$ (**unstable**). **Only major-axis spin is stable.**

> [!example] Explorer 1, 1958 (Question 5, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=16|s. 16]])
> Spun about its long (minimum-inertia) axis, with four flexible whip antennas. The antennas flexed and dissipated energy while $h$ stayed constant; $\dot T < 0$ with $I_z < I_x$ ⇒ $\theta$ grew, and it ended up **tumbling end over end about its maximum-inertia axis**. Rigid-body theory said minor axis = stable; four flexible antennas said otherwise. **Spin about the major axis, or remove the energy sink.**
> ![[R7026R L12 s16 explorer 1 flat spin.png]]

**Simulation ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]):** $I_T = 100$, spin 1 rad/s, viscous wheel damper ($I_w = 1$, $D = 1$) on $Y_B$, full nonlinear equations.
![[R7026R L12 s17 damper major vs minor axis.png]]
*Major axis ($I_S = 120$): 2° nutation → 0.2° in half an hour. Minor axis ($I_S = 60$): the same damper drives it to ~90° within 100 min, a flat spin like Explorer 1. In both runs $|\mathbf h|$ stays constant to $10^{-7}$: **energy leaves, momentum does not** ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=17|s. 17]]).*

### Spin during the ΔV stage ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=19|s. 19–22]])
Spin protects the ΔV direction during high-thrust (apogee) burns: the axis resists parasitic torques, thrust misalignment averages out. (Example: the first Meteosat drum, 100 rpm.) Single spin limits antenna pointing → **dual spin** later.
![[R7026R L12 s20 spinning apogee burn.png]]
*A spinning spacecraft firing its apogee engine: a thrust error $\beta$ makes ΔV cone round $Z_B$ at the nutation angle $\theta$ (Sidi Fig. 6.2.1, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=20|s. 20]]).*

**Parasitic torque ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=21|s. 21]]):** engine $d_{cm}$ from the CM, thrust tilted by $\beta$ (mounting error, plume, CM shift as fuel burns):
$$
T_d = F\,d_{cm}\sin\beta
$$
about a lateral axis → nutation → ΔV off its intended direction.
![[R7026R L12 s21 thrust misalignment.png]]

**ΔV loss ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=22|s. 22]]):** only $\Delta V\cos\theta$ along $Z_B$ adds up; the coning part averages out:
$$
\Delta V_{loss} = \Delta V\left[1 - \cos\theta_{av}\right]
$$

### Nutation from a body-fixed torque ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23–26]])
Axisymmetric, $\omega_z = n$ constant, $T_d$ about $X_B$ (fixed in the body). Euler:
$$
h_x(t) = \frac{T_d}{\lambda}\sin\lambda t, \qquad h_y(t) = \frac{T_d}{\lambda}(1 - \cos\lambda t), \qquad \lambda = \omega_z\frac{I_z - I_x}{I_x}
$$
![[R7026R L12 s23 lateral momentum circle.png]]
*The tip of $\mathbf h_{xy}$ runs round a circle through the origin: lateral momentum never exceeds $2T_d/|\lambda|$ ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=23|s. 23]]).*

Magnitude and nutation angle ($1 - \cos\lambda t = 2\sin^2\frac{\lambda t}{2}$; magnitudes because $\lambda < 0$ for minor-axis spin):
$$
h_{xy} = \frac{\sqrt2T_d}{|\lambda|}\sqrt{1 - \cos\lambda t}, \qquad \theta(t) \approx \frac{h_{xy}}{h_z} = \frac{2T_d}{\omega_z^2I_z|I_z/I_x - 1|}\left|\sin\frac{\lambda t}{2}\right|
$$
Average of $|\sin\frac{\lambda t}{2}|$ over a period = $\frac{2}{\pi}$ ⇒
$$
\boxed{\theta_{av} = \frac{4}{\pi}\frac{T_d}{\omega_z^2I_z|I_z/I_x - 1|}}
$$
- $\theta_{av} \propto 1/\omega_z^2$: spin faster → much smaller nutation.
- $\theta_{av} \propto 1/(I_z|I_z/I_x - 1|)$: a bigger inertia difference helps.

![[R7026R L12 s25 nutation under body-fixed torque.png]]
*$I_x = I_y = 100$, $I_z = 40$ kg m², $T_d = 10$ N m, $\omega_z = 10$ rad/s: $\theta$ swings between 0 and 0.48°, average settles at 0.3° (Sidi Fig. 6.2.2, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=25|s. 25]]). At 4 rad/s: 1.9° (6.25× larger, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=29|s. 29]]).*

> [!tip] Why $1/\omega_z^2$? (Question 6, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=26|s. 26]])
> One factor from the denominator $h_z = I_z\omega_z$: more stored momentum, so the same lateral momentum tilts $\mathbf h$ less. One from the numerator $h_{xy} \le 2T_d/|\lambda|$ with $\lambda \propto \omega_z$: the body-fixed torque keeps turning, so its effect averages out faster. **The spin helps twice.**

**Design trade-off ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=30|s. 30]]):** more spin = more fuel to spin up, and as much again to despin later ($h_z = I_z\omega_z$). Balance ΔV pointing against spin-up/despin fuel.

### Despinning and denutation with thrusters ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31–40]])
After the transfer orbit the spacecraft goes three-axis: (1) **despin** (remove $h_z$), (2) **denutate** (remove $h_{xy}$). The laws below are time-optimal for damping, not fuel-optimal.
![[R7026R L12 s31 despin thruster layout.png]]
*Thruster pairs 1–4 and 3–6 torque the spin axis on the arm $\Delta z$; the others torque the transverse axes on the arm $\Delta x$ (Sidi Fig. 6.5.1, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=31|s. 31]]).*

**Despin ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=32|s. 32–34]]):** two thrusters against the spin: $\dot h_z = -2F\Delta z$ ⇒ $h_z(t) = h_z(0) - 2F\Delta z\,t$, until $h_z$ enters the dead zone (DZ).
![[R7026R L12 s32 despin to dead zone.png]]
$$
t_{DESP} = \frac{h_z(0)}{N_iF\Delta z}, \qquad m_{FUEL} = \frac{N_iFt_{DESP}}{gI_{sp}} = \frac{h_z(0)}{gI_{sp}\Delta z}
$$
(using $F = \dot m\,gI_{sp}$, neglecting DZ.) **Fuel depends only on $h_z(0)$, $I_{sp}$ and $\Delta z$, not on $F$ or $N_i$.**

> [!tip] Why doesn't the thrust level matter? (Question 8)
> Removing $h_z(0)$ needs a fixed angular impulse $\int T\,dt = h_z(0)$, i.e. a fixed linear impulse $\int N_iF\,dt = h_z(0)/\Delta z$. Propellant is paid per unit impulse: $m = \text{impulse}/(gI_{sp})$. $F$ and $N_i$ only set how fast it's delivered. To save fuel: longer arm or higher $I_{sp}$.

**Example 2 ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=35|s. 35]]):** $I_z = 100$, $\omega_z(0) = 10$ rad/s, two 5 N thrusters, $I_{sp} = 200$ s, $\Delta z = 1$ m: $t_{DESP} = \frac{1000}{2\cdot5\cdot1} = 100$ s, $m_{FUEL} = \frac{1000}{9.81\cdot200\cdot1} = 0.51$ kg.

**Denutation ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=36|s. 36–38]]):** while still spinning ($\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ turns at $\lambda$, which needs the spin). Rate gyros + bang-bang:
$$
T_{cx} = -\operatorname{sign}(\omega_x)F\Delta x, \qquad T_{cy} = -\operatorname{sign}(\omega_y)F\Delta x
$$
![[R7026R L12 s37 bang-bang denutation.png]]
*$\omega_x$, $\omega_y$ 90° out of phase and the thruster commands in the four quadrants (Sidi Fig. 6.5.3, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=37|s. 37]]).*

The two torques add to $\mathbf T_{xy}$ at 45°, but $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ keeps turning, so only the part along $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ works. Averaged over a quadrant: $T_{av} = \frac{1}{\pi/2}\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\cos\alpha\,\sqrt2F\Delta x\,d\alpha = \frac4\pi F\Delta x$ (not $\sqrt2F\Delta x$, and not $2F\Delta x$ → not fuel-optimal).
![[R7026R L12 s38 denutation torque angle.png]]
$$
t_{DENU} = \frac{h_{xy}(0)}{(4/\pi)F\Delta x}\cdot\frac{2}{N_i}, \qquad m_{DENU,TOT} = \frac{\pi h_{xy}(0)}{2gI_{sp}\Delta x}
$$

**Example 3 ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39–40]]):** $I_x = 200$, $\omega_{xy} = 2$ rad/s ⇒ $h_{xy} = 400$ N m s; two 5 N thrusters, 1 m: $t_{DENU} = \frac{\pi\cdot400}{4\cdot5\cdot1} = 62.8$ s, $m = \frac{\pi\cdot400}{2\cdot9.81\cdot200\cdot1} = 0.32$ kg. Sanity check (Question 9): $\dot m = F/(gI_{sp}) = 2.55\cdot10^{-3}$ kg/s per thruster; ×62.83 s = 0.16 kg each, **0.32 kg for two** (0.16 is a common slip).
![[R7026R L12 s39 despin and denutation simulation.png]]
*Simulated despin and denutation ($\omega_z(0) = 5$, $\omega_x(0) = 0.5$ rad/s, DZ 0.02 rad/s): $\omega_z$ falls linearly, the transverse rates die out (Sidi Fig. 6.5.5, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=39|s. 39]]).*

### Nutation damping in the mission orbit ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=42|s. 42–48]])
In the mission orbit the spin axis is precessed perpendicular to the orbit plane and held there. Disturbances perpendicular to it excite nutation → damp with the same passive methods as for GG (point-mass dampers, hysteresis rods, boom hinges) or a wheel.

**Passive wheel damper ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43–44]]):** wheel on $Y_B$ in viscous fluid. Nutation rocks the body about $Y_B$; the wheel lags, the relative speed $\Omega$ gives a viscous torque → energy out.
![[R7026R L12 s43 wheel nutation damper.png]]
*Wheel damper ($I_w$, relative speed $\Omega$) on a satellite spinning at $\omega_S$ (Sidi Fig. 6.6.1, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=43|s. 43]]).*

Model ($I_x = I_y = I_T$, $I_z = I_S$, wheel rate in inertial space $\eta = \omega_y + \Omega$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_T\dot\omega_x + (I_S - I_T)\omega_z\omega_y - I_w\omega_z\Omega &= 0 \\
I_T\dot\omega_y + I_w\dot\Omega + (I_T - I_S)\omega_z\omega_x &= 0 \\
I_w(\dot\omega_y + \dot\Omega) &= -D\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$
($I_S\dot\omega_z + I_w\omega_x\Omega = 0$: product of small rates, so $\omega_z = \omega_S$ stays constant.) **L4's nutation example and Project 1 use the same three equations with a motor instead of the fluid.**

Normalize with $\alpha_S = \omega_S\frac{I_T - I_S}{I_T}$ ($= -\lambda$), $\epsilon = I_w/I_T$, $\sigma = D/I_w$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix} s & -\alpha_S & -\epsilon\omega_S \\ \alpha_S & s & \epsilon s \\ 0 & s & s + \sigma \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \omega_x \\ \omega_y \\ \Omega \end{bmatrix} = 0
$$
**Root-locus form ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=46|s. 46–48]]):**
$$
1 + \frac{\sigma}{1 - \epsilon}\frac{s^2 + \alpha_S^2}{s(s^2 + \alpha_1^2)} = 0, \qquad \alpha_1 = \alpha_S\sqrt{1 + \frac{I_SI_w}{(I_S - I_T)(I_T - I_w)}}
$$
$I_S > I_T$ ⇒ $|\alpha_1| > |\alpha_S|$; $I_S < I_T$ ⇒ $|\alpha_1| < |\alpha_S|$. The order of pole and zero on the imaginary axis decides which way the locus bulges:
![[R7026R L12 s47 damper root loci major vs minor.png]]
*Major-axis spin (left): the branch bulges into the left half plane, damped. Minor-axis spin (right): it bulges into the right half plane, unstable (Sidi Fig. 6.6.2, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=47|s. 47]]).*

**Passive wheel damping works only for major-axis spin ($I_S > I_T$).** Question 10: same reason as Explorer 1: with $|\mathbf h|$ fixed, dissipation drives the motion to the lowest energy = spin about the largest inertia.

### Active wheel nutation damping ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=49|s. 49–56]])
To stabilize a minor-axis spinner: drive the wheel with a DC motor from a measured transverse rate (gyro). Motor: $T_M = Ni = I_w\dot\eta$, $e_M = Ri + N\Omega$ ($N = K_M = K_v$ in SI). Eliminate $i$:
$$
\dot\Omega + \dot\omega_y = -\gamma\Omega + \frac{N}{RI_w}e_M, \qquad \gamma = \frac{N^2}{RI_w}
$$
$e_M = 0$ (short-circuited motor) = the passive damper with $D = N^2/R$.
![[R7026R L12 s50 dc motor and wheel.png]]
*Motor and wheel; the reaction $-\dot h_T$ acts on the satellite (Sidi Fig. 6.6.3, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=50|s. 50]]).*

**Transfer function ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=51|s. 51]]):** Cramer's rule, then drop the $\epsilon$ terms for $I_w \ll I_T$:
$$
\frac{\omega_x(s)}{e_M(s)} \approx \frac{NI_S\omega_S}{RI_T^2}\frac{s}{(s^2 + \alpha_S^2)(s + \gamma)}
$$
Poles: nutation $\pm j\alpha_S$ and motor $-\gamma$; zero at 0.

**Control law ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=52|s. 52–53]]):** $e_M = -K\omega_x$, $K > 0$ (Sidi writes $+K$, but with these signs only $-K$ stabilizes). Loop: $L(s) = K'\frac{s}{(s^2 + \alpha_S^2)(s + \gamma)}$.
![[R7026R L12 s53 active wheel root locus.png]]
*Root locus of $L(s)$: stable for any $\alpha_S$ (either sign: major or minor axis) and any $K > 0$, **with the $\epsilon$ terms dropped**. Exactly, a minor-axis spin starts unstable (the motor resistance is already a damper at $K = 0$), so $K$ needs a minimum: Project 1's cylinder ($I_w = 0.5$, $|I_T - I_S| = 1.04$) needs $K \ge 9$ V s/rad (Sidi Fig. 6.6.4, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=53|s. 53]]).*

**One gyro or the full state? ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54]])** Sidi's law stabilizes, but slowly: near $\pm j\alpha_S$ the locus leaves at $\approx K'\gamma/(2\alpha_S^2)$, set by the slow motor pole. For L4's disc at $K = 14$: $\sigma \approx 0.001$ s⁻¹, and no $K$ gets past $\gamma/2 = 0.01$. Why: the wheel torques about $Y_B$, and a torque following $\omega_x$ is 90° out of phase with $\omega_y$, so it shifts the frequency instead of damping. **State feedback (L4)** pushes against $\omega_y$ itself: $\sigma = 0.1$ s⁻¹ at the same voltage.
![[R7026R L12 s54 one gyro vs full state.png]]
*The disc of L4, both laws near 10 V: short-circuited motor and one-gyro law barely decay; state feedback damps in ~40 s ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=54|s. 54]]).*

**One gyro gives stability, the full state gives performance.** Project 1 gets the full state from one gyro with a Kalman filter.

> [!tip] How does the motor beat the energy argument? (Question 11, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=56|s. 56]])
> The energy-sink argument assumes the internal element can only **remove** energy. A motor can also **supply** it, with a torque set by the measured nutation. The loop gain moves the poles left for either sign of $\alpha_S$. Price: a sensor, motor, electronics and power that can fail.

### Dual-spin stabilization ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=57|s. 57–64]])
**Why ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=57|s. 57]]):** on a single spinner the antenna spins with the body; the beam sweeps past the Earth only briefly each turn.
![[R7026R L12 s57 single vs dual spin antenna.png]]
*Left: single spin, the beam sweeps past the Earth. Right: dual spin, the rotor spins while the despun platform keeps the antenna on the Earth ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=57|s. 57]]).*

**Rotor** spins fast (momentum, stiffness); **platform** turns slowly (once per orbit) and points the antennas. Example: Hughes INTELSAT VI, a drum of solar cells with a despun antenna farm on top.
![[R7026R L12 s58 dual spin rotor and platform.png]]
*Rotor ($\omega_R$) and platform ($\omega_P$) on one bearing axis (Sidi Fig. 6.7.1, [[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=58|s. 58]]).*

**Where does the damper go? ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=59|s. 59–62]])** Both parts may dissipate (fuel slosh, flexing antennas, bearings). With $2T = I_T\omega_T^2 + I_R\omega_R^2 + I_P\omega_P^2$, $H^2 = (I_P\omega_P + I_R\omega_R)^2 + (I_T\omega_T)^2$ constant, and the inertial nutation rate $\omega_n = (I_P\omega_P + I_R\omega_R)/I_T$:
$$
\dot T = -\lambda_PI_P\dot\omega_P - \lambda_RI_R\dot\omega_R, \qquad \lambda_P = \omega_n - \omega_P, \quad \lambda_R = \omega_n - \omega_R
$$
Each part's energy loss: $\dot T_P = -\lambda_PI_P\dot\omega_P$, $\dot T_R = -\lambda_RI_R\dot\omega_R$. With $\sin\theta = I_T\omega_T/H$:
$$
\dot\theta = \frac{2I_T\omega_n}{H^2\sin2\theta}\left(\frac{\dot T_P}{\lambda_P} + \frac{\dot T_R}{\lambda_R}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{stable iff}\ \frac{\dot T_P}{\lambda_P} + \frac{\dot T_R}{\lambda_R} < 0
$$
Each part's dissipation counts **with the sign of the nutation rate seen from that part**.

![[R7026R L12 s59 dual spin damper on platform.png]]

**Fast rotor, slow platform** ($\omega_P \approx 0$, $\omega_n \approx I_R\omega_R/I_T$): $\lambda_P \approx \frac{I_R}{I_T}\omega_R$, $\lambda_R \approx \left(\frac{I_R}{I_T} - 1\right)\omega_R$:
$$
\frac{1}{\omega_R}\left(\frac{\dot T_P}{I_R/I_T} + \frac{\dot T_R}{I_R/I_T - 1}\right) < 0
$$
- **Case A, $I_R > I_T$ (disc-like):** both terms negative → always stable; damper anywhere.
- **Case B, $I_R < I_T$ (tall, the usual case since fairings are tall):** rotor dissipation **destabilizes**. Need, with $\rho = I_R/I_T$:
$$
|\dot T_P| > \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}|\dot T_R| \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{the damper goes on the platform}
$$

**Question 12 ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=64|s. 64]]):** $\rho = 0.8$: $|\dot T_P| > 4|\dot T_R|$. The factor is 1 at $\rho = 0.5$, 0.43 at $\rho = 0.3$, and → ∞ as $\rho \to 1$: a rotor only slightly smaller than the transverse inertia is the hardest case.

## Formulas
<!-- Only what I must know. Say what each symbol means -->
**Torque-free axisymmetric spin:** $\lambda = \omega_z\frac{I_z - I_x}{I_x}$ (body-frame nutation rate); $\tan\theta = \frac{I_x\omega_{xy}}{I_z\omega_z}$.

**Spin stability (rigid):** $\beta^2 = n^2\left(1 - \frac{I_z}{I_x}\right)\left(1 - \frac{I_z}{I_y}\right) > 0$ ⇔ major or minor axis. **With dissipation:** major axis only.

**Energy:** $T = h^2/(2I)$; $\dot T = \frac{h^2}{I_z}\cos\theta\sin\theta\left(\frac{I_z}{I_x} - 1\right)\dot\theta$.

**ΔV stage**
$$
T_d = Fd_{cm}\sin\beta, \qquad \theta_{av} = \frac{4}{\pi}\frac{T_d}{\omega_z^2I_z|I_z/I_x - 1|}, \qquad \Delta V_{loss} = \Delta V(1 - \cos\theta_{av})
$$
**Despin:** $t_{DESP} = \frac{h_z(0)}{N_iF\Delta z}$, $m = \frac{h_z(0)}{gI_{sp}\Delta z}$. **Denutation:** $t_{DENU} = \frac{\pi h_{xy}(0)}{2N_iF\Delta x}$, $m = \frac{\pi h_{xy}(0)}{2gI_{sp}\Delta x}$.

**Wheel damper (Sidi):** $\alpha_S = \omega_S\frac{I_T - I_S}{I_T}$, $\epsilon = \frac{I_w}{I_T}$, $\sigma = \frac{D}{I_w}$; passive works iff $I_S > I_T$.

**Active wheel:** $\gamma = \frac{N^2}{RI_w}$, $\frac{\omega_x}{e_M} \approx \frac{NI_S\omega_S}{RI_T^2}\frac{s}{(s^2 + \alpha_S^2)(s + \gamma)}$, $e_M = -K\omega_x$.

**Dual spin:** $\frac{\dot T_P}{\lambda_P} + \frac{\dot T_R}{\lambda_R} < 0$; $I_R < I_T$: $|\dot T_P| > \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}|\dot T_R|$, $\rho = I_R/I_T$.

## Common mistakes
- **Body or inertial nutation rate?** $\lambda = \omega_z(I_z - I_x)/I_x$ is the rate *seen from the body*. Seen from inertial space, $Z_B$ cones around $\mathbf h$ at another rate ($I_z\omega_z/I_x$ for a symmetric body). Say which one you mean.
- **The sign of $\lambda$.** It is negative for minor-axis spin. Use $|\lambda|$ in magnitudes and periods.
- **Major or minor?** Compare the spin-axis inertia $I_z$ (or $I_S$) with the transverse $I_x$ (or $I_T$). $I_z > I_x$ is a major-axis spin: a flat disc, not a pencil.
- **Stopping at rigid-body stability.** A minor-axis spin is stable for a perfectly rigid body. With any dissipation (fuel, antennas, dampers) it is not.
- **rpm.** Multiply by $2\pi/60$ to get rad/s.
- **Fuel for several thrusters.** $m = h_z(0)/(gI_{sp}\Delta z)$ is the *total*. Each of two thrusters burns half of it (0.16 kg each, 0.32 kg in total in Example 3).
- **The bang-bang torque.** The average is $(4/\pi)F\Delta x$, not $\sqrt2F\Delta x$ and not $2F\Delta x$.
- **The misalignment torque.** $T_d = Fd_{cm}\sin\beta$: the angle $\beta$ goes in the sine, $d_{cm}$ is the arm. And the nutation formula has $|I_z/I_x - 1|$.
- **Dual spin with $I_R < I_T$.** Then $\lambda_R \approx (\rho - 1)\omega_R < 0$: dissipation in the rotor *destabilizes*. The damper goes on the platform.
- **The sign of the active law.** With the course's signs it is $e_M = -K\omega_x$ (Sidi writes $+K$ with other sign conventions). Check with the root locus.

## Worked example
<!-- AFTER: redo one example from the slides by hand, without looking -->
**Problem:** ([[Lecture_12_handout.pdf#page=27|s. 27–28, 35]]) A spacecraft with $I_x = I_y = 500$, $I_z = 200$ kg m² fires a 400 N apogee engine misaligned by $\beta = 0.8°$, 1 m from the CM. (a) What spin rate keeps the ΔV loss below 0.5 %? (b) Is this spin axis safe for a long coast? (c) Afterwards a spacecraft with $I_z = 100$ kg m² at 10 rad/s is despun by two 5 N thrusters ($I_{sp} = 200$ s, arm 1 m) down to a dead zone of 0.10 rad/s. Find the time and fuel.

**Solution:**
1. *Torque:* $T_d = 400\cdot1\cdot\sin0.8° = 400\cdot0.01396 = 5.58$ N m.
2. *Allowed nutation:* $1 - \cos\theta_{av} < 0.005$ ⇒ $\cos\theta_{av} > 0.995$ ⇒ $\theta_{av} < 0.100$ rad (5.7°).
3. *Spin rate:* $|I_z/I_x - 1| = |0.4 - 1| = 0.6$:
$$
\omega_z^2 > \frac{4T_d}{\pi\theta_{av}I_z|I_z/I_x - 1|} = \frac{4\cdot5.58}{\pi\cdot0.100\cdot200\cdot0.6} = \frac{22.3}{37.7} = 0.59\ \text{rad}^2/\text{s}^2 \ \Rightarrow\ \omega_z > 0.77\ \text{rad/s} \approx 7.3\ \text{rpm}
$$
4. *Safe for a coast?* $I_z = 200 < I_x = 500$: **minor-axis spin**. Fine for a burn of minutes, but any energy dissipation (fuel slosh, flexible parts) would turn it over within hours (Explorer 1). For a long coast spin about the major axis or add active nutation control.
5. *Despin with dead zone:* $h_z(0) = 100\cdot10 = 1000$ N m s, $h_{DZ} = 100\cdot0.10 = 10$ N m s.
$$
t_{DESP} = \frac{h_z(0) - h_{DZ}}{N_iF\Delta z} = \frac{990}{2\cdot5\cdot1} = 99\ \text{s}, \qquad m_{FUEL} = \frac{h_z(0) - h_{DZ}}{gI_{sp}\Delta z} = \frac{990}{9.81\cdot200\cdot1} = 0.505\ \text{kg}
$$
   (Without the dead zone: 100 s and 0.51 kg, the slide's homework.)

## Self-test
<!-- AFTER: 2–3 questions to quiz myself before the exam -->
- Q: A satellite with $I_z = 1000$, $I_x = I_y = 800$ kg m² spins at 30 rpm. How long does $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ take to go once round, seen from the body?

> [!example]- Answer
> $n = \pi$ rad/s, $\lambda = \pi\cdot200/800 = \pi/4$ rad/s → $2\pi/\lambda = 8$ s (four spin periods of 2 s).

- Q: Which axes give stable spin for a rigid body, and which with energy dissipation? Why?

> [!example]- Answer
> Rigid: major and minor axes ($\beta$ real), not intermediate. With dissipation: only the major axis. With $\mathbf h$ fixed, $T = h^2/(2I)$ is smallest for the largest $I$; dissipation lowers $T$, so the motion drifts toward the major axis.

- Q: Doubling the spin rate during a burn changes the average nutation angle by what factor? Why?

> [!example]- Answer
> Factor 1/4 ($\theta_{av} \propto 1/\omega_z^2$): $h_z = I_z\omega_z$ doubles (more stiffness), and $\lambda \propto \omega_z$ doubles so the body-fixed torque averages out twice as fast ($h_{xy} \le 2T_d/|\lambda|$ halves).

- Q: You double the despin thrusters' force. What happens to the despin time and fuel?

> [!example]- Answer
> Time halves; fuel unchanged. The required impulse $h_z(0)/\Delta z$ is fixed, and fuel = impulse/$(gI_{sp})$.

- Q: Why does bang-bang denutation with two thrusters give only $(4/\pi)F\Delta x$ average torque?

> [!example]- Answer
> The two torques add to $\sqrt2F\Delta x$ at 45°, but $\boldsymbol\omega_{xy}$ keeps turning at $\lambda$, so the angle $\alpha$ between them sweeps ±45° in each quadrant; only $\cos\alpha$ of the torque works. Average: $\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\sqrt2F\Delta x\cos\alpha\,d\alpha = \frac4\pi F\Delta x$.

- Q: A passive wheel damper is put on a minor-axis spinner. What happens? What could work instead?

> [!example]- Answer
> It destabilizes: the root locus bulges into the right half plane, the nutation grows (energy-sink argument). An actively driven wheel ($e_M = -K\omega_x$, $K$ above a minimum) can stabilize it, because the motor can supply energy; full state feedback damps much faster than one gyro.

- Q: A dual-spin satellite has $I_R/I_T = 0.6$. Where must the damper go, and how strong must it be?

> [!example]- Answer
> $I_R < I_T$ → rotor dissipation destabilizes; damper on the platform with $|\dot T_P| > \frac{0.6}{0.4}|\dot T_R| = 1.5|\dot T_R|$.
